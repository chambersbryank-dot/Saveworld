About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a one-semester senior heat transfer course for engineers. It is modeled on the topics and depth of the free online A Heat Transfer Textbook by Lienhard and Lienhard and MIT 2.51 Intermediate Heat and Mass Transfer (free on MIT OpenCourseWare), which we link where they help. None of those authors, schools or agencies endorses this page.

What we add: every unit ties to real work with a cited public source (NASA’s Earth energy budget, Parker Solar Probe and ENERGY STAR heat pumps), every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab has a free simulation or a spreadsheet.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Earth’s climate is a heat transfer problem: sunlight in, thermal infrared out, with convection and evaporation moving heat in between. The same physics decides how much energy our buildings, engines and electronics waste.

Insulation, heat pumps and efficient heat exchangers are some of the cheapest climate solutions. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample or textbook values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Thermodynamics: energy balances and the first and second laws.
  • Fluid Mechanics: boundary layers, Reynolds number and pipe flow.
  • Differential Equations: first-order ODEs and separation of variables.
  • Calculus III: gradients and integrals over areas and volumes.

How to study

  • Draw the system and every heat flow arrow before writing an equation.
  • Use kelvin for radiation and temperature differences for conduction and convection.
  • Turn every problem into a thermal-resistance network when you can.
  • Check dimensionless numbers (Bi, Re, Pr, Nu, Ra) to pick the right correlation.
  • Compare answers with everyday experience: a warm house, a cooling cup, a hot pan.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer, and retake the quizzes a few days later.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer or tablet for the free PhET Energy Forms and Changes, Blackbody Spectrum and Greenhouse Effect simulations.
  • Spreadsheet software (any free one works), or Python.
  • The free online A Heat Transfer Textbook in a web browser.
  • A calculator.

Unit 1 · Week 1

Modes of Heat Transfer and Energy Balances

Conduction, convection and radiation, their rate equations, and surface and control-volume energy balances.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Name the three modes and their rate laws.
  • Compute conduction, convection and radiation rates.
  • Write a surface energy balance.
  • Read Earth’s energy budget as a heat balance.

Key concepts

  • Heat transfer is energy in transit because of a temperature difference; thermodynamics says how much, heat transfer says how fast.
  • Conduction moves energy through a solid or still fluid by molecular contact (Fourier’s law).
  • Convection moves energy between a surface and a moving fluid (Newton’s law of cooling, with a heat transfer coefficient h).
  • Radiation moves energy by electromagnetic waves, needs no medium, and grows with T⁴ in kelvin (Stefan–Boltzmann law).
  • A surface stores no energy, so what arrives by one mode must leave by the others.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 A 0.2 m concrete wall (k = 1.4 W/(m·K), sample) of area 10 m² has a 15 K difference across it. Find q.

Show the steps
  1. q = 1.4 × 10 × 15/0.2.

Answer: 1050 W.

Example 1.2 A plate with ε = 0.9 at 400 K sits in 300 K surroundings. Net radiation per m²?

Show the steps
  1. 0.9σ(400⁴ − 300⁴) = 0.9 × 5.670374419 × 10⁻⁸ × 1.75 × 10¹⁰.

Answer: 893 W/m².

Example 1.3 NASA says about 29% of incoming sunlight is reflected and the global average is about 340 W/m². How much is absorbed?

Show the steps
  1. Reflected: 0.29 × 340 = 98.6 W/m².
  2. Absorbed: 340 − 98.6.

Answer: 241.4 W/m², matching NASA’s roughly 240 W/m².

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Repeat the wall example with k = 0.04 W/(m·K).

    Show answer

    30 W.

  2. Convection with h = 25 W/(m²·K), A = 2 m², ΔT = 40 K?

    Show answer

    2000 W.

  3. Why must radiation use kelvin?

    Show answer

    T⁴ depends on absolute temperature.

  4. Why is 1360/4 the global average sunlight?

    Show answer

    A sphere’s area is 4 times its shadow disk: 1360/4 = 340 W/m².

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Heat and temperature are the same.

    Actually: Temperature is a state; heat is energy in transit.

  • Myth: Radiation needs air.

    Actually: It travels through vacuum.

  • Myth: h is a material property.

    Actually: It depends on flow and geometry.

Flashcards

  • Conduction

    Molecular energy transfer through matter.

  • Convection

    Surface-to-moving-fluid transfer.

  • Radiation

    Electromagnetic energy exchange.

  • h

    Convection coefficient, W/(m²·K).

  • k

    Thermal conductivity, W/(m·K).

  • Surface balance

    In = out at a surface.

How this helps save the planet

NASA’s Earth Observatory says total solar irradiance is about 1,360 W/m², averaging about 340 W/m² over the globe; about 29% is reflected, 23% is absorbed by the atmosphere and 48% by the surface, so Earth absorbs about 240 W/m².

For a stable temperature, Earth must radiate the same 240 W/m² back to space as thermal infrared, the heat balance this course returns to in Unit 10.

Unit 2 · Week 2

Steady Conduction and Thermal Resistance

The heat equation, one-dimensional steady conduction, thermal-resistance networks, composite walls and insulation.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • State the heat equation and its 1D steady form.
  • Build series and parallel resistance networks.
  • Compute heat loss through a composite wall.
  • Quantify the savings from insulation.

Key concepts

  • The heat equation balances conduction, generation and storage; at steady state with no generation, a plane wall has a linear temperature profile.
  • Each layer acts like an electrical resistor: R = L/(kA) for conduction, 1/(hA) for convection.
  • Series resistances add; heat flow equals overall ΔT divided by total resistance, like current.
  • The largest resistance controls the heat flow, which is why insulation (low k) matters most.
  • Contact resistance between layers can add surprising resistance in electronics and joints.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 A wall has inside air (h = 8), 0.1 m insulation (k = 0.04), 0.1 m brick (k = 0.7) and outside air (h = 25), all sample values. Find the resistance per m².

Show the steps
  1. 1/8 + 0.1/0.04 + 0.1/0.7 + 1/25 = 0.125 + 2.5 + 0.1429 + 0.04.

Answer: 2.808 m²·K/W.

Example 2.2 With 20 K between inside and outside, find the heat flux and the loss through 100 m².

Show the steps
  1. q″ = 20/2.808 = 7.12 W/m².
  2. q = 100 × 7.12.

Answer: 712 W.

Example 2.3 Remove the insulation. How much does the loss grow?

Show the steps
  1. R″ = 0.3079 m²·K/W.
  2. q″ = 20/0.3079 = 64.97 W/m².

Answer: About 9.1 times; insulation cuts the loss by 89.0%.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Double the insulation to 0.2 m. New q″?

    Show answer

    R″ = 5.308, q″ = 3.77 W/m².

  2. Which layer dominates the original wall?

    Show answer

    The insulation, 2.5 of 2.808 m²·K/W.

  3. Write R for two layers in parallel.

    Show answer

    1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂.

  4. Shape of T(x) in a plane wall at steady state?

    Show answer

    Linear.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Thin layers never matter.

    Actually: A low-k thin layer can dominate.

  • Myth: Resistances in series multiply.

    Actually: They add.

  • Myth: Air films add nothing.

    Actually: Convection resistance is real.

Flashcards

  • Thermal resistance

    ΔT per unit heat flow.

  • Heat equation

    Energy balance in a solid.

  • Composite wall

    Layers in series.

  • Insulation

    Low-k material.

  • Contact resistance

    Resistance at an interface.

  • U-value

    Overall coefficient, 1/R″.

How this helps save the planet

ENERGY STAR says a typical U.S. household energy bill is around $1,900 a year and almost half of that goes to heating and cooling.

This unit’s sample wall shows why sealing and insulating comes first: adding insulation cut the conduction loss by about 89%, so any heating system has far less heat to replace.

Unit 3 · Weeks 3–4

Cylinders, Spheres and Fins

Radial conduction, pipe insulation and the critical radius, extended surfaces, fin efficiency and electronics cooling.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute radial conduction resistance.
  • Find the critical insulation radius.
  • Compute fin parameter m and fin efficiency.
  • Estimate heat from a fin.

Key concepts

  • In a cylinder, area grows with radius, so the temperature profile is logarithmic and R = ln(r₂/r₁)/(2πkL).
  • Adding insulation to a small wire can increase heat loss until the outer radius passes the critical radius k/h.
  • Fins add area where h is low (usually gases), as on engine cylinders, heat sinks and radiators.
  • The fin parameter m = √(hP/(kA_c)) sets how fast the fin temperature decays from its base.
  • Fin efficiency compares actual heat to the heat if the whole fin were at the base temperature.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 Pipe insulation from r = 0.05 to 0.1 m, k = 0.04 W/(m·K), 1 m long, 100 K across it (sample values). Find R and q.

Show the steps
  1. R = ln 2/(2π × 0.04) = 2.758 K/W.
  2. q = 100/2.758.

Answer: 36.3 W per meter.

Example 3.2 An aluminum pin fin (k = 200) is 5 mm across with h = 50 W/(m²·K) and a 20 mm effective length. Find η.

Show the steps
  1. m = √(4h/(kD)) = √(200/1) = 14.14 m⁻¹.
  2. mL = 0.2828.
  3. η = tanh(0.2828)/0.2828.

Answer: 0.974.

Example 3.3 Heat from that fin with a 50 K base excess?

Show the steps
  1. A_f = πDL = 3.142 × 10⁻⁴ m².
  2. q = ηhA_fθ = 0.974 × 50 × 3.142 × 10⁻⁴ × 50.

Answer: 0.765 W.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Critical radius for k = 0.04 and h = 8?

    Show answer

    5 mm.

  2. Fin efficiency at L = 40 mm?

    Show answer

    mL = 0.566, η = 0.905.

  3. Why do fins suit air more than water?

    Show answer

    Air’s low h makes area the bottleneck.

  4. Profile shape in a cylinder wall?

    Show answer

    Logarithmic.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: More insulation always helps.

    Actually: Not below the critical radius.

  • Myth: Longer fins always help a lot.

    Actually: Efficiency drops with length.

  • Myth: Fins work in any medium.

    Actually: They pay off where h is low.

Flashcards

  • Critical radius

    k/h for cylinders.

  • Fin

    Extended surface.

  • Fin efficiency

    Actual over ideal fin heat.

  • m

    Fin decay parameter.

  • Heat sink

    Array of fins.

  • Log profile

    Cylinder steady temperature.

Show answer

How this helps save the planet

Heat moves the same way in a pipe wall as in a planet’s crust: from hot to cold, through resistances in series.

Insulating hot-water and steam pipes is one of the simplest efficiency steps in buildings and industry. All values in this unit are sample textbook values.

Unit 4 · Week 5

Transient Conduction

The Biot number, the lumped-capacitance model, time constants, and when spatial temperature gradients matter.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute the Biot number.
  • Use the lumped model when Bi < 0.1.
  • Compute a thermal time constant.
  • Find the time to reach a target temperature.

Key concepts

  • The Biot number compares internal conduction resistance to external convection resistance.
  • When Bi < 0.1, the body is nearly uniform in temperature and the lumped model works.
  • In the lumped model, the temperature excess decays exponentially with time constant τ = ρcV/(hA).
  • For larger Bi, use one-term series solutions or charts (Heisler) or a numerical method.
  • High heat capacity slows response: oceans and thick walls are thermal flywheels.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 A 10 mm aluminum sphere (k = 237, ρ = 2700, c = 900) cools in air with h = 50. Find Bi.

Show the steps
  1. L_c = D/6 = 1.667 × 10⁻³ m.
  2. Bi = 50 × 1.667 × 10⁻³/237.

Answer: 3.52 × 10⁻⁴, so the lumped model works.

Example 4.2 Find τ for that sphere.

Show the steps
  1. τ = 2700 × 900 × 1.667 × 10⁻³/50.

Answer: 81 s.

Example 4.3 How long to cool from 300 °C to 50 °C in 20 °C air?

Show the steps
  1. θ_i = 280 K, θ = 30 K.
  2. t = 81 ln(280/30) = 81 × 2.234.

Answer: 181 s.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Fraction of the excess left after one τ?

    Show answer

    e⁻¹ = 0.368.

  2. Time to halve the excess?

    Show answer

    τ ln 2 = 56.1 s.

  3. Bi = 0.5. Is lumped valid?

    Show answer

    No; use series or numerical solutions.

  4. Double h. What happens to τ?

    Show answer

    It halves to 40.5 s.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Bi is the Nusselt number.

    Actually: Bi uses the solid’s k; Nu uses the fluid’s.

  • Myth: Lumped works for any size.

    Actually: Only when Bi < 0.1.

  • Myth: Cooling is linear in time.

    Actually: It is exponential.

Flashcards

  • Biot number

    Internal over external resistance.

  • Lumped capacitance

    Uniform-temperature model.

  • Time constant

    ρcV/(hA).

  • Heisler chart

    One-term transient solution chart.

  • Thermal mass

    Heat capacity that slows change.

  • L_c

    V/A characteristic length.

How this helps save the planet

Unit 5 · Weeks 6–7

External Forced Convection

Boundary layers, Reynolds, Prandtl and Nusselt numbers, flat-plate correlations and flow over cylinders.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute Re, Pr and Nu.
  • Use the laminar flat-plate correlation.
  • Find h and the heat rate.
  • Check transition at Re ≈ 5 × 10⁵.

Key concepts

  • Velocity and thermal boundary layers form on a surface; heat crosses the thin layer of slow fluid by conduction.
  • The Nusselt number Nu = hL/k is the dimensionless heat transfer coefficient.
  • The Prandtl number compares momentum and thermal diffusivity (about 0.7 for air, about 7 for water near room temperature, textbook values).
  • On a flat plate, flow is usually laminar below Re ≈ 5 × 10⁵; turbulence raises h.
  • Correlations are fits to experiments; use them inside their stated ranges.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 Air (ν = 1.5 × 10⁻⁵, k = 0.026, Pr = 0.71) flows at 5 m/s over a 0.5 m plate. Find Re.

Show the steps
  1. Re = 5 × 0.5/1.5 × 10⁻⁵.

Answer: 166,667, laminar.

Example 5.2 Find the average Nu and h.

Show the steps
  1. Nu = 0.664 × 166,667^½ × 0.71^⅓ = 0.664 × 408.2 × 0.892 = 241.8.
  2. h = 241.8 × 0.026/0.5.

Answer: 12.6 W/(m²·K).

Example 5.3 Heat from a 0.5 × 1 m plate 30 K above the air?

Show the steps
  1. q = 12.58 × 0.5 × 30.

Answer: 189 W.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Repeat h at 10 m/s.

    Show answer

    Re = 333,333, h = 17.8 W/(m²·K) (√2 times higher).

  2. Re at 1 m/s?

    Show answer

    33,333.

  3. Name the dimensionless h.

    Show answer

    Nusselt number.

  4. Why does turbulence raise h?

    Show answer

    Mixing thins the slow near-wall layer.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Nu uses the solid’s k.

    Actually: It uses the fluid’s k.

  • Myth: One correlation fits all flows.

    Actually: Check range and geometry.

  • Myth: h is constant along a plate.

    Actually: Local h falls as the layer grows.

Flashcards

  • Reynolds number

    Inertia over viscosity.

  • Prandtl number

    ν/α.

  • Nusselt number

    hL/k.

  • Boundary layer

    Thin near-wall region.

  • Correlation

    Fit to experiments.

  • Transition

    Laminar to turbulent.

Show answer

2. Average h…

Show answer

12.6 W/(m²·K). Nu k/L.

3. Heat rate…

How this helps save the planet

NASA’s Earth Observatory says convection carries about 5% of incoming solar energy from the surface into the atmosphere, while evaporation carries about 25%.

Wind speed sets h over oceans and land just as velocity sets h on this unit’s plate, which is why winds shape how fast the surface sheds heat.

Unit 6 · Weeks 8–9

Internal Flow and Heat Exchangers

Pipe-flow convection, mean temperature, the Dittus–Boelter correlation, heat exchanger types, LMTD and effectiveness–NTU.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use laminar and turbulent pipe correlations.
  • Compute a log-mean temperature difference.
  • Size a heat exchanger with UA and LMTD.
  • Use effectiveness–NTU.

Key concepts

  • In a pipe, the bulk (mean) temperature changes along the length as heat enters or leaves.
  • Fully developed laminar flow with constant wall temperature has Nu = 3.66; turbulent flow uses Dittus–Boelter (textbook correlations).
  • Counterflow exchangers beat parallel flow because the temperature difference stays more even.
  • The LMTD method suits sizing when all four temperatures are known.
  • The ε-NTU method suits rating when outlet temperatures are unknown.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 6.1 Water (k = 0.6, Pr = 7, textbook values) flows at Re = 20,000 in a 20 mm tube. Find h.

Show the steps
  1. Nu = 0.023 × 20,000^0.8 × 7^0.4 = 138.2.
  2. h = 138.2 × 0.6/0.02.

Answer: About 4150 W/(m²·K).

Example 6.2 Counterflow: hot 90 → 50 °C, cold 20 → 40 °C. Find the LMTD.

Show the steps
  1. ΔT₁ = 90 − 40 = 50 K; ΔT₂ = 50 − 20 = 30 K.
  2. LMTD = 20/ln(50/30).

Answer: 39.15 K.

Example 6.3 With U = 500 W/(m²·K) and A = 2 m², find q.

Show the steps

Natural Convection and Boiling

Buoyancy-driven flow, Grashof and Rayleigh numbers, vertical-plate correlations, and an introduction to boiling and condensation.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute the Rayleigh number.
  • Use a vertical-plate correlation.
  • Compare natural and forced h.
  • Describe the boiling curve.

Key concepts

  • Warm fluid is lighter and rises; buoyancy, not a fan, drives the flow.
  • The Rayleigh number Ra = gβΔTL³/(να) plays the role Re plays in forced flow.
  • On a vertical plate, flow is laminar below about Ra = 10⁹ (textbook value).
  • Natural-convection h in air is only a few W/(m²·K), so radiation is often comparable.
  • Boiling and condensation move large heat at small ΔT because of latent heat.

Unit 8 · Week 11

Radiation Fundamentals

Blackbodies, Planck’s law, Wien’s law, the Stefan–Boltzmann law, emissivity, absorptivity and Kirchhoff’s law.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Find a blackbody’s peak wavelength.
  • Compute emissive power.
  • Use emissivity and absorptivity.
  • Find a planet’s effective temperature.

Key concepts

  • A blackbody absorbs all radiation and emits the most possible at each temperature and wavelength.
  • Wien’s law: peak wavelength is inversely proportional to absolute temperature.
  • Total emissive power grows as σT⁴, so doubling temperature multiplies emission by 16.
  • Real surfaces emit a fraction ε of blackbody power; for a gray surface α = ε (Kirchhoff).
  • Solar radiation is short-wave; Earth’s emission is long-wave infrared, which is why gases like CO₂ and water vapor matter.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 8.1 NASA gives the Sun about 5,800 K. Find its peak wavelength.

Show the steps
  1. 2898/5800.

Answer: 0.500 µm, visible light.

Example 8.2 Earth absorbs about 240 W/m² (NASA). Find its effective temperature.

Show the steps
  1. T = (240/5.670374419 × 10⁻⁸)^¼.

Answer: 255.1 K, about −18 °C (NASA rounds to about −20 °C).

Example 8.3 Blackbody emission from a 288 K surface?

Show the steps
  1. σ × 288⁴.

Answer: 390 W/m².

Unit 9 · Weeks 12–13

Radiation Exchange and Shields

View factors, reciprocity, gray-surface networks, parallel plates, radiation shields and thermal protection in space.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use view-factor reciprocity.
  • Compute net exchange between parallel plates.
  • Show how a shield cuts heat flow.
  • Estimate radiation from a hot shield.

Key concepts

  • The view factor F₁₂ is the fraction of radiation leaving surface 1 that reaches surface 2.
  • Reciprocity: A₁F₁₂ = A₂F₂₁; summation: the view factors from a surface add to 1.
  • Gray surfaces exchange heat through surface resistances (1 − ε)/(εA) and space resistances 1/(A₁F₁₂).
  • A thin low-ε shield between two plates adds resistances and cuts exchange sharply; multilayer insulation stacks many.
  • A heat shield facing the Sun gets hot, reradiates, and protects what lies in its shadow.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 9.1 Large parallel plates at 400 K and 300 K, both ε = 0.8. Net flux?

Show the steps

  2. q″ = σ(400⁴ − 300⁴)/1.5.

Answer: 662 W/m².

Example 9.2 Insert a shield with ε = 0.05 on both sides.

Show the steps
  1. Resistance: 2 × (1.25 + 20 − 1) = 40.5.
  2. q″ = σ × 1.75 × 10¹⁰/40.5.

Answer: 24.5 W/m², 27 times less.

Example 9.3 A small sphere (A₁ = 1 m²) sits inside a shell of area 4 m². Find F₂₁.

Show the steps
  1. F₁₂ = 1.
  2. F₂₁ = A₁F₁₂/A₂.

Answer: 0.25.

Unit 10 · Weeks 14–15

Heat Pumps, Buildings and Earth’s Energy Balance

Heat-pump COP, Carnot limits, whole-building heat loss, and the planetary energy imbalance measured from space.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute heat-pump COP and its Carnot limit.
  • Size electricity for a heating load.
  • Convert a flux imbalance to total power.
  • Connect all three modes in one system.

Key concepts

  • A heat pump moves heat from cold to hot using work; its heating COP is heat delivered divided by work.
  • The Carnot COP, T_h/(T_h − T_c), is an upper limit that falls as the outdoor temperature drops.
  • A building’s heat loss is its UA times the indoor–outdoor difference; insulation lowers UA before you buy equipment.
  • Earth’s energy imbalance is a small difference between two large flows, measured by satellites such as NASA’s CERES instruments.
  • Every system in this course combines conduction, convection and radiation in a resistance network.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Glossary

Albedo
Fraction of sunlight reflected.
Biot number
Internal over external thermal resistance.
Blackbody
Perfect absorber and emitter.
Boundary layer
Thin near-wall flow region.
COP
Heat moved per unit work.
Critical radius
Radius of maximum heat loss from an insulated cylinder.
Effectiveness
Actual over maximum exchanger heat.
Emissivity
Emission relative to a blackbody.
Energy imbalance
Absorbed minus emitted planetary energy.
Fin efficiency
Actual over ideal fin heat.
Fourier’s law
Conduction rate law.
Heat exchanger
Device moving heat between fluids.
LMTD
Log-mean temperature difference.
Lumped capacitance
Uniform-temperature transient model.
Nusselt number
Dimensionless convection coefficient.
Prandtl number
Momentum over thermal diffusivity.
Radiation shield
Low-emissivity barrier.
Rayleigh number
Buoyancy-flow strength.
Thermal resistance
Temperature difference per heat flow.
View factor
Fraction of radiation one surface intercepts.
