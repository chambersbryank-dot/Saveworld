About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a one-semester senior course in avionics and systems engineering. It is modeled on the topics and depth of MIT 16.842 Fundamentals of Systems Engineering (free on MIT OpenCourseWare) and the NASA Systems Engineering Handbook, which we link where they help. Neither MIT nor NASA endorses this page.

What we add: every unit ties to real work with a cited public source (Landsat 9, Hubble, Dragonfly and the FAA’s air traffic system), every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab has a free simulation or a spreadsheet.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Earth-observing satellites, weather spacecraft and efficient air traffic all depend on avionics that work for years without a repair visit, and on systems engineering that makes thousands of parts behave as one.

Good requirements, redundancy and testing are what let a satellite keep measuring forests, ice and oceans long past its design life. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample or textbook values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Circuits: voltage, current, resistance and digital signals.
  • Probability & Statistics: probability rules, exponential distribution and variance.
  • Flight Dynamics & Controls: feedback, sensors and actuators.
  • Intro to Programming: loops, functions and timing.

How to study

  • Start every problem from the requirement it serves.
  • Draw block diagrams and interfaces before computing anything.
  • Keep probabilities as numbers between 0 and 1 and check that they stay there.
  • Track units: bits, bytes, seconds, hertz, decibels.
  • Ask “what happens if this part fails?” for every box you draw.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer, and retake the quizzes a few days later.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer or tablet for the free PhET Circuit Construction Kit: DC simulation.
  • Spreadsheet software (any free one works), or Python.
  • The free NASA Systems Engineering Handbook in a web browser.
  • A calculator.

Unit 1 · Week 1

The Systems Engineering Life Cycle and Requirements

Stakeholders, the V-model, life-cycle phases and reviews, good requirements, technical performance measures and margins.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Describe the V-model.
  • Write verifiable requirements.
  • Compute margins against a requirement.
  • Track a technical performance measure.

Key concepts

  • Systems engineering turns stakeholder needs into a system that works, on time and on budget, across its whole life.
  • The V-model goes down the left side (requirements, architecture, design) and up the right (integration, verification, validation).
  • A good requirement is necessary, clear, feasible and verifiable, with a number, a unit and a test method.
  • Technical performance measures such as mass, power and data rate are tracked against allocations with margin.
  • Reviews (concept, preliminary design, critical design) gate each phase; MIT 16.842 students prepare a preliminary-design-review-level design.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 NASA requires Landsat 9 to collect at least 400 scenes a day; it collects 740. Find the margin.

Show the steps
  1. (740 − 400)/400.

Answer: 85% above the requirement.

Example 1.2 An avionics box is estimated at 12 kg. Allocate with 25% margin (sample values).

Show the steps
  1. 12 × 1.25.

Answer: 15 kg.

Example 1.3 Option A scores 7 (cost) and 8 (reliability); B scores 9 and 6. Weights 0.3 and 0.7. Which wins?

Show the steps
  1. A = 2.1 + 5.6 = 7.7.
  2. B = 2.7 + 4.2 = 6.9.

Answer: A, 7.7 to 6.9.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Landsat 9 design life is 5 years and consumables last 10 years. Ratio?

    Show answer

    2.

  2. Allocate 400 W of power at 30% margin.

    Show answer

    520 W.

  3. Rewrite “the radio shall be fast” as a verifiable requirement.

    Show answer

    For example: “The radio shall downlink at least 300 Mbit/s, verified by test.”

  4. Which side of the V holds verification?

    Show answer

    The right side.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Requirements describe the design.

    Actually: They state what is needed, not how.

  • Myth: Margin is waste.

    Actually: It absorbs growth and surprises.

  • Myth: Verification and validation are the same.

    Actually: Verification: built right; validation: right thing built.

    Verifiable need.

  • TPM

    Tracked technical performance measure.

  • PDR

    Preliminary design review.

  • Verification

    Built right.

  • Validation

    Right thing built.

How this helps save the planet

Unit 2 · Week 2

Architecture, Interfaces and Integration

Functional and physical architecture, interface control, N² diagrams, model-based systems engineering and why interfaces grow fast.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Map functions to components.
  • Count potential interfaces.
  • Read an N² diagram.
  • Explain model-based systems engineering.

Key concepts

  • A functional architecture says what the system does; a physical architecture says which boxes do it.
  • Every pair of components could need an interface, so potential interfaces grow as n(n − 1)/2.
  • An N² diagram puts components on the diagonal and the data or power between them off the diagonal.
  • Interface control documents fix connectors, signals, timing and data formats so teams can build in parallel.
  • NASA notes its handbook update includes Model-Based Systems Engineering, which keeps requirements, architecture and analyses in one connected model.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 An avionics suite has 8 boxes. How many potential interfaces?

Show the steps
  1. 8 × 7/2.

Answer: 28.

Example 2.2 Add 4 boxes (n = 12).

Show the steps
  1. 12 × 11/2.

Answer: 66, more than double.

Example 2.3 Three boxes send 20, 35 and 15 kbit/s on a 100 kbit/s bus. Utilization?

Show the steps
  1. 70/100.

Answer: 70%.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Off-diagonal N² cells for n = 8?

    Show answer

    56.

  2. Interfaces for n = 4?

    Show answer

    6.

  3. Why keep bus utilization below 100%?

    Show answer

    Room for growth, retries and timing jitter.

  4. What is an ICD?

    Show answer

    Interface control document.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Interfaces grow linearly.

    Actually: They grow roughly as n².

  • Myth: Architecture is just a parts list.

    Actually: It includes functions and connections.

  • Myth: MBSE replaces engineers.

    Actually: It connects their work.

    Sum of data rates.

Unit 3 · Weeks 3–4

Reliability and Redundancy

Failure rates, exponential reliability, MTBF, series and parallel systems, triple modular redundancy, k-of-n systems and availability.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute reliability from a failure rate.
  • Combine series and parallel blocks.
  • Compute 2-of-3 voting reliability.
  • Compute availability.

Key concepts

  • With a constant failure rate λ, reliability falls as e^(−λt) and the mean time between failures is 1/λ.
  • Series systems fail if any part fails, so reliabilities multiply.
  • Parallel (redundant) systems fail only if all parts fail.
  • Triple modular redundancy votes 2 of 3; it beats a single unit early in life but not after R drops below 0.5.
  • Availability is the fraction of time a system works: MTBF/(MTBF + MTTR).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 A unit has λ = 10⁻⁵ per hour. Reliability over 10,000 h?

Show the steps
  1. e^(−0.1).

Answer: 0.905.

Example 3.2 Two such units in parallel?

Show the steps
  1. 1 − (1 − 0.9048)².

Answer: 0.991.

Example 3.3 Three units voting 2 of 3?

Show the steps
  1. 3(0.9048)² − 2(0.9048)³.

Answer: 0.975.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. MTBF for λ = 10⁻⁵ /h?

    Show answer

    100,000 h.

  2. Single, dual and TMR at 50,000 h?

    Show answer

    0.607, 0.845, 0.657.

  3. Availability with MTBF 1000 h and MTTR 2 h?

    Show answer

    0.998.

  4. Hubble needs one of three working gyros. Which model?

    Show answer

    1-of-3 (parallel).

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Redundancy always helps.

    Actually: TMR loses to a single unit once R < 0.5.

  • Myth: MTBF is guaranteed life.

    Actually: It is a mean; many fail sooner.

  • Myth: Series reliability adds.

    Actually: It multiplies.

Flashcards

  • Failure rate

    Failures per hour.

  • MTBF

    Mean time between failures.

  • Redundancy

    Backup units.

  • TMR

    Triple modular redundancy, 2-of-3 voting.

  • Availability

    Fraction of time working.

  • k-of-n

    Works if k of n work.

Show answer

How this helps save the planet

Unit 4 · Week 5

Safety Analysis: FMEA and Fault Trees

Hazards, failure modes and effects analysis, risk priority numbers, fault trees with AND and OR gates, and safety-critical design.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Run a simple FMEA.
  • Compute a risk priority number.
  • Evaluate AND and OR gates.
  • Choose mitigations.

Key concepts

  • FMEA works bottom-up: for each part, list how it can fail, the effect, and how it will be detected.
  • A risk priority number multiplies severity, occurrence and detection scores (each 1–10, textbook scale).
  • A fault tree works top-down from an unwanted event; OR gates add risk, AND gates (redundancy) shrink it.
  • For small independent probabilities, an OR gate is nearly the sum and an AND gate is the product.
  • Lessons from accidents shape practice; NASA notes its handbook drew on the Columbia Accident Investigation Board and other reports.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 A sensor failure scores S = 8, O = 4, D = 5. RPN?

Show the steps
  1. 8 × 4 × 5.

Answer: 160.

Example 4.2 Either of two faults (p = 10⁻³ and 2 × 10⁻³) causes loss of data. Probability?

Show the steps
  1. 1 − (0.999)(0.998).

Answer: 2.998 × 10⁻³.

Example 4.3 Loss needs both faults (redundant design).

Show the steps
  1. 10⁻³ × 2 × 10⁻³.

Answer: 2 × 10⁻⁶.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Better detection drops D to 2. New RPN?

    Show answer

    64.

  2. Rare-event approximation for the OR gate?

    Show answer

    3 × 10⁻³.

  3. Which gate does redundancy create?

    Show answer

    AND.

  4. Top-down or bottom-up: fault tree?

    Show answer

    Top-down.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: RPN is a probability.

    Actually: It is a ranking score.

  • Myth: OR gates multiply.

    Actually: AND gates multiply.

  • Myth: Redundant parts fail independently.

    Actually: Common causes can fail both.

Flashcards

  • FMEA

    Failure modes and effects analysis.

  • RPN

    Severity × occurrence × detection.

  • Fault tree

    Top-down logic of failure.

  • OR gate

    Any input causes the event.

  • AND gate

    All inputs needed.

  • Common cause

    One event failing several parts.

Show answer

The same safety tools protect the satellites that track wildfires, storms and sea ice. Probabilities in this unit are sample values.

Unit 5 · Weeks 6–7

Digital Avionics and Data Buses

Binary data, analog-to-digital conversion, quantization, serial data buses (ARINC 429, MIL-STD-1553), message timing and data volume.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute ADC resolution and SNR.
  • Compute message transmission time.
  • Compare bus types.
  • Estimate data volume.

Key concepts

  • An N-bit converter divides its input range into 2ᴺ levels; one step is the least significant bit.
  • Ideal quantization SNR is about 6.02N + 1.76 dB.
  • ARINC 429 is a one-transmitter, many-receiver bus at 100 kbit/s high speed (textbook value); MIL-STD-1553 is a command/response bus at 1 Mbit/s (textbook value).
  • Message time is bits divided by bit rate; overhead and gaps add to it.
  • Higher radiometric resolution (more bits) shows finer differences but makes more data.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 A 12-bit ADC spans 10 V. Step size?

Show the steps
  1. 10/4096.

Answer: 2.44 mV.

Example 5.2 Its ideal SNR?

Show the steps
  1. 6.02 × 12 + 1.76.

Answer: 74.0 dB.

Example 5.3 A 32-bit ARINC 429 word at 100 kbit/s takes how long?

Show the steps
  1. 32/100,000.

Answer: 0.32 ms.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Landsat 9 has 14-bit radiometric resolution. Levels?

    Show answer

    16,384.

  2. SNR for 8 bits?

    Show answer

    49.9 dB.

  3. Same 32-bit word on a 1 Mbit/s bus?

    Show answer

    0.032 ms.

  4. Step for 16 bits over 10 V?

    Show answer

    0.153 mV.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: More bits means more accuracy automatically.

    Actually: Noise and calibration also limit it.

  • Myth: Bit rate equals data rate of useful data.

    Actually: Overhead takes a share.

  • Myth: One bus fits all.

    Actually: Pick by timing, integrity and cost.

    Rounding to levels.

  • Bit rate

    Bits per second.

Unit 6 · Week 8

Navigation Sensors: IMUs, GNSS, Cameras and Lidar

Gyros and accelerometers, inertial navigation drift, satellite navigation ranging, and vision and lidar sensors for autonomy.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute drift from an accelerometer bias.
  • Relate timing error to range error.
  • Compare sensor strengths.
  • Explain why sensors are combined.

Key concepts

  • An inertial measurement unit (IMU) measures rotation rates (gyros) and specific force (accelerometers).
  • Integrating twice turns a small accelerometer bias into a position error that grows with t².
  • Satellite navigation measures range from signal travel time; 1 µs of clock error is about 300 m of range.
  • Cameras and lidar measure the surroundings directly, giving position and hazard information that does not drift.
  • No single sensor is enough: IMUs are fast but drift; external sensors are slower but bounded.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 6.1 An accelerometer bias of 1 milli-g (9.807 × 10⁻³ m/s²) runs uncorrected for 60 s. Position error?

Show the steps
  1. ½ × 9.807 × 10⁻³ × 60².

Answer: 17.7 m.

Example 6.2 After 600 s?

Show the steps
  1. ½ × 9.807 × 10⁻³ × 600².

Answer: 1765 m, 100 times worse for 10 times the time.

Example 6.3 A receiver clock is off by 1 µs. Range error?

Show the steps
  1. 299,792,458 × 10⁻⁶.

Answer: 300 m.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Error after 10 s with the same bias?

    Show answer

    0.49 m.

  2. Gyro bias 0.01°/h for 10 h?

    Show answer

    0.1° of heading.

  3. Why does Dragonfly need lidar at Titan?

    Show answer

    To measure terrain altitude and slope and spot hazards.

  4. Clock error for 3 m of range?

    Show answer

    10 ns.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: IMUs never need correction.

    Actually: Their errors grow with time.

  • Myth: GNSS works everywhere.

    Actually: Not indoors, underwater or at Titan.

  • Myth: Drift grows linearly.

    Actually: Bias drift grows with t².

Flashcards

  • IMU

    Gyros and accelerometers.

  • Dead reckoning

    Navigation by integration.

  • GNSS

    Satellite navigation.

  • Lidar

    Laser ranging.

  • Bias

    Constant sensor offset.

  • Pseudorange

    Range including clock error.

Unit 7 · Week 9

Sensor Fusion and the Kalman Filter

Combining noisy measurements, inverse-variance weighting, the scalar Kalman filter and its predict-update cycle.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Fuse two measurements by variance.
  • Compute a Kalman gain.
  • Run one predict-update step.
  • Explain why fusion beats either sensor.

Key concepts

  • Two independent estimates are best combined by weighting each by the inverse of its variance.
  • The fused variance is smaller than either input variance.
  • The Kalman filter predicts the state forward, then corrects it with a measurement using gain K.
  • K = P/(P + R): trust the measurement more when the prediction is uncertain (large P) and the sensor is good (small R).
  • Navigation systems fuse fast IMU predictions with slower camera, lidar or GNSS updates.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 7.1 Sensors with σ = 4 m and 3 m are fused. Fused σ?

Show the steps
  1. σ² = 16 × 9/25 = 5.76.
  2. σ = √5.76.

Answer: 2.4 m.

Example 7.2 Prediction variance P = 16 m², measurement R = 9 m². Gain?

Show the steps
  1. 16/25.

Answer: 0.64.

Example 7.3 Prediction 100 m, measurement 105 m. Update and new variance?

Show the steps
  1. x = 100 + 0.64 × 5 = 103.2 m.
  2. P = 0.36 × 16.

Answer: 103.2 m with variance 5.76 m².

Work each on paper first, then check.

    Show answer

    σ = 1.41 m.

  2. Gain if R = 0?

    Show answer

    1: trust the measurement fully.

  3. Gain if R is huge?

    Show answer

  4. What grows P between updates?

    Show answer

    Process noise in the prediction.

  • Myth: Average sensors equally.

    Actually: Weight by inverse variance.

  • Myth: Fusion can’t beat the best sensor.

    Actually: It beats both.

  • Myth: Kalman filters remove all error.

    Actually: They minimize variance under assumptions.

Communication and Surveillance: ADS-B

Aircraft radios, line-of-sight range, free-space path loss, ADS-B Out and In, and how surveillance supports air traffic control.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute radio horizon range.
  • Compute free-space path loss.
  • Explain ADS-B Out and In.
  • Put FAA traffic numbers in scale.

Key concepts

  • VHF and higher frequencies travel by line of sight, so range depends on antenna heights.
  • Free-space path loss grows with distance and frequency, 20 log₁₀(4πd/λ).

Unit 9 · Weeks 11–12

Real-Time Flight Software

Tasks, periods and deadlines, CPU utilization, rate-monotonic scheduling, frame rates, watchdogs and fault protection.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute CPU utilization.
  • Apply the rate-monotonic bound.
  • Relate frame rate and period.
  • Describe fault protection.

Key concepts

  • Flight software runs periodic tasks that must finish before their deadlines, not just quickly on average.
  • Utilization is the sum of each task’s run time divided by its period.
  • Rate-monotonic scheduling gives higher priority to shorter periods; it is guaranteed when U ≤ n(2^(1/n) − 1).
  • Watchdog timers reset a processor that stops responding; fault protection puts a spacecraft in a safe mode.
  • Autonomy matters when ground help is far away; NASA says Dragonfly must fly without real-time help from Earth.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 9.1 Tasks take 2, 3 and 5 ms with periods 10, 20 and 50 ms. Utilization?

Show the steps
  1. 0.2 + 0.15 + 0.1.

Answer: 0.45.

Show the steps
  1. 3(2^(1/3) − 1).

Answer: 0.780, so 0.45 is guaranteed schedulable.

Example 9.3 Dragonfly’s camera exposures run about 20 to 70 ms. Matching frame rates?

Show the steps
  1. 1/0.07 and 1/0.02.

Answer: About 14.3 to 50 Hz.
