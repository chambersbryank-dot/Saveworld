How Flight Works

Share to Google Classroom(opens in a new tab)

The four forces, how wings lift, why jets cruise high, how helicopters hover and why planes stay stable, in plain language.

Standards:

  • NGSS HS-PS2-1
  • NGSS MS-PS2-2

Our alignment; not reviewed by the standards bodies.

The four forces

  • Kids

Every airplane feels four pushes and pulls: weight pulls it down, lift from the wings holds it up, thrust from the engines pushes it forward, and drag from the air holds it back. When lift equals weight and thrust equals drag, the plane flies steady.

Check yourself: Which force do the engines make?

Thrust.

How wings lift

  • Kids

A wing is tilted a little and curved, so it turns the air that flows past it downward. Pushing air down pushes the wing up. Faster air and bigger wings make more lift: a small plane needs about 26 m/s (51 knots) just to stay up.

Check yourself: What happens to lift if the plane flies slower?

It drops, unless the wing tilts up more. Too slow and the wing stalls.

Why jets fly high

  • Teens

At 11 km (36 000 ft) the air is only 0.36 kg per cubic metre, less than a third of sea level, and 217 K (-56 °C). Thin air means less drag, so airliners burn less fuel per kilometre, as long as the wings and engines can still work there.

Check yourself: Why can’t a small propeller plane just fly at 11 km too?

Its engine needs air to breathe and its wings would need much more speed; there is not enough power for either.

How helicopters hover

  • Teens

The spinning rotor is a big wing that pushes a column of air downward. A 2700 kg helicopter with 5.1 m blades pushes air down at about 12 m/s through the rotor, and needs at least 305 kW just to hover.

Check yourself: Would longer blades need more or less power to hover?

Less: they move more air more slowly, which costs less energy.

Staying stable

  • Community college

If a gust tips the nose up, the tail is pushed up too and swings the nose back down. Engineers describe this wobble with two numbers: how fast it oscillates (natural frequency) and how quickly it dies out (damping ratio). Pilots like a damping ratio between about 0.35 and 1.3.

Check yourself: What would happen with a damping ratio near zero?

The nose would keep bobbing up and down for a long time, which is tiring and hard to control.
