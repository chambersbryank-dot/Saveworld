About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a sophomore course in ordinary differential equations, modeled on the topics and depth of MIT OpenCourseWare 18.03 and the free textbooks by Jiří Lebl (Notes on Diffy Qs) and William Trench, and on the syllabi of courses such as Georgia Tech MATH 2552 and Stanford Math 53. We link to the free materials throughout. None of those authors or organizations endorses this page.

What we add: every unit connects to a real planet problem with a cited public source (lakes, methane, fisheries, carbon, climate, epidemics, wind turbines), every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and four computer labs solve real models numerically.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Almost every model of the living planet is a differential equation: how fast a lake flushes out a pollutant, how a fish stock recovers, how carbon moves between air and sea, how quickly Earth warms when its energy balance shifts, how an epidemic spreads, and how a wind-turbine tower sways.

Each unit ends with a short, sourced section showing how its ideas help both people and nature. Model parameters that are not from a source are labeled as sample values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Calculus I and II: derivatives, integrals, partial fractions, Taylor series.
  • Matrices, determinants and eigenvalues of 2 × 2 matrices (taught briefly in Unit 6).
  • Calculus III helps for the heat equation but is not required.

How to study

  • Check every solution by substituting it back into the equation.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer.
  • Sketch a slope field or phase portrait before solving; know what the answer should look like.
  • Ask of every model: what are the units, and does the long-run behavior make sense?
  • Solve one problem from each unit numerically too, and compare with the formula.
  • Retake the quizzes a few days later. Spaced practice beats one long session.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer or tablet with a spreadsheet.
  • A free graphing calculator such as Desmos.
  • Optional: Python with NumPy (free in Google Colab).

Unit 1 · Weeks 1–2

First-Order Equations

Slope fields, separable equations, linear equations with integrating factors, exact equations, and when a solution exists and is unique.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Read a slope field and sketch solutions.
  • Solve separable and linear first-order equations.
  • Recognize and solve exact equations.
  • State the existence and uniqueness theorem and spot when it fails.

Key concepts

  • A differential equation relates an unknown function to its derivatives; a solution is a function that makes it true.
  • Separable: dy/dx = g(x)h(y). Divide and integrate both sides.
  • Linear: y′ + p(x)y = q(x). Multiply by the integrating factor μ = e^(∫p dx) so the left side becomes (μy)′.
  • Exact: M dx + N dy = 0 with M_y = N_x is dF = 0, so F(x, y) = C.
  • If f and ∂f/∂y are continuous near (x₀, y₀), then y′ = f(x, y), y(x₀) = y₀ has exactly one solution nearby. Solutions may still blow up in finite time.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 Solve dy/dx = xy, y(0) = 2, and find y(1).

Show the steps
  1. dy/y = x dx, so ln|y| = x²/2 + C.
  2. y = 2e^(x²/2).
  3. y(1) = 2e^0.5.

Answer: ≈ 3.297.

Example 1.2 Solve y′ + 2y = 4, y(0) = 0, and find y(1).

Show the steps
  1. μ = e^(2t), so (e^(2t)y)′ = 4e^(2t).
  2. e^(2t)y = 2e^(2t) + C; y(0) = 0 gives C = −2.
  3. y = 2(1 − e^(−2t)).

Answer: y(1) ≈ 1.729, approaching the steady state 2.

Example 1.3 Solve 2xy dx + (x² + 2y) dy = 0 with y(0) = 1, and find y when x = 1.

Show the steps
  1. M_y = 2x = N_x: exact.
  2. F = x²y + y²; F(0, 1) = 1.
  3. At x = 1: y + y² = 1, so y = (−1 + √5)/2.

Answer: ≈ 0.618.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Solve y′ = y², y(0) = 1. Where does the solution exist?

    Show answer

    y = 1/(1 − t); it blows up at t = 1, even though y² is smooth everywhere.

  2. Solve t y′ + y = t² for t > 0.

    Show answer

    (ty)′ = t², so y = t²/3 + C/t.

  3. Solve y′ = −y/τ.

    Show answer

    y = y₀e^(−t/τ).

  4. Is (y cos x) dx + (sin x + 2y) dy = 0 exact? Solve.

    Show answer

    M_y = cos x = N_x; F = y sin x + y² = C.

  5. Does y′ = √y, y(0) = 0 have a unique solution?

    Show answer

    No: y = 0 and y = t²/4 both work; ∂f/∂y = 1/(2√y) is not continuous at y = 0.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Every first-order equation is separable.

    Actually: y′ = x + y is not; it is linear.

  • Myth: Forgetting +C until the end is fine.

    Actually: Add C when you integrate, before solving for y, or the initial condition comes out wrong.

  • Myth: A smooth right-hand side means the solution lives forever.

    Actually: y′ = y² blows up at a finite time.

Flashcards

  • Separable

    dy/dx = g(x)h(y).

  • Linear first-order

    y′ + p y = q.

  • Integrating factor

    μ = e^(∫p dx).

  • Exact

    M_y = N_x; F = C.

  • Initial value problem

    An ODE plus y(x₀) = y₀.

  • Existence and uniqueness

    f, f_y continuous ⇒ one local solution.

Self-check quiz

1. The integrating factor for y′ + 3y = x is…

Show answer

e^(3x). e^(∫3 dx).

e^(∫3 dx).

2. M dx + N dy = 0 is exact when…

Show answer

M_y = N_x. Mixed partials of F agree.

Mixed partials of F agree.

3. y′ = ky has solution…

Show answer

y₀e^(kt). Separable.

Separable.

4. Uniqueness can fail when…

Show answer

∂f/∂y is discontinuous. e.g. y′ = √y at 0.

e.g. y′ = √y at 0.

How this helps save the planet

The simplest first-order equation, y′ = −y/τ, describes how a pollutant clears from a well-mixed lake or how a gas leaves the atmosphere. Its one number, the time scale τ, decides whether a mistake fades in years or lasts for centuries (Unit 2).

Unit 2 · Week 3

Modeling with First-Order Equations

Turning a situation into an equation: mixing and lake pollution, decay and lifetimes, Newton cooling, logistic growth, harvesting, and equilibria with their stability.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Build rate-in minus rate-out models.
  • Interpret time constants and lifetimes.
  • Find equilibria of autonomous equations and classify their stability.
  • Use the logistic model with harvesting to find maximum sustainable yield.

Key concepts

  • Rate of change = rate in − rate out. Write the units of every term.
  • A well-mixed lake with retention time τ loses a pollutant at rate C/τ once the inflow is clean.
  • For an autonomous equation y′ = f(y), equilibria solve f(y) = 0; an equilibrium is stable if f′(y) < 0.
  • Logistic growth P′ = rP(1 − P/K) levels off at K. With constant harvest H, the largest sustainable harvest is rK/4, at P = K/2.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 Lake Erie’s retention time is 2.6 years and Lake Superior’s 191 years (EPA). If a pollutant’s inflow stops, how long until each lake’s concentration falls to 10%, treating each as well mixed?

Show the steps
  1. C/C₀ = e^(−t/τ) = 0.1, so t = τ ln 10.
  2. Erie: 2.6 × 2.303 = 5.99 years.
  3. Superior: 191 × 2.303 = 440 years.

Answer: About 6 years for Erie but about 440 for Superior. Prevention matters most where flushing is slow.

Example 2.2 EPA says methane lasts about a decade in the atmosphere on average. Treat a methane pulse as decaying with τ = 10 years. What fraction is left after 30 years?

Show the steps
  1. Fraction = e^(−30/10) = e^(−3).

Answer: About 5%. Cutting methane leaks therefore pays off within decades, unlike CO₂, which lingers far longer.

Example 2.3 A fish stock follows P′ = 0.4P(1 − P/10,000) − H (tonnes, years; sample values). Find the maximum sustainable yield.

Show the steps
  1. Growth rP(1 − P/K) is largest at P = K/2 = 5,000 t.
  2. There it equals rK/4 = 0.4 × 10,000/4.

Answer: 1,000 tonnes per year. Harvesting more than that drives the stock to collapse.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Coffee at 90 °C cools in a 20 °C room with T′ = −0.1(T − 20) (minutes). Find T(10).

    Show answer

    T = 20 + 70e^(−1) ≈ 45.8 °C.

  2. A 100 L tank of fresh water gets brine of 0.5 g/L at 2 L/min and drains at 2 L/min. Find the salt after 50 minutes.

    Show answer

    x′ = 1 − x/50, x = 50(1 − e^(−t/50)); x(50) = 50(1 − e^(−1)) ≈ 31.6 g.

  3. Find and classify the equilibria of y′ = y(1 − y).

    Show answer

    y = 0 (f′ = 1, unstable) and y = 1 (f′ = −1, stable).

  4. In the fishery example, what happens if H = 1,200 t/yr?

    Show answer

    rP(1 − P/K) − H < 0 for every P, so the stock declines to zero.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Retention time is how long it takes to remove all the pollutant.

    Actually: After one τ, 37% is still there; exponential decay never quite reaches zero.

  • Myth: More fishing effort always means more catch.

    Actually: Past the maximum sustainable yield, more harvest shrinks the stock and future catch.

  • Myth: An equilibrium is always where the system ends up.

    Actually: Only stable equilibria attract nearby solutions.

Flashcards

  • Balance law

    Rate in − rate out.

  • Time constant

    τ in e^(−t/τ); 63% of the change is done after τ.

  • Retention time

    Lake volume ÷ outflow.

  • Equilibrium

    f(y*) = 0.

  • Stability test

    f′(y*) < 0 stable.

  • Max sustainable yield

    rK/4 at P = K/2.

Self-check quiz

1. With τ = 5 years, the fraction left after 5 years is…

Show answer

e^(−1) ≈ 0.37. e^(−t/τ) at t = τ.

e^(−t/τ) at t = τ.

2. Maximum sustainable yield of logistic growth is…

Show answer

rK/4. Peak of rP(1 − P/K).

Peak of rP(1 − P/K).

3. y* is a stable equilibrium of y′ = f(y) if…

Show answer

f′(y*) < 0. Nearby solutions are pushed back.

Nearby solutions are pushed back.

4. Which lake recovers faster from pollution?

Show answer

Erie (2.6 yr). Shorter retention time.

Shorter retention time.

How this helps save the planet

The EPA lists Great Lakes retention times from 2.6 years (Erie) to 191 years (Superior), which is why toxins entering Lake Superior are a multi-generation problem.

Fishery scientists at NOAA assess stocks with population models built on these ideas; the logistic model’s maximum sustainable yield is the classic starting point.

Unit 3 · Weeks 4–5

Second-Order Linear Equations and Oscillations

Linear equations with constant coefficients, the characteristic equation, and free vibrations: undamped, underdamped, critically damped and overdamped.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Solve ay″ + by′ + cy = 0 with the characteristic equation.
  • Handle real, repeated and complex roots.
  • Model mass-spring-damper systems.
  • Classify damping and compute natural frequency and critical damping.

Key concepts

  • Try y = e^(rt): ar² + br + c = 0. Two real roots give two exponentials; a repeated root gives (c₁ + c₂t)e^(rt); complex roots α ± iβ give e^(αt)(c₁ cos βt + c₂ sin βt).
  • Superposition: sums of solutions of a homogeneous linear equation are solutions.
  • mx″ + cx′ + kx = 0 has natural frequency ω₀ = √(k/m).
  • Damping c < 2√(mk) oscillates and decays (underdamped); c = 2√(mk) returns fastest without overshoot (critical).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 Solve y″ + 4y = 0, y(0) = 1, y′(0) = 0. What is the period?

Show the steps
  1. r² + 4 = 0, r = ±2i.
  2. y = cos 2t.
  3. Period 2π/2.

Answer: y = cos 2t, period π ≈ 3.14.

Example 3.2 Solve y″ + 2y′ + 5y = 0, y(0) = 1, y′(0) = 0, and find y(1).

Show the steps
  1. r = −1 ± 2i.
  2. y = e^(−t)(cos 2t + ½ sin 2t) fits both conditions.
  3. y(1) = e^(−1)(cos 2 + ½ sin 2).

Answer: ≈ 0.0142: the oscillation has nearly died out.

Example 3.3 A 2 kg mass hangs on a spring with k = 50 N/m. Find ω₀, the frequency in hertz, and the critical damping.

Show the steps
  1. ω₀ = √(50/2) = 5 rad/s.
  2. f = 5/(2π) = 0.796 Hz.
  3. c_crit = 2√(2 × 50) = 20 N·s/m.

Answer: 5 rad/s, 0.80 Hz, 20 N·s/m.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Solve y″ − 3y′ + 2y = 0.

    Show answer

    r = 1, 2: y = c₁eᵗ + c₂e^(2t).

  2. Solve y″ + 2y′ + y = 0.

    Show answer

    Repeated r = −1: y = (c₁ + c₂t)e^(−t).

  3. Classify x″ + 6x′ + 5x = 0.

    Show answer

    r = −1, −5: overdamped (c = 6 > 2√5).

  4. Find the period of x″ + 9x = 0.

    Show answer

    ω = 3, period 2π/3 ≈ 2.09.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Complex roots mean complex solutions.

    Actually: Real and imaginary parts give real solutions: e^(αt)cos βt and e^(αt)sin βt.

  • Myth: A repeated root gives only one solution.

    Actually: The second is t e^(rt).

  • Myth: More damping always means faster settling.

    Actually: Overdamped systems creep back slower than critically damped ones.

Flashcards

  • Characteristic equation

    ar² + br + c = 0.

  • Superposition

    Sums of solutions are solutions (homogeneous linear).

  • Repeated root

    (c₁ + c₂t)e^(rt).

  • Natural frequency

    ω₀ = √(k/m).

  • Underdamped

    Oscillates while decaying.

  • Critical damping

    c = 2√(mk).

Self-check quiz

1. Roots 2 ± 3i give solutions…

Show answer

e^(2t)cos 3t, e^(2t)sin 3t. α = 2, β = 3.

α = 2, β = 3.

2. Natural frequency of mx″ + kx = 0:

Show answer

√(k/m). r² = −k/m.

r² = −k/m.

3. Critical damping c equals…

Show answer

2√(mk). Discriminant zero.

Discriminant zero.

4. y″ + 2y′ + y = 0 is…

Show answer

How this helps save the planet

Every tall structure, from a wind-turbine tower to a bridge, has natural frequencies set by its stiffness and mass, exactly ω₀ = √(k/m) in the simplest model. Damping decides how fast gust-driven sway dies out, which affects fatigue and how long a structure lasts.

Unit 4 · Week 6

Forced Vibration and Resonance

Nonhomogeneous equations, undetermined coefficients, the amplitude response of a driven oscillator, beats and resonance.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Find particular solutions by undetermined coefficients.
  • Write the general solution as homogeneous plus particular.
  • Compute the steady-state amplitude of a driven damped oscillator.
  • Explain resonance and how engineers avoid it.

Key concepts

  • General solution = homogeneous solution + one particular solution.
  • Guess the form of the forcing: for cos ωt try A cos ωt + B sin ωt.
  • Driven at frequency ω, a damped oscillator settles into steady motion with amplitude X = F₀/√((k − mω²)² + (cω)²).
  • Near ω = ω₀ the amplitude peaks; with damping ratio ζ = c/c_crit, the peak dynamic amplification is about 1/(2ζ). With no damping, the response grows without bound.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 Find a particular solution of y″ + y = cos 2t.

Show the steps
  1. Try y_p = A cos 2t: −4A + A = 1.
  2. A = −1/3.

Answer: y_p = −⅓ cos 2t (out of phase, since the drive is above the natural frequency).

Example 4.2 Model a wind-turbine tower as a single oscillator with natural frequency 0.30 Hz and damping ratio ζ = 0.01 (sample values). The rotor turns at 12 rpm = 0.20 Hz, and three blades pass the tower at 0.60 Hz. Find the dynamic amplification at each, and at resonance.

Show the steps
  1. At 0.20 Hz, r = 0.667: 1/√((1 − 0.444)² + (0.0133)²) = 1.80.
  2. At 0.60 Hz, r = 2: 1/√(9 + 0.0016) = 0.333.
  3. At r = 1: 1/(2ζ) = 50.

Answer: 1.8 and 0.33, but 50 at resonance. That is why designers keep a tower’s natural frequency away from the rotor and blade-passing frequencies.

Example 4.3 Solve y″ + y = cos t, y(0) = y′(0) = 0.

Show the steps
  1. The forcing matches the natural frequency, so try y_p = t(A cos t + B sin t).
  2. Substituting gives A = 0, B = ½.
  3. y = ½ t sin t.

Answer: The amplitude grows like t/2 without limit: pure resonance.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Find y_p for y″ + 3y′ + 2y = 4.

    Show answer

    y_p = 2.

  2. Find y_p for y″ − y = eᵗ.

    Show answer

    eᵗ solves the homogeneous equation, so y_p = ½ t eᵗ.

  3. A 1 kg mass, k = 100 N/m, c = 2 N·s/m is driven by 10 cos 10t N. Find the steady amplitude.

    Show answer

    k − mω² = 0, so X = 10/(2 × 10) = 0.5 m.

  4. For ζ = 0.05, what is the peak amplification?

    Show answer

    About 1/(2 × 0.05) = 10.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Resonance needs a large force.

    Actually: A small periodic force at the natural frequency builds up a large response.

  • Myth: Damping removes resonance.

    Actually: It caps the peak at about 1/(2ζ), which is still large for lightly damped structures.

  • Myth: Undetermined coefficients works for any forcing.

    Actually: Only for polynomials, exponentials, sines and cosines and their products; otherwise use variation of parameters or Laplace.

Flashcards

  • Particular solution

    Any one solution of the forced equation.

  • Undetermined coefficients

    Guess the forcing’s form.

  • Amplitude response

    X = F₀/√((k − mω²)² + (cω)²).

  • Damping ratio

    ζ = c/(2√(mk)).

  • Resonance

    Large response near ω₀; peak ≈ 1/(2ζ).

  • Beats

    Two close frequencies make a slow envelope.

How this helps save the planet

Unit 5 · Weeks 7–8

Laplace Transforms

Transforming differential equations into algebra: the transform and its table, derivatives, step functions for switched inputs, impulses, and convolution.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute Laplace transforms from the definition and a table.
  • Solve initial value problems with transforms and partial fractions.
  • Model switched inputs with unit step functions and shifts.
  • Model sudden impulses with the delta function.

Key concepts

  • L{f}(s) = ∫₀^∞ e^(−st) f(t) dt turns derivatives into multiplication by s, with initial values built in.
  • Solve for Y(s), split with partial fractions, then invert with the table.
  • A switch flipped at time a is u(t − a); the shift rule is L{u(t − a)f(t − a)} = e^(−as)F(s).
  • An instant kick is δ(t − a), with L{δ(t − a)} = e^(−as).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 Solve y′ − 2y = e^(3t), y(0) = 1.

Show the steps
  1. sY − 1 − 2Y = 1/(s − 3).
  2. Y = 1/((s − 2)(s − 3)) + 1/(s − 2) = [1/(s − 3) − 1/(s − 2)] + 1/(s − 2).
  3. Y = 1/(s − 3).

Answer: y = e^(3t). Check: 3e^(3t) − 2e^(3t) = e^(3t).

Example 5.2 A clean lake starts receiving a steady pollutant inflow at t = 2: y′ + y = u(t − 2), y(0) = 0. Find y(3).

Show the steps
  1. (s + 1)Y = e^(−2s)/s.
  2. 1/(s(s + 1)) = 1/s − 1/(s + 1), so y = (1 − e^(−(t − 2)))u(t − 2).
  3. y(3) = 1 − e^(−1).

Answer: ≈ 0.632 of the final level, one time constant after the switch.

Example 5.3 A resting oscillator y″ + 4y = δ(t − 1) gets a kick at t = 1. Find y(1 + π/4).

Show the steps
  1. (s² + 4)Y = e^(−s).
  2. y = ½ sin(2(t − 1)) u(t − 1).
  3. At t = 1 + π/4: ½ sin(π/2).

Answer: 0.5, the first peak.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: L{fg} = L{f}L{g}.

    Actually: The product of transforms corresponds to convolution, not the product.

  • Myth: Initial conditions are applied at the end.

    Actually: They enter immediately through L{y′} = sY − y(0).

  • Myth: Step functions need piecewise solving.

    Actually: Laplace handles switches in one pass.

Unit 6 · Weeks 9–10

Linear Systems, Eigenvalues and Phase Planes

Systems x′ = Ax, eigenvalue methods, the phase plane, and the classification of equilibria as nodes, saddles, spirals and centers.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Write coupled equations and higher-order equations as first-order systems.
  • Solve x′ = Ax with eigenvalues and eigenvectors.
  • Sketch phase portraits.
  • Classify the origin with the trace and determinant.

Key concepts

  • If Av = λv, then x = e^(λt)v solves x′ = Ax; the general solution combines one such term per eigenvalue.
  • Real eigenvalues of opposite sign: a saddle. Both negative: a stable node.
  • Complex eigenvalues α ± iβ: spirals (stable if α < 0); centers if α = 0.
  • For a 2 × 2 matrix, λ² − (tr A)λ + det A = 0.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 6.1 A two-box carbon model: atmosphere A and ocean O exchange carbon with A′ = −0.2A + 0.1O, O′ = 0.2A − 0.1O (per year; sample rates). Start with 3 units in the air and none in the ocean.

Show the steps
  1. Eigenvalues: tr = −0.3, det = 0, so λ = 0 and −0.3.
  2. λ = 0 has eigenvector (1, 2): the equilibrium split is 1 : 2. Total carbon (3) is conserved, so A → 1, O → 2.
  3. A(t) = 1 + 2e^(−0.3t).

Answer: The air keeps one third of the pulse in this toy model, with time scale 1/0.3 ≈ 3.33 years.

Example 6.2 Classify the origin for A = [[1, 2], [2, 1]].

Show the steps
  1. λ² − 2λ − 3 = 0, λ = 3, −1.

Answer: Opposite signs: a saddle (unstable).

Example 6.3 Classify the origin for A = [[−1, −2], [2, −1]].

Show the steps
  1. tr = −2, det = 5: λ² + 2λ + 5 = 0.
  2. λ = −1 ± 2i.

Answer: A stable spiral: solutions circle inward, decaying like e^(−t).

  2. Eigenvalues of [[2, 0], [0, −3]]?

    Show answer

    2 and −3: a saddle.

  3. Classify [[0, 1], [−4, 0]].

    Show answer

    λ = ±2i: a center (undamped oscillation).

  4. Classify [[−3, 1], [0, −2]].

    Show answer

    λ = −3, −2: a stable node.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Eigenvectors are unique.

    Actually: Any nonzero multiple works.

  • Myth: A zero eigenvalue means no motion.

    Actually: It means a line of equilibria or a conserved quantity, as in the carbon box model.

  • Myth: Complex eigenvalues give complex solutions only.

    Actually: Real and imaginary parts give real solutions.

Flashcards

    x′ = Ax.

  • Eigenvalue method

    x = e^(λt)v.

  • Saddle

    Real eigenvalues of opposite sign.

  • Node

    Real eigenvalues of the same sign.

  • Spiral

    Complex eigenvalues, α ≠ 0.

  • Trace–determinant

    λ² − tr λ + det = 0.

Their eigenvalues are the time scales on which a CO₂ pulse is absorbed: some fast, some taking centuries or longer. NASA’s carbon-cycle overview describes the fast and slow cycles these boxes represent.

Unit 7 · Weeks 11–12

Nonlinear Systems and Stability

Equilibria of nonlinear systems, linearization with the Jacobian, predator-prey cycles, and the SIR epidemic model.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Find equilibria of nonlinear systems.
  • Linearize with the Jacobian and classify equilibria.
  • Analyze the Lotka–Volterra predator-prey model.
  • Use the SIR model: R₀, herd-immunity threshold and final size.

Key concepts

  • Near an equilibrium, a nonlinear system behaves like its linearization x′ = J x, where J is the Jacobian (unless the linearization is a center).
  • Lotka–Volterra: prey x′ = ax − bxy, predators y′ = −cy + dxy. The equilibrium is (c/d, a/b); populations cycle around it.
  • SIR: S′ = −βSI, I′ = βSI − γI, R′ = γI. The basic reproduction number R₀ = β/γ; an outbreak grows only if R₀S > 1.
  • The herd-immunity threshold is 1 − 1/R₀; the final uninfected share s∞ solves ln s∞ = R₀(s∞ − 1).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 7.1 Prey and predators follow x′ = x − 0.02xy, y′ = −0.5y + 0.005xy (sample values, years). Find the coexistence equilibrium and the period of small cycles.

Show the steps
  1. x′ = 0 needs y = 1/0.02 = 50; y′ = 0 needs x = 0.5/0.005 = 100.
  2. The Jacobian there has eigenvalues ±i√(ac) = ±i√0.5.
  3. Period 2π/√0.5.

Answer: 100 prey and 50 predators; small cycles last about 8.9 years, with predators lagging prey.

Example 7.2 An epidemic has β = 0.3 per day and γ = 0.1 per day. Find R₀, the herd-immunity threshold, and the final share ever infected.

Show the steps
  1. R₀ = 3.
  2. Threshold 1 − 1/3 = 0.667.
  3. Iterate s = e^(3(s − 1)): s∞ = 0.0595.

Answer: R₀ = 3; immunity in 67% stops growth; with no intervention about 94% are eventually infected, more than the threshold because the epidemic overshoots.

Example 7.3 Classify the equilibrium P = K of logistic growth P′ = rP(1 − P/K).

Show the steps
  1. f′(P) = r − 2rP/K; f′(K) = −r < 0.

Answer: Stable: small disturbances decay at rate r.

Practice problems

    Show answer

  2. Linearize x′ = sin x near x = 0 and x = π.

    Show answer

  3. If R₀ = 2, what is the herd-immunity threshold?

    Show answer

  4. In the predator-prey example, what happens to the prey if predators are removed?

    Show answer

    x′ = x: exponential growth (until other limits apply).

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Linearization always predicts the behavior.

    Actually: Not when it gives a center; the nonlinear terms decide.

  • Myth: Herd immunity means the epidemic stops at once.

    Actually: Infections decline after the threshold but can still overshoot.

  • Myth: Predator-prey cycles mean the model is unstable.

    Actually: Lotka–Volterra orbits are closed: neutrally stable, neither growing nor shrinking.

Unit 8 · Week 13

Numerical Methods

Euler, improved Euler and Runge–Kutta methods, step size and error, and a climate energy-balance model solved numerically.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Apply Euler, improved Euler (Heun) and RK4.
  • Explain the order of a method and how error scales with step size.
  • Solve systems numerically.
  • Build and solve a zero-dimensional energy-balance climate model.

Key concepts

  • Euler steps along the slope: y_{n+1} = y_n + h f(t_n, y_n). Its global error is proportional to h.
  • Improved Euler averages two slopes (error ∝ h²); RK4 averages four (error ∝ h⁴).
  • Halving h cuts RK4 error about 16 times, which is why it is the workhorse.
  • Energy balance: C dT/dt = absorbed sunlight − εσT⁴. Its equilibrium and time scale come from the equation directly.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 8.1 Approximate y(1) for y′ = y, y(0) = 1 with h = 0.1 using Euler, improved Euler and RK4.

Show the steps
  1. Each Euler step multiplies by 1.1: 1.1¹⁰ = 2.5937.
  2. Improved Euler multiplies by 1.105: 2.7141.
  3. RK4 multiplies by 1.1051708: 2.7182797.

Answer: True e = 2.7182818. Errors: 0.12, 0.0042 and 0.000002.

Example 8.2 Earth absorbs 340 × (1 − 0.29) = 241.4 W/m² of sunlight (NASA). If it radiated as a blackbody (ε = 1), what temperature would balance it? What effective ε gives today’s average of about 288 K?

Show the steps
  1. σT⁴ = 241.4 gives T = (241.4/5.67 × 10⁻⁸)^(1/4) = 255 K.
  2. ε = 241.4/(σ × 288⁴) = 0.619.

Answer: 255 K (−18 °C) without a greenhouse effect; an effective emissivity of 0.62 represents the greenhouse effect that keeps Earth habitable.

Example 8.3 With ε = 0.619 at 288 K and an ocean mixed layer 50 m deep (C = 1,000 × 4,186 × 50 J/(m²·K); sample depth), find λ, the time scale, and the no-feedback warming for a 4 W/m² forcing (sample value).

Show the steps
  1. λ = 4εσT³ = 3.35 W/(m²·K).
  2. τ = C/λ = 6.24 × 10⁷ s ≈ 1.98 years (about 2).
  3. ΔT = 4/3.35.

Answer: About 1.2 K of warming, approached on a 2-year time scale in this simple model. Real feedbacks (water vapor, ice) make the warming larger, as the IPCC assesses.

  • Myth: Smaller steps are always better.

    Actually: Round-off and cost grow; a higher-order method is usually better than tiny Euler steps.

  • Myth: A numerical answer is exact if the code runs.

    Actually: Always check convergence by halving h.

  • Myth: The 255 K result means the model is wrong.

    Actually: It shows how much the greenhouse effect warms Earth: about 33 K.

Unit 9 · Week 14

Series Solutions, Fourier Series and the Heat Equation

Power-series solutions of ODEs, Fourier series of periodic functions, and separation of variables for the heat equation.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Find power-series solutions from a recurrence.
  • Compute Fourier coefficients of simple periodic functions.
  • Solve the heat equation on a rod by separation of variables.
  • Estimate how deep seasonal temperature waves reach into the ground.

Key concepts

  • Substitute y = Σaₙxⁿ, match powers, and get a recurrence for the coefficients.
  • A periodic function is a sum of sines and cosines; for an odd function on (−π, π), bₙ = (2/π)∫₀^π f sin nx dx.
  • The heat equation u_t = α u_xx spreads heat; each sine mode decays like e^(−α(nπ/L)²t), fast modes first.
  • A periodic surface temperature penetrates the ground with damping depth d = √(2α/ω).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 9.1 Solve y″ + y = 0 by power series.

Show the steps
  1. Σ(n + 2)(n + 1)aₙ₊₂xⁿ + Σaₙxⁿ = 0.
  2. aₙ₊₂ = −aₙ/((n + 2)(n + 1)).
  3. Even terms build cos x, odd terms sin x.

Answer: y = a₀ cos x + a₁ sin x, recovered from the recurrence.

Example 9.2 Find the Fourier series of the square wave f = 1 on (0, π), −1 on (−π, 0).

Show the steps
  1. Odd, so only sines: bₙ = (2/π)∫₀^π sin nx dx = (2/(nπ))(1 − cos nπ).
  2. bₙ = 4/(nπ) for odd n, 0 for even n.

Answer: f = (4/π)(sin x + sin 3x/3 + sin 5x/5 + …); b₁ ≈ 1.273.

Example 9.3 Soil with diffusivity α = 5 × 10⁻⁷ m²/s (sample value) feels a yearly surface temperature cycle. Find the damping depth.

Show the steps
  1. ω = 2π/(3.156 × 10⁷ s) = 1.99 × 10⁻⁷ s⁻¹.
  2. d = √(2 × 5 × 10⁻⁷/1.99 × 10⁻⁷).

Answer: About 2.24 m: the yearly swing shrinks by a factor e for each 2.2 m of depth, so deeper ground responds to warming slowly, over many years.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Fourier series only work for smooth functions.

    Actually: They work for jumps too, converging to the midpoint at a jump.

  • Myth: Heat diffuses at a fixed speed.

    Actually: Diffusion time grows with distance squared.

  • Myth: Series solutions are always better than formulas.

    Actually: They are most useful when no closed form exists, as for Airy or Bessel equations.

Midterm practice set (Units 1–5)

  1. Solve y′ = −2ty, y(0) = 3, and find y(1).

    Show full solution
    1. dy/y = −2t dt; y = 3e^(−t²).

    Answer: 3e^(−1) ≈ 1.104.

  2. A lake with retention time 4 years: how long until a pollutant falls to half after inflow stops?

    Show full solution
    1. t = 4 ln 2.

    Answer: ≈ 2.77 years.

  3. Solve y″ + 6y′ + 9y = 0, y(0) = 1, y′(0) = 0, and find y(1).

    Show full solution
    1. y = (1 + 3t)e^(−3t).

    Answer: 4e^(−3) ≈ 0.199.

    1. Y = 1/(s(s + 1)) = 1/s − 1/(s + 1).
