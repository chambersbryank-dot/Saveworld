About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows first-year engineering graphics and design courses such as MIT 2.007 and 2.00b, Purdue’s engineering and computer graphics courses, Georgia Tech’s ME 1770 and Embry-Riddle’s engineering graphics sequence, using free tools: the Onshape Free plan and FreeCAD. We link to free materials throughout. None of these organizations endorse this page.

What we add: every unit connects design choices to material, energy and waste, every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and four hands-on labs take you from a sketch to a printed, lighter part.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Most of a product’s material, energy and waste is locked in at the drawing board. Engineers who model and draw well can remove material that does no work, choose parts that last and can be repaired, and specify tolerances no tighter than needed.

Each unit ends with a short, sourced section showing how its ideas help people and the planet.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • High-school geometry and trigonometry.
  • Comfort with a computer and a mouse; a three-button mouse helps for 3D.
  • No drawing talent required: sketching is a skill you will practice.

How to study

  • Sketch by hand before you model. Five minutes on paper saves an hour in CAD.
  • Rebuild each worked example in CAD yourself.
  • Name your sketches and features; future you will thank you.
  • Check every drawing as if you had to make the part from it alone.
  • Do every practice problem before opening the answer.
  • Retake the quizzes a few days later. Spaced practice beats one long session.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer with a web browser (Onshape runs in the browser; a free account is needed) or FreeCAD installed.
  • Grid or isometric paper, a pencil, an eraser and a ruler.
  • A caliper (a low-cost digital caliper is fine).
  • Optional: access to a 3D printer at a library, school or makerspace.

Unit 1 · Week 1

Sketching & Visualization

Freehand sketching as an engineering tool: lines, circles, proportion, and seeing 3D objects in your head.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Sketch straight lines, circles and arcs freehand with good proportion.
  • Use construction boxes to sketch objects in proportion.
  • Read and sketch simple objects in oblique and isometric pictorial views.
  • Explain the role of sketching in the design process.

Key concepts

  • Engineers sketch to think, to explore many ideas fast and to communicate. A quick sketch costs minutes; a wrong CAD model can cost hours.
  • Block in the overall size with a light construction box first, then add features, then darken the final lines.
  • Proportion matters more than exact size in a sketch: estimate ratios such as width : height.
  • Spatial visualization, the ability to rotate and slice objects mentally, is a skill that improves with practice.
  • Line weights carry meaning: thick visible lines, thin hidden (dashed) lines, thin center lines (long-short dash).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 A block is 120 mm wide and 80 mm tall. You sketch its width as 60 mm. How tall should the sketch be?

Show the steps
  1. Scale = 60/120 = 0.5.
  2. Height = 80 × 0.5.

Answer: 40 mm (a 1:2 sketch).

Example 1.2 A part is 450 mm long. Which standard scale fits it on a 250 mm drawing area: 1:1, 1:2 or 1:5?

Show the steps
  1. 1:1 → 450 mm (too big).
  2. 1:2 → 225 mm (fits).

Answer: 1:2, the largest scale that fits, so details stay readable.

Example 1.3 At scale 2:1, a 3 mm hole is drawn how large?

Show the steps
  1. 3 × 2.

Answer: 6 mm on paper (dimensions still say the real 3 mm).

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. At 1:5, how long is a 1,200 mm beam on paper?

    Show answer

    240 mm.

  2. A sketch is drawn at 1:2. A line measures 35 mm. What is the real length?

    Show answer

    70 mm.

  3. Which line type shows a hidden edge?

    Show answer

    A thin dashed line.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Sketching is only for artists.

    Actually: It is a learnable engineering skill that improves quickly with practice.

  • Myth: Dimensions on a scaled drawing show the drawn size.

    Actually: They always show the real size.

  • Myth: Start with the details.

    Actually: Block in the overall envelope first, then add details.

Flashcards

  • Construction lines

    Light guide lines, erased or left faint.

  • Visible line

    Thick, solid.

  • Hidden line

    Thin, dashed.

  • Center line

    Thin, long-short dash.

  • Scale

    Drawing size ÷ real size.

  • Spatial visualization

    Mentally rotating and slicing 3D objects.

Self-check quiz

1. A center line is drawn as…

Show answer

thin long-short dash. Standard line convention.

Standard line convention.

2. At 1:2, a 100 mm part is drawn…

Show answer

50 mm. Half size.

Half size.

3. The first step in sketching an object is…

Show answer

block in a light construction box. Envelope first.

Envelope first.

4. Hidden edges use…

Show answer

dashed lines. Thin dashed lines.

Thin dashed lines.

How this helps save the planet

The cheapest place to save material is in a sketch. Ideas such as fewer parts, standard fasteners and repairable joints are easy to explore on paper and expensive to change after tooling is made.

EPA’s sustainable materials management approach looks at materials across their whole life cycle, starting with design.

Unit 2 · Weeks 2–3

Orthographic & Isometric Projection

The language of technical drawing: multiview (orthographic) projection and isometric pictorials.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Create front, top and right-side views with correct alignment.
  • Know first-angle and third-angle projection symbols.
  • Draw isometric views of simple parts.
  • Find true length and foreshortening.

Key concepts

  • Orthographic projection shows an object with parallel projectors perpendicular to each view plane. Three views (front, top, right) usually describe a part fully.
  • Third-angle projection (U.S., ASME) places the top view above the front view; first-angle (much of Europe and Asia, ISO) places it below. A symbol in the title block says which.
  • Views stay aligned: width is shared by front and top, height by front and side, depth by top and side.
  • An isometric view tilts the object so its three axes are 120° apart. Edges along the axes are foreshortened by √(2/3) ≈ 0.816; an isometric drawing ignores that and draws them full length.
  • A line shows its true length only in a view parallel to it.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 A cube edge is 100 mm. How long is it in a true isometric projection, and in an isometric drawing?

Show the steps
  1. Projection: 100 × 0.816.
  2. Drawing: full size by convention.

Answer: 81.6 mm in projection; 100 mm in an isometric drawing.

Example 2.2 An edge runs from (0, 0, 0) to (30, 40, 0) mm. What is its true length, and in which view does it appear true length?

Show the steps
  1. √(30² + 40²) = 50.

Answer: 50 mm; true length in the view looking along z (it lies in the x–y plane).

Example 2.3 An edge runs from (0, 0, 0) to (20, 20, 10). Find its true length.

Show the steps
  1. √(400 + 400 + 100) = √900.

Answer: 30 mm. No principal view shows it true length; you need an auxiliary view (Unit 3).

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. In third-angle projection, where is the right-side view placed?

    Show answer

    To the right of the front view.

  2. Which two views share the depth dimension?

    Show answer

    Top and side.

  3. Find the true length of an edge from (0, 0, 0) to (12, 16, 0).

    Show answer

    20.

  4. In a true isometric projection, how long does a 50 mm edge along an axis appear?

    Show answer

    ≈ 40.8 mm.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Isometric drawings are drawn at 81.6%.

    Actually: Isometric drawings use full length; only isometric projection is foreshortened.

  • Myth: First- and third-angle are just different styles.

    Actually: They swap view positions; misreading the symbol mirrors the part.

  • Myth: More views are always better.

    Actually: Use the fewest views that fully describe the part.

Flashcards

  • Orthographic projection

    Parallel, perpendicular projectors; multiple views.

  • Third-angle

    Top above front (U.S.).

  • First-angle

    Top below front (ISO in much of the world).

  • Isometric

    Axes 120° apart.

  • Foreshortening

    Edge appears shorter than true length.

  • True length

    Seen only in a parallel view.

Self-check quiz

1. In third-angle projection the top view is…

Show answer

above the front view. ASME convention.

ASME convention.

2. Isometric axes are separated by…

Show answer

120°. Equal angles.

Equal angles.

3. Isometric foreshortening factor ≈

Show answer

0.816. √(2/3).

√(2/3).

4. Front and top views share…

Show answer

width. They are aligned vertically.

They are aligned vertically.

How this helps save the planet

Clear drawings prevent scrap. A misread projection symbol can produce a mirror-image part, and every scrapped part wastes the material and energy that went into it.

International standards for projection and tolerancing let one drawing be made correctly anywhere, so parts can be sourced locally and replacement parts can be made to fit.

Unit 3 · Week 4

Section & Auxiliary Views

Cut parts open to show their insides, and rotate the view to see slanted faces at true size.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Draw full, half, offset and broken-out sections with hatching.
  • Apply section conventions (ribs, shafts and fasteners not hatched lengthwise).
  • Construct a primary auxiliary view of an inclined surface.
  • Find true size and true length.

Key concepts

  • A section view imagines a cutting plane slicing the part; the removed half lets interior features show as visible lines instead of confusing hidden lines.
  • Cut material is hatched with thin lines, typically at 45°. Thin ribs, shafts, bolts and pins cut along their length are conventionally not hatched.
  • A cutting-plane line with arrows and letters (A–A) shows where the cut is and which way you look.
  • An inclined surface is foreshortened in the principal views. An auxiliary view projects onto a plane parallel to it, showing its true size.
  • Inclined length = √(horizontal² + vertical²) in the view where it appears as an edge.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 An inclined face appears as an edge in the front view, rising 30 mm over 40 mm. The part is 25 mm deep. Find the face’s true size.

Show the steps
  1. Edge length = √(30² + 40²) = 50 mm.
  2. True size = 50 × 25.

Answer: 50 mm × 25 mm = 1,250 mm².

Example 3.2 That face is projected in the top view. What area does it appear to have there?

Show the steps
  1. Top view sees the 40 mm horizontal run × 25 mm.

Answer: 1,000 mm², which is 1,250 × cos θ with cos θ = 40/50 = 0.8.

Example 3.3 A tube is 40 mm outside diameter with 3 mm walls. In a full section, how wide is each hatched band?

Show the steps
  1. Wall thickness = 3 mm on each side.
  2. Inner diameter = 40 − 6 = 34 mm.

Answer: Two 3 mm hatched bands, with a 34 mm open bore between them.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Why are ribs not hatched when cut lengthwise?

    Show answer

    Hatching would make a thin rib look like a solid mass and misrepresent the part.

  2. A face rises 5 mm over 12 mm. True length of its edge?

    Show answer

    13 mm.

  3. A 60 mm-long face is tilted so cos θ = 0.6 in the top view. What length does the top view show?

    Show answer

    36 mm.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Sections show hidden lines too.

    Actually: Normally hidden lines are omitted in sections.

  • Myth: Auxiliary views must show the whole part.

    Actually: Partial auxiliary views showing just the inclined feature are standard.

  • Myth: Every cut surface is hatched.

    Actually: Ribs, shafts and fasteners cut lengthwise are conventionally left unhatched.

Flashcards

  • Section view

    Imaginary cut to show the interior.

  • Cutting-plane line

    Thick line with arrows and letters.

  • Hatching

    Thin lines on cut material.

  • Half section

    Quarter removed; half shows inside.

  • Auxiliary view

    View parallel to an inclined face.

  • True size

    Undistorted shape and area.

Self-check quiz

1. Hatching shows…

Show answer

cut material. Material the cutting plane passes through.

Material the cutting plane passes through.

2. An auxiliary view is parallel to…

Show answer

the inclined surface. So it shows true size.

So it shows true size.

3. A half section shows…

Show answer

one quarter removed. Inside and outside at once for symmetric parts.

Inside and outside at once for symmetric parts.

4. True length of an edge rising 9 over 12 is…

Show answer

15. √(81 + 144).

√(81 + 144).

How this helps save the planet

Sections are how designers check wall thicknesses, and thinner walls where loads allow mean less material. That is lightweighting at the drawing board (Unit 9).

DOE reports that replacing heavy steel parts with lighter materials can cut component weight by 10–60% in the short term.

Unit 4 · Weeks 5–6

Dimensioning & GD&T Basics

Say exactly what size and shape a part must be: dimensioning rules, datums and geometric tolerances.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Dimension parts clearly following ASME Y14.5 conventions.
  • Choose datums based on function.
  • Read feature control frames for flatness, perpendicularity, position and profile.
  • Compute position error and bonus tolerance at MMC.

Key concepts

  • Every feature is dimensioned once, in the view that shows it best, without redundant or chained dimensions that stack up errors.
  • Geometric dimensioning and tolerancing (GD&T), standardized in ASME Y14.5 (current edition 2018), controls form, orientation, location and profile relative to datums, the reference surfaces that mate in assembly.
  • A feature control frame reads: symbol | tolerance (with Ø for a cylindrical zone) | modifier | datums, e.g. ⌖ Ø0.1 Ⓜ A B C.
  • Position error of a hole = 2√(Δx² + Δy²); it must lie within the cylindrical tolerance zone.
  • At maximum material condition (MMC), a hole gets a bonus: as it departs from MMC (gets bigger), its allowed position tolerance grows by the same amount.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 A hole should be at (50, 30). It measures (50.03, 29.98). Position tolerance is Ø0.1. Does it pass?

Show the steps
  1. Δx = 0.03, Δy = 0.02.
  2. Ø = 2√(0.0009 + 0.0004) = 0.072.

Answer: Ø0.072 ≤ Ø0.1: passes.

Example 4.2 The hole is Ø10.0–10.2 with position Ø0.1 at MMC. It measures Ø10.15. What position tolerance is allowed?

Show the steps
  1. MMC of a hole = smallest size = 10.0.
  2. Bonus = 10.15 − 10.0 = 0.15.
  3. Allowed = 0.1 + 0.15.

Answer: Ø0.25.

Example 4.3 Three chained dimensions of 20 ± 0.1 each locate a feature. What is the worst-case variation of the total?

Show the steps
  1. Tolerances add: 3 × 0.1.

Answer: ± 0.3 mm. Dimensioning each from one datum would hold it to ± 0.1.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. A hole measures 0.04 off in x and 0.03 off in y. Position error?

    Show answer

    Ø0.10.

  2. With Ø0.1 at MMC, a Ø10.0–10.2 hole measured at Ø10.05 is allowed what position tolerance?

    Show answer

    Ø0.15.

  3. What does a datum represent?

    Show answer

    A theoretically exact reference, usually where the part mates or is fixtured.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Position error equals the distance off.

    Actually: The tolerance zone is a diameter, so error = 2 × the radial offset.

  • Myth: Tighter tolerances always make better parts.

    Actually: They raise cost and scrap; tolerance only what function needs.

  • Myth: MMC of a hole is its largest size.

    Actually: Maximum material for a hole is the smallest hole.

Flashcards

  • GD&T

    Geometric dimensioning and tolerancing.

  • Datum

    Exact reference feature.

  • Feature control frame

    Symbol | tolerance | modifiers | datums.

  • Position

    Location tolerance, usually a diameter zone.

  • MMC

    Maximum material condition.

  • Bonus tolerance

    Extra tolerance as a feature departs from MMC.

Self-check quiz

1. ASME Y14.5 covers…

Show answer

dimensioning and tolerancing. The GD&T standard.

The GD&T standard.

2. A hole 0.03 and 0.04 off has position error…

Show answer

Ø0.10. 2 × 0.05.

2 × 0.05.

3. MMC of a shaft is…

Show answer

largest size. Most material.

Most material.

4. Chained dimensions…

Show answer

stack up tolerances. Errors add along the chain.

Errors add along the chain.

How this helps save the planet

Tolerancing well is a waste-reduction tool. Functional GD&T and MMC bonus tolerance accept parts that will assemble and work, instead of scrapping them over arbitrary tight numbers.

NIST works on model-based definition, putting tolerances directly in the 3D model so they are read correctly by machines and inspectors, which reduces errors and rework.

Unit 5 · Week 7

Tolerances & Fits

How much size variation parts can have and still fit together: limits, ISO fits, and tolerance stack-ups.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute limits, clearance and interference.
  • Read ISO hole-basis fits such as H7/g6 and H7/p6.
  • Do worst-case and root-sum-square (RSS) stack-ups.
  • Choose a fit for a function.

Key concepts

  • Limits are the largest and smallest allowed sizes. Tolerance = upper limit − lower limit.
  • Clearance fits always leave a gap; interference fits always overlap (pressed together); transition fits may do either.
  • ISO 286 codes fits as hole letter/number over shaft letter/number. H holes start at the nominal size; the number is the IT grade. For sizes 18–30 mm, IT7 = 21 µm and IT6 = 13 µm.
  • Worst-case stack-up adds all tolerances; RSS assumes independent random variation: √(Σtᵢ²), smaller and more realistic for many parts.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 A Ø25 H7/g6 fit: hole 25.000–25.021, shaft 24.980–24.993. Find the clearance range.

Show the steps
  1. c_min = 25.000 − 24.993 = 0.007.
  2. c_max = 25.021 − 24.980 = 0.041.

Answer: Clearance 0.007–0.041 mm: a precise sliding fit.

Example 5.2 A Ø25 H7/p6 fit: hole 25.000–25.021, shaft 25.022–25.035. Find the fit.

Show the steps
  1. Smallest overlap = 25.022 − 25.021 = 0.001.
  2. Largest = 25.035 − 25.000 = 0.035.

Answer: Interference 0.001–0.035 mm: a press fit.

Example 5.3 Parts of 20 ± 0.10, 30 ± 0.05 and 10 ± 0.05 mm stack. Find worst-case and RSS tolerance.

Show the steps
  1. Worst case: 0.10 + 0.05 + 0.05 = 0.20.
  2. RSS: √(0.01 + 0.0025 + 0.0025) = 0.122.

Answer: 60 ± 0.20 mm worst case; 60 ± 0.12 mm RSS.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. A hole is 12.000–12.018 and a shaft 11.984–11.995. Clearance range?

    Show answer

    0.005–0.034 mm.

  2. What is the tolerance of the H7 hole above?

    Show answer

    0.021 mm = 21 µm.

  3. Four parts each ± 0.05: worst-case and RSS?

    Show answer

    ± 0.20 and ± 0.10.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: RSS is always safe.

    Actually: It assumes independent, centered variation; for safety-critical fits use worst case.

  • Myth: Interference fits need no calculation.

    Actually: Too much interference can crack parts; compute the range.

  • Myth: The tolerance number is in millimeters.

    Actually: IT grades map to micrometer values that depend on size.

Flashcards

  • Limits

    Largest and smallest allowed sizes.

  • Clearance fit

    Always a gap.

  • Interference fit

    Always overlap.

  • Transition fit

    Gap or overlap.

  • Worst-case stack

    Σtᵢ.

  • RSS stack

    √Σtᵢ².

Self-check quiz

1. H7/g6 is a…

Show answer

clearance fit. g shafts are below the H hole.

g shafts are below the H hole.

2. RSS for ±0.3 and ±0.4 is…

Show answer

±0.5. √(0.09 + 0.16).

√(0.09 + 0.16).

3. IT7 for 18–30 mm sizes is…

Show answer

21 µm. ISO 286 table.

ISO 286 table.

4. An H hole’s lower limit is…

Show answer

exactly nominal. Hole-basis system.

Hole-basis system.

How this helps save the planet

Good fits make products repairable: standard ISO fits let bearings, pins and bushings be replaced with off-the-shelf parts years later, keeping a machine in service instead of in a landfill.

Choosing RSS where appropriate allows looser individual tolerances, which means cheaper processes and less scrap for the same assembly quality.

Unit 6 · Weeks 8–9

Parametric 3D Modeling

Build solid models from constrained sketches and features in Onshape or FreeCAD, and make them easy to change.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Create fully constrained 2D sketches.
  • Use extrude, revolve, fillet, chamfer, hole, shell and pattern features.
  • Drive models with variables and equations (design intent).
  • Compute mass properties and use them in design.

Key concepts

  • A parametric model is a recipe: sketches plus features in a history tree. Change a dimension and the model rebuilds.
  • A sketch is fully constrained when geometric constraints (parallel, tangent, coincident) and dimensions fix every degree of freedom. Each free 2D point has 2 degrees of freedom.
  • Design intent: dimension the way the part functions. If holes must stay centered, constrain them to the midpoint, not to an edge.
  • Variables (Onshape) and spreadsheets or expressions (FreeCAD) let one number drive many features.
  • CAD computes volume, mass, center of mass and moments of inertia from the material density.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 6.1 An aluminum plate is 100 × 60 × 5 mm with four Ø6 through holes. Find its mass (ρ = 2.70 g/cm³).

Show the steps
  1. V = 100 × 60 × 5 − 4 × π × 3² × 5 = 30,000 − 565.5 = 29,434.5 mm³.
  2. m = 29.43 cm³ × 2.70.

Answer: ≈ 79.5 g.

Example 6.2 A rectangle drawn with the rectangle tool has 4 corner points (8 DOF) and comes with 2 horizontal and 2 vertical constraints. How many more dimensions or constraints fully constrain it?

Show the steps
  1. 8 − 4 = 4 DOF remain: width, height, and x and y position.
  2. Add width and height, then make one corner coincident with the origin (removes 2).

Answer: 4 more: two dimensions plus two for position (e.g. a corner coincident with the origin).

Example 6.3 A ring is made by revolving a 10 × 4 mm rectangle whose centroid is 25 mm from the axis. Find its volume.

Show the steps
  1. A = 40 mm².
  2. V = 2π × 25 × 40.

Answer: ≈ 6,283 mm³ (6.28 cm³).

Practice problems

    Actually: Check for under-constrained sketches and fragile references; they break on edits.

  • Myth: Dimension from whatever edge is handy.

    Actually: Dimension from functional datums to capture intent.

    Actually: Only as good as the density and model you enter.

Unit 7 · Weeks 10–11

Assemblies & Drawings

Put parts together with mates, check motion and interference, and produce drawings someone can build from.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Assemble parts with mates (fastened, revolute, slider) and count degrees of freedom.
  • Check interference and clearance.
  • Produce detail and assembly drawings with a title block, bill of materials and balloons.
  • Use Gruebler’s equation for planar mechanisms.

Key concepts

  • A free rigid body has 6 degrees of freedom (3 translations, 3 rotations). A revolute mate leaves 1; a slider leaves 1; a fastened mate leaves 0.
  • Bottom-up assembly inserts finished parts; top-down designs parts in context of the assembly.
  • A planar mechanism’s mobility is M = 3(n − 1) − 2j₁ − j₂ (Gruebler), with n links including ground, j₁ full joints and j₂ half joints.
  • Drawings come from the model: views, sections, dimensions and notes, a title block (part, material, scale, projection symbol, units, tolerances) and, for assemblies, a bill of materials (BOM) with balloons.
  • Interference checks catch parts that overlap before anything is made.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 7.1 Find the mobility of a four-bar linkage (4 links including ground, 4 pin joints).

Show the steps
  1. M = 3(4 − 1) − 2(4) = 9 − 8.

Answer: M = 1: one input drives it.

Example 7.2 A slider-crank: ground, crank, connecting rod, slider; 3 pins and 1 slider joint.

Show the steps
  1. n = 4, j₁ = 4 (pins and slider are full joints).
  2. M = 9 − 8.

Answer: M = 1, as in an engine.

Example 7.3 A BOM lists 4 × M5 screws at 1.2 g, 1 plate at 79.5 g and 2 brackets at 15.0 g. Total assembly mass?

Show the steps
  1. 4.8 + 79.5 + 30.0.

Answer: 114.3 g.

Practice problems

  2. Mobility of 3 links, 3 pins?

    Show answer

  3. What must every title block state?

    Show answer

    Part name/number, material, scale, units, projection method, general tolerances, author and revision.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: More mates are safer.

    Actually: Redundant mates over-constrain and can fail to solve or hide errors.

  • Myth: Assembly drawings need every dimension.

    Actually: Detail drawings carry part dimensions; assembly drawings show how parts go together.

  • Myth: If the model fits, the real parts will.

    Actually: Only if tolerances are checked (Unit 5).

A clear assembly drawing and BOM are a repair manual. Designs that use standard fasteners and can be taken apart are easier to fix, refurbish and recycle, which is a core idea of sustainable materials management.

Unit 8 · Weeks 12–13

3D Printing & Design for Manufacture

How parts are actually made, and how to design them so they are easy, cheap and low-waste to make, including 3D printing.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Describe additive (3D printing), subtractive (machining) and forming processes.
  • Design for FDM printing: overhangs, supports, wall thickness, orientation.
  • Estimate print mass, filament length and time.
  • Apply design-for-manufacture and assembly (DFM/DFA) rules.

Key concepts

  • Additive manufacturing builds parts layer by layer; NIST works on measurement science and standards to make AM parts reliable. Machining removes material; molding and casting shape it.
  • Fused-filament (FDM) printing: overhangs steeper than about 45° from vertical usually need supports; orient parts so loads do not pull layers apart, because layer bonds are the weak direction.
  • Infill (often 10–20%) and a few perimeter walls make prints light; solid prints waste material and time.
  • Filament length per gram = 1/(ρπr²). For 1.75 mm PLA (ρ = 1.24 g/cm³) that is about 33.5 cm per gram.
  • DFM/DFA: fewer parts, standard features and fasteners, generous radii, self-locating features, and avoiding needlessly tight tolerances.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 8.1 How much 1.75 mm PLA filament does a 10 g print use?

Show the steps
  1. Cross-section π(0.0875 cm)² = 0.02405 cm².
  2. Length per gram = 1/(1.24 × 0.02405) = 33.5 cm.
  3. × 10.

Answer: ≈ 3.35 m.

Example 8.2 Estimate print time for 10 g of PLA with a 0.4 mm line, 0.2 mm layers at 50 mm/s.

Show the steps
  1. V = 10/1.24 = 8.065 cm³ = 8,065 mm³.
  2. Rate = 0.4 × 0.2 × 50 = 4 mm³/s.
  3. t = 8,065/4 = 2,016 s.

Answer: ≈ 34 minutes of extrusion (real prints take longer due to travel moves).

Example 8.3 A 20 mm solid PLA cube weighs 9.9 g. At about 15% infill with walls, it weighs about 3.5 g. What fraction of material is saved?

Show the steps
  1. (9.9 − 3.5)/9.9.

Answer: ≈ 65% less plastic (sample estimate; your slicer reports the real figure).

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  2. Why print a bracket so layers run along the load path?

    Show answer

  3. Name two DFA rules.

    Show answer

    Reduce part count; use self-locating features and standard fasteners.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: 3D printing is always greener.

    Actually: It can save material, but energy, failed prints and non-recycled waste matter; compare per part.

  • Myth: Prints are as strong in every direction.

    Actually: FDM parts are weakest between layers.

  • Myth: 100% infill is strongest so always best.

    Actually: Walls carry most bending load; extra infill adds mass and time with little benefit.

How this helps save the planet

Plastics are a large waste stream: EPA reports that U.S. plastics generation was 35.7 million tons in 2018, 12.2% of municipal solid waste, and only 8.7% was recycled. Designing prints with low infill, few supports and fewer failed attempts directly cuts that waste.

Additive manufacturing can place material only where loads need it, enabling lighter parts; NIST’s work aims to make those parts reliable enough for demanding uses.

Unit 9 · Week 14

Design for Sustainability & Lightweighting

Use your CAD skills to make parts lighter, longer-lasting and easier to repair and recycle, and estimate the payoff.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compare sections by mass and stiffness (second moment of area).
  • Estimate fuel and CO₂ savings from vehicle lightweighting.
  • Apply design-for-repair, disassembly and recycling principles.
  • Choose materials with their whole life cycle in mind.

Key concepts

  • Bending stiffness depends on EI. Moving material away from the neutral axis (tubes, I-beams) raises I with less mass.
  • DOE reports that a 10% reduction in vehicle weight can improve fuel economy by 6–8%, and that lightweight materials can reduce component weight by 10–60% in the short term.
  • EPA reports that every gallon of gasoline burned creates about 8,887 g of CO₂ (about 2.35 kg per liter).
  • Design for disassembly: screws over glue, single materials where possible, and labeled plastics.
  • Lightest is not always greenest: compare materials over the life cycle, including production energy and recyclability.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 9.1 Compare a solid 20 × 20 mm square bar with a 25 × 25 mm square tube with 2 mm walls: area (mass) and I.

Show the steps
  1. Bar: A = 400 mm², I = 20⁴/12 = 13,333 mm⁴.
  2. Tube: A = 625 − 441 = 184 mm², I = (25⁴ − 21⁴)/12 = 16,345 mm⁴.

Answer: The tube uses 54% less material and is 23% stiffer in bending.

Example 9.2 A 1,500 kg car is lightened by 10% (150 kg). It uses 8 L/100 km and drives 15,000 km a year. Using DOE’s 6–8%, estimate the fuel saved per year.

Show the steps
  1. Yearly fuel = 15,000/100 × 8 = 1,200 L.
  2. 6%: 72 L; 8%: 96 L.

Answer: About 72–96 L per year.

Example 9.3 Convert that to CO₂ with EPA’s 8,887 g per gallon.

Show the steps
  1. 8.887 kg / 3.785 L = 2.35 kg/L.
  2. 72 × 2.35 = 169; 96 × 2.35 = 225.

Answer: ≈ 170–225 kg of CO₂ per car per year (a simplified estimate).

Practice problems

    10 × 30³/12 = 22,500 mm⁴.

  2. Same bar turned flat (h = 10, b = 30)?

    Show answer

    30 × 10³/12 = 2,500 mm⁴: 9 times less stiff.

  3. How many liters of gasoline make 1 tonne of CO₂?

    Show answer

    1,000/2.35 ≈ 426 L.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Lighter always means greener.

    Actually: Some light materials need much more energy to produce; judge over the life cycle.

  • Myth: Stiffness depends only on the amount of material.

    Actually: Where the material sits (I) matters more.

  • Myth: Glue makes products better.

    Actually: It often blocks repair and recycling.

Glossary

Auxiliary view
View projected parallel to an inclined surface.
Bill of materials
List of parts and quantities in an assembly.
Clearance fit
Fit that always leaves a gap.
Datum
Exact reference feature for measurement.
Design for assembly
Designing parts to be easy to put together.
Design intent
How a model should behave when changed.
Feature control frame
GD&T callout box.
GD&T
Geometric dimensioning and tolerancing.
Infill
Internal fill percentage of a 3D print.
Interference fit
Fit with guaranteed overlap.
Isometric
Pictorial view with axes 120° apart.
Lightweighting
Reducing mass while keeping function.
MMC
Maximum material condition.
Orthographic projection
Multiview drawing with perpendicular projectors.
Parametric model
CAD model driven by editable dimensions and features.
RSS stack-up
Statistical tolerance sum √Σt².
Second moment of area
Geometric property I that sets bending stiffness.
Section view
View of an imaginary cut.
Third-angle projection
U.S. convention: top view above front.
Title block
Drawing information area.
