About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a senior elective on aviation and the environment. It draws on the environmental-factors lecture in MIT 16.885J Aircraft Systems Engineering (free on MIT OpenCourseWare) and on public NASA, DOE, EIA and EPA material, which we link where it helps. None of them endorses this page.

What we add: every unit ties to real work with a cited public source, every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab runs in a free spreadsheet or simulation.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

This whole course is a planet section: DOE’s Alternative Fuels Data Center says aviation produces about 2% of all CO₂ worldwide and 12% of CO₂ from transportation, and contrails add warming beyond CO₂.

NASA’s Sustainable Flight National Partnership works toward the aviation community’s goal of net-zero carbon emissions by 2050. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample or textbook values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Propulsion: thrust, efficiency and TSFC.
  • Aircraft Performance: lift, drag and the Breguet range equation.
  • Thermodynamics: energy, heating values and cycle efficiency.
  • Capstone Design or Avionics & Systems Engineering: requirements and trades.

How to study

  • Keep units straight: kg, gallons, liters, MJ and Wh.
  • Always ask “per what?”: per flight, per passenger-km, per kg of fuel.
  • Separate combustion CO₂ from lifecycle CO₂.
  • Check every claimed saving against a baseline.
  • Remember non-CO₂ effects such as contrails.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer, and retake the quizzes a few days later.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • Spreadsheet software (any free one works), or Python.
  • A computer or tablet for the free PhET Greenhouse Effect simulation.
  • The free MIT 16.885J lecture notes in a web browser.
  • A calculator.

Unit 1 · Week 1

Aviation and Climate: Counting CO₂

Where aviation emissions come from, emission factors, combustion versus lifecycle CO₂, and aviation’s share of the total.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use an emission factor to compute CO₂.
  • Convert between gallons, liters and kilograms.
  • Explain combustion versus lifecycle CO₂.
  • State aviation’s share of global CO₂.

Key concepts

  • Burning jet fuel turns its carbon into CO₂; the mass of CO₂ is about three times the fuel mass because each carbon atom gains two oxygen atoms.
  • The U.S. EIA gives 21.50 lb (9.75 kg) of CO₂ per gallon of jet fuel burned.
  • Lifecycle CO₂ adds production and transport of the fuel; for sustainable fuels, growing the feedstock can absorb CO₂.
  • DOE’s Alternative Fuels Data Center says aviation accounts for 2% of all CO₂ and 12% of transportation CO₂ worldwide.
  • Aviation also warms the climate through non-CO₂ effects such as contrails and NOx.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 A flight burns 5000 gallons of jet fuel. CO₂?

Show the steps
  1. 5000 × 9.75.

Answer: 48,750 kg (48.75 t).

Example 1.2 Check EIA’s pound and kilogram values.

Show the steps
  1. 21.50 × 0.45359237.

Answer: 9.75 kg, consistent.

Example 1.3 How many liters is 5000 gallons?

Show the steps
  1. 5000 × 3.785.

Answer: 18,927 L.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

    Show answer

  2. Mass ratio of CO₂ to carbon?

    Show answer

  3. Aviation’s share of transport CO₂ (DOE)?

    Show answer

    12%.

  4. What does lifecycle add?

    Show answer

    Production, transport and feedstock effects.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: CO₂ weighs less than the fuel.

    Actually: It weighs about three times more.

  • Myth: Aviation only emits CO₂.

    Actually: Contrails and NOx matter too.

  • Myth: Lifecycle and combustion CO₂ are equal.

    Actually: They differ, especially for SAF.

    Well-to-wake total.

  • Non-CO₂ effects

    Contrails, NOx, soot.

  • Jet A

    Standard jet fuel.

  • t CO₂

    Tonne of CO₂.

Unit 2 · Week 2

Efficiency Metrics

Fuel and CO₂ per passenger-kilometer, energy intensity, and how to compare aircraft fairly.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute fuel per passenger-km.
  • Compute CO₂ per passenger-km.
  • Apply a percentage efficiency gain.
  • Compare fairly across aircraft.

Key concepts

  • Total emissions mislead; normalize by passengers and distance.
  • Load factor matters: empty seats raise emissions per passenger.
  • Energy intensity (MJ per passenger-km) compares different fuels on one scale.
  • An efficiency gain lowers fuel per unit of transport, not necessarily total fuel if traffic grows.
  • NASA’s Sustainable Flight National Partnership targets 25–30% energy-efficiency improvements in next-generation transports.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 5000 gallons carry 180 passengers 2000 km (sample values). Liters per passenger-km?

Show the steps
  1. 18,927/(180 × 2000).

Answer: 0.0526 L.

Example 2.2 CO₂ per passenger-km?

Show the steps
  1. 48,750/360,000.

Answer: 0.135 kg (135 g).

Example 2.3 A 25% efficiency gain on the same trip. New CO₂?

Show the steps
  1. 48,750 × 0.75.

Answer: 36,563 kg.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Same flight with 150 passengers: g CO₂ per pkm?

    Show answer

    162.5 g.

  2. Load factor, 180 of 200 seats?

    Show answer

    0.9.

  3. A 30% gain on 48,750 kg?

    Show answer

    34,125 kg.

  4. Why normalize?

    Show answer

    To compare different trips and aircraft fairly.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Bigger planes always emit more per passenger.

    Actually: Often less per passenger.

  • Myth: Efficiency cuts total emissions.

    Actually: Only if traffic doesn’t grow faster.

  • Myth: Seats equal passengers.

    Actually: Use load factor.

    One passenger carried one km.

  • Load factor

    Passengers over seats.

  • Energy intensity

    MJ per pkm.

  • Rebound

    Growth offsetting efficiency.

  • Baseline

    Reference case.

  • Normalization

    Per-unit comparison.

Unit 3 · Weeks 3–4

Aerodynamic Efficiency

L/D, induced drag and aspect ratio, high-aspect-ratio and truss-braced wings, and fuel burn from the Breguet equation.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute fuel fraction from Breguet.
  • Show how L/D cuts fuel.
  • Compute induced drag coefficient.
  • Explain why long, slender wings save fuel.

Key concepts

  • From Breguet, the fuel fraction is 1 − exp(−Rc/(V·L/D)).
  • Higher L/D means less fuel for the same range.
  • Induced drag C_Di = C_L²/(πeAR) falls as aspect ratio rises.
  • Very slender wings need bracing (truss-braced designs) to stay light and stiff.
  • NASA’s partnership centerpiece is a full-scale X-plane demonstrator testing an ultra-efficient aerodynamic design.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 3000 km at 230 m/s, c = 0.6 /h, L/D = 17 (textbook values). Fuel fraction?

Show the steps
  1. Rc/(V L/D) = 3 × 10⁶ × 1.667 × 10⁻⁴/(230 × 17) = 0.1279.
  2. 1 − e^(−0.1279).

Answer: 0.120.

Example 3.2 Same with L/D = 20.

Show the steps
  1. 1 − e^(−0.1087).

Answer: 0.103: 14.2% less fuel.

Example 3.3 C_Di at C_L = 0.5, e = 0.85, AR = 9?

Show the steps
  1. 0.25/(π × 0.85 × 9).

Answer: 0.0104.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. C_Di at AR = 12?

    Show answer

    0.0078.

  2. At AR = 19 (truss-braced class)?

    Show answer

    0.0049.

  3. Aspect ratio for b = 36 m, S = 120 m²?

    Show answer

    10.8.

  4. Why brace a slender wing?

    Show answer

    To keep it light and stiff.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Drag is all friction.

    Actually: Induced drag is large at cruise.

  • Myth: Longer wings are free.

    Actually: Weight and gate limits.

  • Myth: Fuel scales linearly with L/D.

    Actually: It follows the exponential.

Flashcards

  • L/D

    Lift-to-drag ratio.

  • Induced drag

    Drag due to lift.

  • Aspect ratio

    b²/S.

  • Oswald factor

    Span efficiency e.

  • Truss-braced wing

    Slender wing with struts.

  • Fuel fraction

    Fuel over takeoff weight.

Unit 4 · Week 5

Engine Efficiency

Thermal, propulsive and overall efficiency, bypass ratio, and advanced engine cores.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute propulsive efficiency.
  • Combine thermal and propulsive efficiency.
  • Explain why high bypass saves fuel.
  • Relate efficiency to TSFC.

Key concepts

  • Overall efficiency is thermal efficiency times propulsive efficiency.
  • Propulsive efficiency 2/(1 + V_e/V₀) rises as exhaust velocity approaches flight speed.
  • High-bypass turbofans move more air more slowly, raising propulsive efficiency.
  • Hotter, higher-pressure, compact cores raise thermal efficiency.
  • NASA’s Hybrid Thermally Efficient Core work develops high-power-density turbine cores for next-generation transports.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 V₀ = 230 m/s, V_e = 400 m/s. η_p?

Show the steps
  1. 2/(1 + 400/230).

Answer: 0.730.

Example 4.2 Lower V_e to 300 m/s (higher bypass).

Show the steps
  1. 2/(1 + 300/230).

Answer: 0.868.

Example 4.3 With η_th = 0.5, overall efficiency at V_e = 300?

Show the steps
  1. 0.5 × 0.868.

Answer: 0.434.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. η_p at V_e = 350 m/s?

    Show answer

    0.793.

  2. Overall at V_e = 400, η_th = 0.5?

    Show answer

    0.365.

  3. Fuel saving from 0.365 to 0.434 overall?

    Show answer

    15.9% less fuel for the same work.

  4. What is bypass ratio?

    Show answer

    Bypass over core mass flow.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Faster exhaust is more efficient.

    Actually: It lowers propulsive efficiency.

  • Myth: Thermal efficiency is everything.

    Actually: Propulsive matters as much.

  • Myth: Bypass is free.

    Actually: Bigger fans add weight and drag.

Flashcards

  • Propulsive efficiency

    Useful thrust power fraction.

  • Thermal efficiency

    Cycle efficiency.

  • Overall efficiency

    Product of both.

  • Bypass ratio

    Fan air over core air.

  • TSFC

    Fuel per thrust per time.

  • Engine core

    Compressor, burner, turbine.

Unit 5 · Weeks 6–7

Sustainable Aviation Fuels

What SAF is, production pathways, blend limits, lifecycle reductions and national goals.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Define SAF.
  • Compute blended lifecycle savings.
  • Size goal-level savings.
  • Explain blend limits.

Key concepts

  • DOE defines SAF as an alternative fuel made from non-petroleum feedstocks that reduces air pollution from air transportation.
  • SAF can be blended at levels between 10% and 50%, depending on feedstock and production pathway (ASTM D7566).
  • Blended SAF works in existing aircraft and infrastructure.
  • Lifecycle savings scale with blend fraction times the SAF’s own lifecycle reduction.
  • The SAF Grand Challenge aims for 3 billion gallons in 2030 and 35 billion in 2050 with at least 50% lifecycle reduction.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 A 50% blend of SAF with a 50% lifecycle reduction. Overall saving?

Show the steps
  1. 0.5 × 0.5.

Answer: 25%.

Example 5.2 3 billion gallons with a 50% reduction, using the EIA factor as the baseline (simplified).

Show the steps
  1. 3 × 10⁹ × 9.75 × 0.5.

Answer: About 14.6 million tonnes CO₂ a year.

Example 5.3 Same for 35 billion gallons.

Show the steps
  1. 35 × 10⁹ × 9.75 × 0.5.

Answer: About 171 million tonnes.

  3. Why the blend limit?

  4. Does SAF need new engines?

    Actually: It burns to CO₂; savings are lifecycle.

  • Myth: Any blend works.

    Actually: Limits depend on pathway.

  • Myth: SAF only affects CO₂.

    Actually: NASA-DLR found fewer contrail ice crystals.

Unit 6 · Week 8

Contrails and Non-CO₂ Effects

How contrails form, why they warm, soot and ice crystals, and how fuels and routing reduce them.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Explain contrail formation.
  • Compute ice-crystal reductions.
  • Describe non-CO₂ warming.
  • Name mitigation options.

Key concepts

  • Contrails form when hot, humid exhaust mixes with cold air and water condenses and freezes on soot particles.
  • Persistent contrails can spread into cirrus that trap outgoing heat.
  • Fewer soot particles mean fewer, larger ice crystals that disappear sooner.
  • NASA and DLR found cleaner-burning sustainable fuels produced 50%–70% fewer ice crystals at cruise.
  • Routing around ice-supersaturated air can avoid persistent contrails.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 6.1 A sample emission index of 10¹⁵ ice crystals per kg of fuel. With a 50% reduction?

Show the steps
  1. 10¹⁵ × 0.5.

Answer: 5 × 10¹⁴ per kg.

Example 6.2 With a 70% reduction?

Show the steps
  1. 10¹⁵ × 0.3.

Answer: 3 × 10¹⁴ per kg.

Example 6.3 A flight burns 15,000 kg of fuel at cruise (sample). Crystals at the 70% reduction?

Show the steps
  1. 3 × 10¹⁴ × 15,000.

Answer: 4.5 × 10¹⁸.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Why do contrails warm at night?

    Show answer

  2. What seeds contrail ice?

    Show answer

  3. Reduction from 5 × 10¹⁴ to 3 × 10¹⁴?

    Show answer

  4. One operational fix?

    Show answer

    Route around ice-supersaturated air.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Contrails are just water, so harmless.

    Actually: They can add warming.

  • Myth: All contrails persist.

    Actually: Only in ice-supersaturated air.

  • Myth: Only CO₂ matters.

    Actually: Non-CO₂ effects are significant.

    Air able to grow ice.

    Carbon particles.

  • Emission index

    Amount per kg of fuel.

  • Non-CO₂ forcing

    Warming besides CO₂.

Unit 7 · Weeks 9–10

Electric and Hybrid Propulsion

Battery energy density, drivetrain efficiency, electric range, and where hybrid systems help.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compare battery and fuel energy density.
  • Account for drivetrain efficiency.
  • Compute electric aircraft range.
  • Identify suitable missions.

Key concepts

  • Jet fuel stores about 43 MJ/kg (11,944 Wh/kg); today’s batteries store a few hundred Wh/kg (textbook values).
  • Electric drivetrains convert energy far more efficiently (about 90%) than turbines (about 35%), shrinking the gap (textbook values).
  • Battery-electric range is R = (e η/g)(L/D)(m_b/m).
  • Batteries don’t get lighter as they discharge, unlike fuel.
  • NASA says electrified aircraft propulsion offers new possibilities for reducing fuel and energy use, from small passenger aircraft to single-aisle transports.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Unit 8 · Week 11

Hydrogen Aircraft

Hydrogen’s energy per mass and volume, tank sizing, water emissions, and design trade-offs.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compare hydrogen and jet fuel by mass and volume.
  • Size a hydrogen tank volume.
  • Compute water produced.
  • Explain design trade-offs.

Key concepts

  • Hydrogen holds about 120 MJ/kg, 2.8 times jet fuel, but liquid hydrogen is only about 70.8 kg/m³ (textbook values).
  • Per liter, liquid hydrogen holds about 8.5 MJ versus about 34.4 MJ for jet fuel, so tanks are about four times larger.
  • Burning or using hydrogen in a fuel cell makes water, about 8.9 kg per kg of hydrogen, and no CO₂ at the aircraft.
  • Cryogenic tanks are bulky and heavy, shaping the whole aircraft.
  • Green hydrogen depends on clean electricity for electrolysis.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Unit 9 · Weeks 12–13

Operations, Noise and Air Quality

Efficient taxi and trajectories, decibel arithmetic, airport noise, local air quality and leaded aviation gasoline.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Estimate taxi fuel savings.
  • Add sound levels in decibels.
  • Apply the inverse-square law.
  • Explain the leaded-fuel issue.

Key concepts

  • Operations matter: single-engine taxi, continuous descents and optimal trajectories cut fuel with today’s aircraft.
  • Decibels add logarithmically: two equal sources are 3 dB louder, not twice the number.
  • In open air, sound level drops about 6 dB per doubling of distance.
  • Engine NOx, particles and CO affect air quality near airports.
  • EPA says most aircraft using leaded fuel are piston-engine aircraft carrying 2–10 passengers; commercial jets don’t use leaded fuel.

Unit 10 · Weeks 14–15

Pathways to Net Zero

Traffic growth, efficiency, SAF, new aircraft and offsets combined into scenarios toward 2050.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Project traffic growth.
  • Combine reductions multiplicatively.
  • Build a simple wedge scenario.
  • Discuss policy tools.

Key concepts

  • Emissions = traffic × energy per traffic unit × carbon per energy.
  • Growth compounds: 3% a year doubles traffic in about 24 years (sample rate).
  • Reductions multiply: (1 − efficiency gain) × (1 − SAF lifecycle saving).
  • Remaining emissions need new aircraft, more SAF, or removals.
  • DOE notes ICAO’s CORSIA caps net CO₂ from international aviation at 2020 levels through 2035.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step
