About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows the first semester of calculus-based college physics (classical mechanics), the course every engineering and physics major takes first. It is modeled on the topics and depth of open materials such as OpenStax University Physics Volume 1 and MIT OpenCourseWare 8.01SC Classical Mechanics, which we link to throughout. Those organizations do not endorse this page.

What we add: every unit is tied to real aerospace and Earth-observation work, every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab has a home or virtual version so anyone can do it.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Mechanics is the physics of launching, flying and orbiting. The same equations put Earth-observing satellites such as Landsat 9 and GRACE-FO into orbit, keep them pointed, and turn tiny changes in their motion into maps of water, ice and land.

On the ground, the same ideas size wind turbines, make cars and aircraft use less energy, and help engineers plan how to move an asteroid. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section showing how its ideas help both people and nature.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Algebra and trigonometry: solving equations, sine, cosine and tangent, the Pythagorean theorem.
  • Calculus I, at least at the same time: derivatives as rates of change and integrals as areas. This course uses dv/dt and ∫v dt from Unit 1.
  • Vectors in two dimensions: components, magnitude and direction.
  • SI units and scientific notation.

How to study

  • Read the unit, then close it and try the worked examples yourself before checking each step.
  • Draw a picture for every problem. For any force problem, draw a free-body diagram before writing an equation.
  • Solve with symbols first and put numbers in at the end. Then check the units and whether the size of the answer makes sense.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer. Getting it wrong first and fixing it is how the method sticks.
  • Use the PhET simulations to test your intuition: predict what will happen, then run it.
  • Retake the quizzes a few days later. Spaced practice beats one long session.
  • Use g = 9.81 m/s² unless a problem says otherwise, and keep three significant figures.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • Meter stick and tape measure; vernier caliper if available.
  • Stopwatch (a phone works) and, ideally, a phone app or school sensor that records motion or acceleration.
  • Low-friction cart and track (or a smooth board), a pulley and a set of hanging masses.
  • A small ball, a ramp, carbon or plain paper for marking landing spots.
  • String, a dense bob (a fishing weight works) and a protractor.
  • Spreadsheet software for averages, standard deviations and graphs.

Unit 1 · Weeks 1–2

Measurement, Vectors & Motion in One Dimension

How physicists measure, how vectors describe direction, and how position, velocity and acceleration are linked by calculus.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use SI units, significant figures and a stated uncertainty for every measured value.
  • Add vectors by components and find a magnitude and direction.
  • Define velocity as v = dx/dt and acceleration as a = dv/dt, and read them from graphs.
  • Solve constant-acceleration problems, including free fall, with the four kinematic equations.

Key concepts

  • Every measurement has an uncertainty. Report a value as best estimate ± uncertainty, with units, and round the uncertainty to one or two significant figures.
  • A vector has a size and a direction. Add vectors by adding their x and y components, never their magnitudes.
  • Velocity is the slope of a position–time graph; acceleration is the slope of a velocity–time graph. The area under a velocity–time graph is the displacement.
  • With constant acceleration, the four kinematic equations follow from integrating a = constant twice.
  • Near Earth’s surface, every object in free fall (no air resistance) accelerates downward at g ≈ 9.81 m/s², whatever its mass.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 A car starts from rest and speeds up at a steady 3.0 m/s² for 8.0 s. How fast is it going, and how far has it traveled?

Show the steps
  1. Known: v₀ = 0, a = 3.0 m/s², t = 8.0 s.
  2. v = v₀ + at = 0 + 3.0 × 8.0 = 24 m/s.
  3. x = v₀t + ½at² = 0 + ½ × 3.0 × 8.0² = 96 m.

Answer: 24 m/s (86 km/h) after 96 m.

Example 1.2 A ball is thrown straight up at 15 m/s. How long does it take to reach the top, and how high does it go?

Show the steps
  1. At the top, v = 0. Take up as positive, so a = −9.81 m/s².
  2. Time: 0 = 15 − 9.81t, so t = 15 ÷ 9.81 = 1.53 s.
  3. Height: 0 = 15² − 2 × 9.81 × h, so h = 225 ÷ 19.62 = 11.5 m.

Answer: 1.53 s to the top, 11.5 m above the release point.

Example 1.3 A hiker walks 3.0 km east and then 4.0 km north. What is her displacement?

Show the steps
  1. Components: x = 3.0 km, y = 4.0 km.
  2. Magnitude: √(3.0² + 4.0²) = √25 = 5.0 km.
  3. Direction: tan⁻¹(4.0/3.0) = 53.1° north of east.

Answer: 5.0 km at 53.1° north of east. She walked 7.0 km, but her displacement is 5.0 km.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. A car moving at 27 m/s brakes at a steady 6.0 m/s². How far does it travel before stopping, and how long does it take?

    Show answer

    Distance: 0 = 27² − 2 × 6.0 × d, so d = 729 ÷ 12 = 60.8 m. Time: t = 27 ÷ 6.0 = 4.5 s.

  2. A stone is dropped from rest off a 20 m bridge. Ignoring air resistance, how long until it hits the water and how fast is it going?

    Show answer

    t = √(2h/g) = √(40 ÷ 9.81) = 2.02 s. v = gt = 9.81 × 2.02 = 19.8 m/s.

  3. The position of a drone is x(t) = 2.0t³ − 3.0t (meters, seconds). Find its velocity and acceleration at t = 2.0 s.

    Show answer

    v = dx/dt = 6.0t² − 3.0 = 6.0 × 4 − 3.0 = 21 m/s. a = dv/dt = 12t = 24 m/s².

  4. A low Earth orbit speed is about 7.8 km/s. Convert this to km/h.

    Show answer

    7.8 km/s × 3,600 s/h = 28,080 km/h, about 28,000 km/h.

  5. A solar panel measures 2.50 ± 0.02 m by 1.20 ± 0.01 m. Find its area and the uncertainty.

    Show answer

    A = 2.50 × 1.20 = 3.00 m². Relative uncertainties: 0.02/2.50 = 0.8% and 0.01/1.20 = 0.83%. Combine in quadrature: √(0.8² + 0.83²) = 1.16%. ΔA = 0.0116 × 3.00 = 0.035 m². A = 3.00 ± 0.03 m².

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: If the velocity is zero, the acceleration must be zero too.

    Actually: At the top of its flight a thrown ball has v = 0 but a = −9.81 m/s². Its velocity is still changing.

  • Myth: Heavier objects fall faster.

    Actually: Without air resistance all objects fall with the same acceleration g. Air resistance, not weight, makes a feather fall slowly.

  • Myth: Distance and displacement are the same thing.

    Actually: Displacement is a vector from start to finish. Walking around a running track once gives a distance of 400 m and a displacement of zero.

Flashcards

  • Displacement

    Change in position, a vector: Δx = x_final − x_initial.

  • Velocity

    Rate of change of position, v = dx/dt (m/s).

  • Acceleration

    Rate of change of velocity, a = dv/dt (m/s²).

  • Free fall

    Motion under gravity alone; a = g ≈ 9.81 m/s² downward.

  • Uncertainty

    The range within which the true value probably lies, reported as ± with units.

  • Vector components

    A_x = A cos θ, A_y = A sin θ.

Show answer

3. Vectors of 3 N east and 4 N north are added. The resultant is…

Show answer

How this helps save the planet

Earth-observing satellites are only useful if we know exactly where they were when each picture was taken. Their positions come from integrating velocity and acceleration over time, the same calculus as this unit, checked against GPS.

Landsat 9 orbits 705 km above Earth. Knowing its position to within meters lets scientists compare images taken years apart and measure forest loss, glacier retreat and crop health, which helps people manage land and protects habitats.

Uncertainty matters just as much: a measured change only counts if it is bigger than the uncertainty in the measurement.

Unit 2 · Week 3

Motion in Two Dimensions: Projectiles & Circles

Split motion into independent horizontal and vertical parts, then add circular motion, the key to every orbit.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Treat horizontal and vertical motion of a projectile separately and find time of flight, range and maximum height.
  • Find relative velocity by vector addition.
  • Calculate centripetal acceleration and explain why it points to the center.

Key concepts

  • Without air resistance, a projectile has constant horizontal velocity and constant downward acceleration g. The two directions are independent and share only time.
  • On level ground the range is R = v₀² sin 2θ / g, largest at 45°.
  • An object moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating, because its direction changes. The acceleration a = v²/r points to the center.
  • Velocities add as vectors: the velocity of a boat relative to the shore is its velocity relative to the water plus the water’s velocity.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 A ball is launched at 20 m/s, 30° above level ground. Find the time of flight, range and maximum height.

Show the steps
  1. Components: vₓ = 20 cos 30° = 17.3 m/s; v_y = 20 sin 30° = 10.0 m/s.
  2. Time of flight: t = 2v_y/g = 20.0 ÷ 9.81 = 2.04 s.
  3. Range: R = vₓt = 17.3 × 2.04 = 35.3 m.
  4. Height: H = v_y²/2g = 100 ÷ 19.62 = 5.10 m.

Answer: 2.04 s, 35.3 m, 5.10 m.

Example 2.2 A package is thrown horizontally at 12 m/s from a 45 m cliff. Where does it land?

Show the steps
  1. Vertical: 45 = ½ × 9.81 × t², so t = √(90 ÷ 9.81) = 3.03 s.
  2. Horizontal: x = 12 × 3.03 = 36.3 m.

Answer: 36.3 m from the base of the cliff, after 3.03 s.

Example 2.3 A satellite circles Earth at 420 km altitude at 7.66 km/s. What is its centripetal acceleration? (Earth radius 6,371 km.)

Show the steps
  1. Orbit radius r = 6,371 + 420 = 6,791 km = 6.791 × 10⁶ m.
  2. a = v²/r = (7,660)² ÷ 6.791 × 10⁶ = 8.64 m/s².

Answer: 8.64 m/s², about 88% of g at the surface. That acceleration is gravity itself: the satellite is in free fall around Earth.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. At what angle does a projectile go farthest on level ground, and how far does it go if launched at 20 m/s?

    Show answer

    45°. R = v₀²/g = 400 ÷ 9.81 = 40.8 m.

  2. A boat heads straight across a river at 4.0 m/s relative to the water. The current flows at 3.0 m/s. Find the boat’s speed relative to the shore and its direction.

    Show answer

    √(4.0² + 3.0²) = 5.0 m/s, at tan⁻¹(3.0/4.0) = 36.9° downstream of straight across.

  3. A car rounds a 50 m radius curve at 15 m/s. What is its centripetal acceleration?

    Show answer

    a = 15² ÷ 50 = 4.5 m/s².

  4. How fast does a point on the equator move because of Earth’s rotation? (Equatorial radius 6,378 km, one turn in 86,164 s.)

    Show answer

    v = 2πr/T = 2π × 6.378 × 10⁶ ÷ 86,164 = 465 m/s. Rockets launched eastward near the equator get this speed for free.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: A bullet fired horizontally hits the ground later than one dropped from the same height.

    Actually: Vertical motion does not depend on horizontal motion. Both hit at the same time (ignoring air resistance and Earth’s curvature).

  • Myth: Something moving in a circle at constant speed is not accelerating.

    Actually: Its velocity changes direction, so it accelerates toward the center at v²/r.

  • Myth: At the top of a projectile’s path its velocity is zero.

    Actually: Only the vertical part is zero. The horizontal part is unchanged.

Flashcards

  • Projectile

    An object moving under gravity alone after launch.

  • Range

    Horizontal distance on level ground: R = v₀² sin 2θ / g.

  • Centripetal acceleration

    a = v²/r toward the center of a circle.

  • Angular speed ω

    Rate of turning in rad/s; v = ωr.

  • Relative velocity

    v_A rel ground = v_A rel B + v_B rel ground.

  • Period T

    Time for one full circle: T = 2πr/v.

Self-check quiz

1. For a projectile without air resistance, the horizontal velocity…

Show answer

stays constant. No horizontal force means no horizontal acceleration.

No horizontal force means no horizontal acceleration.

2. Two launch angles give the same range on level ground. Which pair?

Show answer

30° and 60°. sin 2θ is the same for complementary angles: sin 60° = sin 120°.

sin 2θ is the same for complementary angles: sin 60° = sin 120°.

3. If speed in a circle doubles at the same radius, centripetal acceleration…

Show answer

quadruples. a = v²/r, so 2² = 4 times.

a = v²/r, so 2² = 4 times.

4. The direction of centripetal acceleration is…

Show answer

toward the center. It always points to the center of the circle.

It always points to the center of the circle.

How this helps save the planet

An orbit is a projectile that never lands: fired fast enough sideways, it falls toward Earth at the same rate Earth’s surface curves away. That is why the satellite in Example 2.3 accelerates at v²/r while going nowhere closer.

Launch sites take advantage of Earth’s rotation. Firing eastward from near the equator adds up to about 465 m/s, which saves fuel on every launch of a weather or climate satellite.

Projectile thinking also protects people on the ground: the same equations, plus drag, are used to predict where a reentering stage or piece of debris could land.

Unit 3 · Weeks 4–5

Newton’s Laws, Friction & Circular Dynamics

Forces cause acceleration. Free-body diagrams and ΣF = ma solve almost every problem in the course.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • State Newton’s three laws and identify action–reaction pairs.
  • Draw a free-body diagram and apply ΣF = ma along chosen axes.
  • Solve incline, pulley and connected-body problems with static and kinetic friction.
  • Apply Newton’s second law to circular motion.

Key concepts

  • First law: an object keeps its velocity unless a net force acts on it.
  • Second law: ΣF = ma. Net force, not any single force, sets the acceleration.
  • Third law: forces come in pairs, equal in size and opposite in direction, acting on different objects.
  • Static friction can be anything up to μₛN; kinetic friction is μₖN and opposes sliding.
  • In circular motion, the net inward force is m v²/r. It is supplied by real forces such as tension, friction, normal force or gravity.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 A 1,500 kg car goes from 0 to 25 m/s in 10 s. What net force acts on it?

Show the steps
  1. a = Δv/Δt = 25 ÷ 10 = 2.5 m/s².
  2. ΣF = ma = 1,500 × 2.5 = 3,750 N.

Answer: 3,750 N forward.

Example 3.2 A 5.0 kg box slides down a 30° ramp with μₖ = 0.20. Find its acceleration.

Show the steps
  1. Axes along and perpendicular to the ramp.
  2. Perpendicular: N = mg cos 30°.
  3. Along: ma = mg sin 30° − μₖ mg cos 30°.
  4. a = 9.81 × (0.500 − 0.20 × 0.866) = 3.21 m/s². The mass cancels.

Answer: 3.21 m/s² down the ramp.

Example 3.3 An Atwood machine hangs 3.0 kg and 2.0 kg over a light, frictionless pulley. Find the acceleration and the tension.

Show the steps
  1. Heavier side moves down. For 3.0 kg: 3.0g − T = 3.0a. For 2.0 kg: T − 2.0g = 2.0a.
  2. Add: 1.0g = 5.0a, so a = 9.81 ÷ 5 = 1.96 m/s².
  3. T = 2.0 × (9.81 + 1.96) = 23.5 N.

Answer: a = 1.96 m/s², T = 23.5 N.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. A 70 kg person stands on a scale in an elevator that accelerates upward at 1.5 m/s². What does the scale read?

    Show answer

    N − mg = ma, so N = 70 × (9.81 + 1.5) = 792 N (it reads as if they had a mass of 80.7 kg).

  2. A 40 kg crate sits on a floor with μₛ = 0.50. What horizontal push just starts it moving?

    Show answer

    F = μₛ mg = 0.50 × 40 × 9.81 = 196 N.

  3. A rocket of mass 5.0 × 10⁵ kg has 7.6 × 10⁶ N of thrust at liftoff. Find its initial acceleration.

    Show answer

    a = (T − mg)/m = 7.6 × 10⁶ ÷ 5.0 × 10⁵ − 9.81 = 15.2 − 9.81 = 5.39 m/s² upward. Its thrust-to-weight ratio is 1.55.

  4. A 10 N push acts on a 2.0 kg block that pushes a 3.0 kg block on a frictionless floor. Find the acceleration and the force between the blocks.

    Show answer

    a = 10 ÷ 5.0 = 2.0 m/s². The force on the 3.0 kg block is 3.0 × 2.0 = 6.0 N.

  5. What is the top speed for a car on a flat 40 m radius curve if μₛ = 0.60?

    Show answer

    Friction gives the inward force: μₛ mg = mv²/r, so v = √(μₛ g r) = √(0.60 × 9.81 × 40) = 15.3 m/s.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: A moving object needs a force to keep it moving.

    Actually: Only to overcome friction or drag. In space, a probe coasts for years with its engines off.

  • Myth: Action and reaction cancel, so nothing can accelerate.

    Actually: They act on different objects, so they never cancel on one object.

  • Myth: The normal force always equals mg.

    Actually: Only on a level surface with no vertical acceleration. On a ramp it is mg cos θ; in an accelerating elevator it changes.

Flashcards

  • Newton’s first law

    Velocity stays constant unless a net force acts.

  • Newton’s second law

    ΣF = ma.

  • Newton’s third law

    Forces come in equal, opposite pairs on different objects.

  • Free-body diagram

    Sketch of one object with every force acting on it.

  • Coefficient of friction μ

    Ratio of friction force to normal force; no units.

  • Newton (N)

    1 N = 1 kg·m/s².

How this helps save the planet

Newton’s third law is how every rocket works: it pushes exhaust backward and the exhaust pushes it forward. Those launches carry the satellites that monitor weather, wildfires, sea level and greenhouse gases.

Friction and drag are where much of our energy goes. Lower-friction tires and bearings, and more aerodynamic vehicles, need less force at the same speed and so less fuel or battery energy.

Thrust-to-weight is the first number engineers check on any launch vehicle: below 1, it cannot lift off.

Unit 4 · Weeks 6–7

Work, Energy & Power

Energy is the bookkeeping that makes hard force problems easy, and the language of every power plant, battery and engine.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute work as W = ∫F·dx, including for a spring.
  • Use the work–energy theorem and conservation of mechanical energy.
  • Account for energy lost to friction, and compute power and efficiency.
  • Estimate the power in wind with P = ½ρAv³.

Key concepts

  • Work is force times displacement along the force: W = Fd cos θ for a constant force.
  • The net work on an object equals its change in kinetic energy.
  • Gravity and springs are conservative forces: their work can be stored as potential energy and given back.
  • Friction turns mechanical energy into thermal energy; total energy is still conserved.
  • Power is the rate of doing work, in watts (J/s).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 A 1,200 kg car moving at 20 m/s brakes with a steady 6,000 N friction force. How far does it travel?

Show the steps
  1. K = ½ × 1,200 × 20² = 240,000 J.
  2. Friction work removes it all: 6,000 × d = 240,000.
  3. d = 40 m.

Answer: 40 m.

Example 4.2 A roller coaster starts from rest 30 m above the bottom of a dip. Ignoring friction, how fast is it at the bottom?

Show the steps
  1. mgh = ½mv², so the mass cancels.
  2. v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.81 × 30) = 24.3 m/s.

Answer: 24.3 m/s, whatever the path shape.

Example 4.3 A wind turbine has 50 m blades. How much power does a 10 m/s wind carry through its swept area, and what is the most any turbine could extract?

Show the steps
  1. A = πr² = π × 50² = 7,854 m².
  2. P = ½ρAv³ = ½ × 1.225 × 7,854 × 10³ = 4.81 × 10⁶ W.
  3. The Betz limit says no turbine can capture more than 16/27 (59.3%) of this: 0.593 × 4.81 MW = 2.85 MW.

Answer: The wind carries 4.81 MW; at most 2.85 MW could be captured. Real turbines get less.

  3. How much power does it take to lift 70 kg up 3.0 m in 2.0 s?

    Show answer

    P = mgh/t = 70 × 9.81 × 3.0 ÷ 2.0 = 1,030 W.

  4. A 1,500 kg car brakes from 25 m/s to a stop. How much kinetic energy is removed? If regenerative braking stored 60% of it, how much would that be?

    Show answer

    K = ½ × 1,500 × 25² = 468,750 J ≈ 469 kJ. 60% is 281 kJ.

  5. If the wind speed falls from 10 m/s to 7 m/s, by what fraction does the wind power fall?

    Show answer

    Power goes as v³: (7/10)³ = 0.343, so it falls to about 34% of the original.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Carrying a heavy box across a level room at steady speed does a lot of work on it.

    Actually: Gravity and your upward force are perpendicular to the motion, so they do no work on the box.

  • Myth: Energy is lost to friction.

    Actually: It is converted to thermal energy, not destroyed. It is lost only as useful mechanical energy.

  • Myth: A turbine twice as big in diameter gets twice the power.

    Actually: Power scales with area, so doubling the diameter gives four times the power.

How this helps save the planet

Wind turbines turn the kinetic energy of moving air into electricity. Because wind power grows with the cube of wind speed and with the square of blade length, engineers build taller towers and longer blades, which is why offshore turbines are so large.

Electric and hybrid vehicles use regenerative braking: the motor works as a generator and turns some of the car’s kinetic energy back into stored battery energy instead of heat.

Energy accounting is how engineers compare clean-energy options fairly, in joules and watts rather than claims.

Unit 5 · Week 8

Momentum, Collisions & Rockets

Momentum is conserved in every collision and explosion. It explains rockets, and how a spacecraft moved an asteroid.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Relate impulse to change in momentum, J = ∫F dt = Δp.
  • Apply conservation of momentum to elastic and inelastic collisions in one dimension.
  • Find the center of mass of a system.
  • Use the rocket equation Δv = vₑ ln(m₀/m_f).

Key concepts

  • Momentum p = mv is a vector. With no external net force, total momentum of a system stays constant.
  • In an elastic collision kinetic energy is also conserved; in an inelastic one some becomes heat, sound and deformation. In a perfectly inelastic collision the objects stick together.
  • A longer collision time means a smaller average force for the same change in momentum. That is how airbags and crumple zones work.
  • A rocket gains speed by throwing mass backward. Its speed change depends on exhaust speed and on the ratio of starting to final mass, not on how fast the fuel burns.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 A 0.145 kg baseball arrives at 40 m/s and leaves the bat at 50 m/s in the opposite direction. Contact lasts 1.0 ms. Find the impulse and average force.

Show the steps
  1. Take the outgoing direction as positive: Δp = 0.145 × (50 − (−40)) = 0.145 × 90 = 13.1 kg·m/s.
  2. F = Δp/Δt = 13.05 ÷ 0.0010 = 13,000 N.

Answer: Impulse 13.1 kg·m/s; average force about 13,000 N.

Example 5.2 A 1,000 kg car at 20 m/s rear-ends a stopped 1,500 kg car and they lock together. Find their speed and the kinetic energy lost.

Show the steps
  1. Momentum: 1,000 × 20 = 2,500 × v, so v = 8.0 m/s.
  2. K before: ½ × 1,000 × 20² = 200 kJ. K after: ½ × 2,500 × 8.0² = 80 kJ.
  3. Lost: 120 kJ, 60% of the original.

Answer: 8.0 m/s; 120 kJ turned into deformation, heat and sound.

Example 5.3 A rocket stage has an exhaust speed of 3,000 m/s and a mass ratio m₀/m_f = 5. What speed change can it give, ignoring gravity and drag?

Show the steps
  1. Δv = vₑ ln(m₀/m_f) = 3,000 × ln 5.
  2. ln 5 = 1.609, so Δv = 4,830 m/s.

Answer: About 4.83 km/s. Reaching orbit at about 7.8 km/s needs more, which is why rockets use stages.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. A 2.0 kg cart at 3.0 m/s hits a 1.0 kg cart at rest in a perfectly elastic collision. Find both final velocities.

    Show answer

    v₁ = (m₁ − m₂)/(m₁ + m₂) × 3.0 = 1.0 m/s; v₂ = 2m₁/(m₁ + m₂) × 3.0 = 4.0 m/s. Check: K before 9.0 J, after 1.0 + 8.0 = 9.0 J.

  2. A 4.0 kg rifle fires a 0.010 kg bullet at 400 m/s. Find the rifle’s recoil speed.

    Show answer

    0 = 0.010 × 400 − 4.0v, so v = 1.0 m/s backward.

  3. A 2.0 kg mass sits at x = 0 and a 3.0 kg mass at x = 5.0 m. Where is the center of mass?

    Show answer

    x_cm = (2.0 × 0 + 3.0 × 5.0) ÷ 5.0 = 3.0 m.

  4. A stage with exhaust speed 3.5 km/s must provide Δv = 9.0 km/s on its own. What mass ratio is needed?

    Show answer

    m₀/m_f = e^(9.0/3.5) = e^2.571 = 13.1. Only about 7.6% of the starting mass could be anything other than propellant.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: A rocket needs air to push against.

    Actually: It pushes on its own exhaust. Rockets work best in a vacuum.

  • Myth: In an inelastic collision momentum is lost.

    Actually: Momentum is conserved in every collision with no external force. Kinetic energy is what is lost.

  • Myth: A bigger force always means a bigger change in momentum.

    Actually: Impulse is force × time. A small force over a long time can change momentum as much as a big force over a short time.

  • Perfectly inelastic collision

How this helps save the planet

On Sept. 26, 2022, NASA’s DART spacecraft deliberately hit the asteroid moonlet Dimorphos. Its momentum shortened Dimorphos’s 11-hour-55-minute orbit around Didymos by about 33 minutes, far more than the 73-second minimum for success, because debris thrown off the asteroid added extra push.

It was the first test of moving an asteroid by kinetic impact, a way to protect people and every other living thing on Earth from a future impact. Neither asteroid was ever a threat to Earth.

The rocket equation drives cleaner launch design too: higher exhaust speed means less propellant for the same mission.

Unit 6 · Weeks 9–10

Rotation, Torque & Angular Momentum

Everything that spins, from wheels and turbines to reaction wheels that point satellites, follows rotational versions of Newton’s laws.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use angular position, velocity and acceleration, and link them to linear motion with v = ωr.
  • Compute moments of inertia and use τ = Iα.
  • Solve rolling problems with energy conservation.
  • Apply conservation of angular momentum.

Key concepts

  • Rotational quantities mirror linear ones: θ ↔ x, ω ↔ v, α ↔ a, I ↔ m, τ ↔ F, L ↔ p.
  • Moment of inertia depends on how mass is spread from the axis. Mass far from the axis is harder to spin up.
  • Torque is a force times its lever arm: τ = rF sin θ.
  • A rolling object has both translational and rotational kinetic energy, so it rolls down a slope slower than a frictionless block slides.
  • With no external torque, angular momentum L = Iω stays constant.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 6.1 A wind-turbine rotor speeds up from rest to 1.5 rad/s in 30 s at a steady rate. Find α and how many turns it makes.

Show the steps
  1. α = 1.5 ÷ 30 = 0.050 rad/s².
  2. θ = ½αt² = ½ × 0.050 × 30² = 22.5 rad.
  3. Turns: 22.5 ÷ 2π = 3.58.

Answer: α = 0.050 rad/s²; 3.58 turns.

Example 6.2 A solid ball rolls without slipping down a slope from 2.0 m high. How fast is it at the bottom?

Show the steps
  1. For a solid sphere, I = (2/5)mr², and ω = v/r.
  2. mgh = ½mv² + ½(2/5)mr²(v/r)² = (7/10)mv².
  3. v = √(10gh/7) = √(10 × 9.81 × 2.0 ÷ 7) = 5.29 m/s.

Answer: 5.29 m/s, less than the 6.26 m/s of a frictionless sliding block.

Example 6.3 A figure skater spinning at 2.0 rev/s pulls in her arms, cutting her moment of inertia from 4.0 to 1.6 kg·m². Find her new spin rate.

Show the steps
  1. L is conserved: I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂.
  2. ω₂ = 4.0 × 2.0 ÷ 1.6 = 5.0 rev/s.
  3. Her kinetic energy rises by 2.5 times; the extra comes from the work her arms do.

Answer: 5.0 rev/s.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. A 50 N force is applied at right angles to the end of a 0.30 m wrench. Find the torque.

    Show answer

    τ = 0.30 × 50 = 15 N·m.

  2. A child of 30 kg sits 2.0 m from a seesaw pivot. Where must a 40 kg child sit to balance?

    Show answer

    30 × 2.0 = 40 × x, so x = 1.5 m on the other side.

  3. A solid steel flywheel is a 100 kg disk of radius 0.50 m spinning at 300 rad/s. How much energy does it store?

    Show answer

    I = ½ × 100 × 0.50² = 12.5 kg·m². K = ½ × 12.5 × 300² = 562,500 J ≈ 0.563 MJ (0.156 kWh).

  4. A satellite has I = 500 kg·m² about one axis. An onboard reaction wheel with I = 0.10 kg·m² spins up from rest to 600 rad/s. How fast does the satellite turn?

    Show answer

    Total L stays zero: 0.10 × 600 = 500 × ω, so ω = 0.12 rad/s the other way.

  5. A torque of 2.0 N·m acts on a disk with I = 0.50 kg·m². Find α.

    Show answer

    α = τ/I = 4.0 rad/s².

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Torque depends only on the size of the force.

    Actually: It also depends on where and at what angle you push. Pushing a door near the hinge barely turns it.

  • Myth: All round objects roll down a slope at the same speed.

    Actually: A solid ball beats a solid cylinder, which beats a hoop. Less of their energy goes into spinning.

  • Myth: Pulling in your arms creates angular momentum.

    Actually: Angular momentum stays the same; the spin rate rises because I falls.

Flashcards

    Rate of rotation, rad/s.

  • Moment of inertia I

    Resistance to angular acceleration; I = Σmr².

  • Torque τ

    Turning effect of a force; τ = rF sin θ (N·m).

  • τ = Iα

    Newton’s second law for rotation.

  • Angular momentum L

    L = Iω; conserved with no external torque.

  • Rolling without slipping

    v = ωr at the contact point; total K = ½mv² + ½Iω².

Unit 7 · Weeks 11–12

Gravitation & Orbits

Newton’s law of gravity explains why satellites stay up, how long an orbit takes, and how to get from one orbit to another.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use Newton’s law of universal gravitation and find g at any altitude.
  • Find the speed and period of a circular orbit and the radius of a geostationary orbit.
  • Use gravitational potential energy to find escape speed.
  • Apply Kepler’s three laws and estimate a Hohmann transfer.

Key concepts

  • Every mass attracts every other mass with F = Gm₁m₂/r². Gravity weakens with the square of the distance from the center.
  • A circular orbit happens when gravity supplies exactly the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r.
  • Kepler’s laws: orbits are ellipses with the planet at one focus; a line to the planet sweeps equal areas in equal times; T² ∝ a³.
  • Far from Earth, gravitational potential energy is U = −GMm/r. Escape speed is the speed that makes total energy zero.
  • A Hohmann transfer moves between two circular orbits with two burns along a half-ellipse.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 7.1 Find g at Earth’s surface from G, Earth’s mass and radius.

Show the steps
  1. g = GM/R² = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.972 × 10²⁴ ÷ (6.371 × 10⁶)².
  2. GM = 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³/s²; R² = 4.059 × 10¹³ m².
  3. g = 9.82 m/s².

Answer: 9.82 m/s². Measured g varies from about 9.78 m/s² at the equator to 9.83 m/s² at the poles, because Earth spins and bulges at the equator; 9.81 m/s² is a convenient average.

Example 7.2 Landsat 9 orbits about 705 km above Earth. Treating the orbit as a circle, find its speed and period.

Show the steps
  1. r = 6,371 + 705 = 7,076 km = 7.076 × 10⁶ m.
  2. v = √(GM/r) = √(3.986 × 10¹⁴ ÷ 7.076 × 10⁶) = 7,505 m/s.
  3. T = 2πr/v = 2π × 7.076 × 10⁶ ÷ 7,505 = 5,924 s = 98.7 min.

Answer: 7.51 km/s and about 99 minutes per orbit, which matches the published figure.

Example 7.3 At what radius does a satellite orbit once per sidereal day (86,164 s), staying over one spot on the equator?

Show the steps
  1. r³ = GM T²/4π² = 3.986 × 10¹⁴ × 86,164² ÷ 39.48.
  2. r³ = 7.496 × 10²².
  3. r = 4.216 × 10⁷ m = 42,160 km from Earth’s center, about 35,790 km above the equator.

Answer: About 42,160 km from Earth’s center: geostationary orbit, where many weather satellites sit.

  4. Estimate the total Δv for a Hohmann transfer from a 300 km circular orbit to geostationary radius (42,164 km).

    Show answer

Common misconceptions

    Actually: At 420 km, gravity is still 88% of its surface value. Astronauts and the station fall together.

  • Myth: A heavier satellite needs to go faster to stay in the same orbit.

    Actually: v = √(GM/r) has no satellite mass in it.

  • Myth: To catch up with a spacecraft ahead of you in the same orbit, speed up.

    Actually: Speeding up raises your orbit and lengthens your period, so you fall behind. You slow down to drop into a faster, lower orbit.

Orbit choice is physics too: sun-synchronous orbits like Landsat 9’s repeat lighting conditions so changes on the ground can be compared fairly, and geostationary orbits let weather satellites watch storms continuously.

Oscillations & Introduction to Fluids

Springs and pendulums repeat in simple harmonic motion; fluids push with pressure and lift with buoyancy and flow.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Describe simple harmonic motion and find period, frequency and maximum speed for a spring and a pendulum.
  • Explain damping and resonance.
  • Compute pressure at depth and buoyant force.
  • Use the continuity equation and Bernoulli’s equation for steady flow.

Key concepts

  • A restoring force proportional to displacement (F = −kx) gives simple harmonic motion, x = A cos(ωt + φ).
  • The period of a mass on a spring does not depend on amplitude; a pendulum’s period depends only on its length and g for small swings.
  • Pressure in a fluid increases with depth: P = P₀ + ρgh.
  • Archimedes: the buoyant force equals the weight of fluid pushed aside.
  • In steady flow, a fluid speeds up where a pipe narrows (continuity), and faster flow has lower pressure (Bernoulli).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 8.1 A 0.50 kg mass on a spring with k = 200 N/m is pulled 5.0 cm and released. Find ω, the period, the frequency and the maximum speed.

Show the steps
  1. ω = √(k/m) = √(200 ÷ 0.50) = 20 rad/s.
  2. T = 2π/ω = 0.314 s; f = 1/T = 3.18 Hz.
  3. v_max = Aω = 0.050 × 20 = 1.0 m/s.

Answer: ω = 20 rad/s, T = 0.314 s, f = 3.18 Hz, v_max = 1.0 m/s.

Example 8.2 What is the period of a 1.00 m pendulum on Earth, and on the Moon (g = 1.62 m/s²)?

Show the steps
  1. Earth: T = 2π√(1.00 ÷ 9.81) = 2.01 s.
  2. Moon: T = 2π√(1.00 ÷ 1.62) = 4.94 s.

Answer: 2.01 s on Earth, 4.94 s on the Moon.

Example 8.3 Water flows at 2.0 m/s through a level pipe 4.0 cm across, which narrows to 2.0 cm. Find the new speed and the pressure drop.

Show the steps
  1. Area falls by (2.0/4.0)² = ¼, so v₂ = 4 × 2.0 = 8.0 m/s.
  2. Bernoulli, level pipe: ΔP = ½ρ(v₂² − v₁²) = ½ × 1,000 × (64 − 4.0) = 30,000 Pa.

Answer: 8.0 m/s and a pressure drop of 30 kPa.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. What is the extra pressure 10 m below the surface of seawater (ρ = 1,025 kg/m³)?

    Show answer

    ρgh = 1,025 × 9.81 × 10 = 100,600 Pa, about one more atmosphere.

  2. A block of wood with density 600 kg/m³ floats in fresh water. What fraction is under water?

    Show answer

    Floating: ρ_wood V g = ρ_water V_sub g, so V_sub/V = 600/1,000 = 0.60.

  3. A pendulum must have a period of exactly 2.00 s. How long should it be?

    Show answer

    L = g(T/2π)² = 9.81 × (2.00 ÷ 6.283)² = 0.994 m.

  4. Why can soldiers be told to break step when crossing a bridge?

    Show answer

    Marching in step pushes the bridge at a steady frequency. If that matches a natural frequency of the bridge, resonance can build up large oscillations.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: A bigger swing makes a pendulum take longer.

    Actually: For small swings the period does not depend on amplitude, only on L and g.

  • Myth: Heavy things sink and light things float.

    Actually: Density decides. A steel ship floats because its shape pushes aside a weight of water equal to its own weight.

  • Myth: Faster-flowing fluid has higher pressure.

    Actually: In steady, level flow, faster flow has lower pressure (Bernoulli).

How this helps save the planet

Lift on a wing and on a wind-turbine blade comes from the air flowing faster over one side, which lowers the pressure there. Bernoulli’s equation is the first step in designing blades and wings that waste less energy.

Oscillation physics matters for clean-energy machines too: any tall structure that is pushed rhythmically, as a turbine tower is by its spinning rotor, can build up large vibrations if the push matches one of its natural frequencies (resonance).

Fluid pressure and buoyancy are behind ocean science too, from floats that measure the deep ocean to satellites such as Sentinel-6 that measure sea level to help coastal communities plan.

Midterm practice set (Units 1–4)

  1. A rocket accelerates straight up from rest at 5.0 m/s² for 20 s, then its engine stops. What is the maximum height it reaches?

    Show full solution
    1. At burnout: v = 5.0 × 20 = 100 m/s; height = ½ × 5.0 × 20² = 1,000 m.
    2. Coasting up: extra height = v²/2g = 100² ÷ 19.62 = 510 m.

    Answer: 1,510 m, ignoring drag.

  2. A ball is kicked at 25 m/s at 40° above level ground. Find its range and time of flight.

    Show full solution
    1. v_y = 25 sin 40° = 16.1 m/s; t = 2 × 16.1 ÷ 9.81 = 3.28 s.
    2. R = 25² × sin 80° ÷ 9.81 = 625 × 0.985 ÷ 9.81 = 62.7 m.

    Answer: 62.7 m in 3.28 s.

  3. A 20 kg sled is pulled at constant speed across snow (μₖ = 0.10) by a horizontal rope. Find the rope tension and the work done over 50 m.

    Show full solution
    1. Constant speed: T = μₖmg = 0.10 × 20 × 9.81 = 19.6 N.
    2. W = 19.6 × 50 = 981 J.

    Answer: 19.6 N; 981 J, all turned into heat by friction.

  4. A 2.0 kg block slides from rest down a frictionless curved track from 5.0 m high and then compresses a spring (k = 400 N/m) on level ground. Find the maximum compression.

    Show full solution
    1. mgh = ½kx².
    2. x = √(2 × 2.0 × 9.81 × 5.0 ÷ 400) = √0.4905 = 0.700 m.

    Answer: 0.700 m.

Final practice set (all units)

  1. A 3.0 kg cart at 4.0 m/s collides with a 1.0 kg cart at rest and they stick. Find the final speed and the fraction of kinetic energy lost.

    Show full solution
    1. v = 3.0 × 4.0 ÷ 4.0 = 3.0 m/s.
    2. K before = 24 J; after = ½ × 4.0 × 9.0 = 18 J.

    Answer: 3.0 m/s; 25% of the kinetic energy is lost.

  2. A hollow ring and a solid disk of equal mass and radius roll from rest down a ramp 1.0 m high. Find each speed at the bottom.

    Show full solution
    1. Ring (I = mr²): mgh = mv², so v = √(gh) = 3.13 m/s.

    Answer: Ring 3.13 m/s, disk 3.62 m/s; the disk wins.

  3. Find the orbital period of a satellite in a circular orbit 1,336 km above Earth.

    Show full solution
    1. r = 6,371 + 1,336 = 7,707 km.
    2. T = 2π√(r³/GM) = 2π√((7.707 × 10⁶)³ ÷ 3.986 × 10¹⁴) = 6,733 s.

  4. A 0.20 kg mass on a spring oscillates with a period of 0.50 s and amplitude 0.10 m. Find k and the maximum speed.
