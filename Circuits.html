About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a one-semester sophomore circuit analysis course with a lab. It is modeled on the topics and depth of MIT 6.002 Circuits and Electronics (free on MIT OpenCourseWare), Georgia Tech ECE 2040 Circuit Analysis and Purdue ECE 20001 Electrical Engineering Fundamentals I, and on the free OpenStax University Physics Volume 2 and PhET simulations, which we link where they help. None of those authors or schools endorses this page.

What we add: every unit ties to real clean-energy work with a cited public source (LED lighting, solar panels and inverters, battery storage, EV charging, data centers and the power grid), every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab has a free PhET simulation or a safe low-voltage home version.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Circuits are how clean energy reaches people. Solar panels make direct current that inverters turn into alternating current, batteries store it, transformers and power lines carry it, and efficient lights, motors and chargers use it.

Every percent of loss in a wire, transformer or converter is energy that had to be generated somewhere. The analysis in this course is what engineers use to cut those losses. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Physics II: charge, electric field, potential, current, resistance, capacitance, inductance and Faraday’s law.
  • Calculus I and II: derivatives and integrals; Units 7 and 8 solve first- and second-order differential equations.
  • Complex numbers (reviewed in Unit 9) for phasors; Linear Algebra helps with nodal and mesh equations.
  • Differential Equations can be taken alongside this course.

How to study

  • Draw and label the circuit first: node voltages, branch currents with arrows, and polarity marks on every element.
  • Use the passive sign convention: current entering the + terminal means the element absorbs power.
  • Write KCL at nodes or KVL around meshes, count unknowns and equations, then solve (by hand for 2 × 2, by matrix for more).
  • Check every answer: power absorbed must equal power supplied, and limits (t = 0, t → ∞, ω → 0 or ∞) must make sense.
  • Keep units in SI: V, A, Ω, W, F, H, s, and watch prefixes (mA, kΩ, µF, mH).
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer, and retake the quizzes a few days later.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer or tablet for the free PhET simulations (they run in a browser).
  • A digital multimeter (inexpensive models measure V, A and Ω).
  • A solderless breadboard, jumper wires, a 2 × AA battery holder (3 V) and AA batteries.
  • Resistors (100 Ω, 1 kΩ, 10 kΩ), a 1000 µF capacitor rated at least 6.3 V, and LEDs.
  • Optional: a plug-in power meter for comparing light bulbs and appliances (it plugs into the wall; do not open it).
  • Safety glasses, a stopwatch or phone, and spreadsheet software.

Unit 1 · Weeks 1–2

Circuit Variables and Elements: Charge, Current, Voltage, Power, Energy and Ohm’s Law

Charge and current, voltage as energy per charge, power and energy, the passive sign convention, ideal independent and dependent sources, resistance and resistivity, Ohm’s law, and power in resistors.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Relate charge, current, voltage, power and energy with correct units.
  • Apply the passive sign convention to decide whether an element absorbs or delivers power.
  • Use Ohm’s law and R = ρL/A.
  • Compute energy use in kWh and compare devices.

Key concepts

  • Current is the rate of charge flow, i = dq/dt, in amperes (1 A = 1 C/s). Voltage is energy per unit charge between two points, in volts (1 V = 1 J/C).
  • Power p = vi. With the passive sign convention (current enters the + terminal), p > 0 means the element absorbs power and p < 0 means it delivers power. In any circuit, total power absorbed = total power delivered.
  • An ideal voltage source holds its voltage whatever the current; an ideal current source holds its current whatever the voltage. Dependent sources are controlled by a voltage or current elsewhere and model transistors and amplifiers.
  • Ohm’s law v = iR. A resistor always absorbs power p = i²R = v²/R. Resistance of a wire R = ρL/A grows with length and falls with cross-section; conductance G = 1/R in siemens.
  • Energy w = ∫p dt; at constant power w = Pt. Utilities bill in kilowatt-hours: 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 A 4 Ω heater is connected to 12 V. Find the current and power.

Show the steps
  1. i = v/R = 12/4 = 3 A.
  2. p = vi = 12 × 3.

Answer: i = 3 A, p = 36 W.

Example 1.2 Find the resistance of 10 m of copper wire with a 1 mm² cross-section (ρ = 1.68 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m).

Show the steps
  1. A = 1 mm² = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m².
  2. R = ρL/A = 1.68 × 10⁻⁸ × 10/10⁻⁶.

Answer: R = 0.168 Ω.

Example 1.3 A 60 W incandescent bulb is replaced by an LED that uses 75% less energy. Both run 3 hours a day for a year. Find the energy saved.

Show the steps
  1. LED power = 0.25 × 60 = 15 W.
  2. Incandescent: 60 × 3 × 365 = 65,700 Wh = 65.7 kWh; LED: 16.4 kWh.

Answer: About 49.3 kWh saved per bulb per year.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Current is used up by a resistor.

    Actually: Charge is conserved; the same current leaves as enters. Energy is converted, not current.

  • Myth: Voltage exists at a point.

    Actually: Voltage is always between two points; a “node voltage” is measured from a chosen ground.

  • Myth: Power is always vi with a plus sign.

    Actually: Use the passive sign convention to get the sign right.

How this helps save the planet

Lighting is the simplest place to see power and energy at work. The U.S. Department of Energy’s guide to energy-efficient lighting says LEDs use at least 75 percent less energy than incandescent bulbs, emit virtually no heat, and last 25 times longer.

With p = vi and w = Pt, the worked example shows what that means for one bulb: replacing a 60 W incandescent with a 15 W LED, used 3 hours a day, saves about 49.3 kWh a year. Multiply by the dozens of bulbs in a home and the millions of homes in a country and the power plants that never have to run become visible.

Unit 2 · Week 3

Kirchhoff’s Laws, Series and Parallel Resistors, and Voltage and Current Dividers

Nodes, branches and loops, Kirchhoff’s current and voltage laws, series and parallel combinations, equivalent resistance, voltage and current dividers, delta–wye conversions, and measuring with voltmeters and ammeters.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Write KCL and KVL for any circuit.
  • Reduce series–parallel networks to an equivalent resistance.
  • Use voltage and current dividers.
  • Explain how meters are connected and how they load a circuit.

Key concepts

  • KCL: the sum of currents leaving any node is zero (charge is conserved). KVL: the sum of voltages around any closed loop is zero (energy is conserved).
  • Series elements carry the same current; resistances add: R_eq = R₁ + R₂ + …. Parallel elements share the same voltage; conductances add: 1/R_eq = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …; for two resistors R₁R₂/(R₁ + R₂).
  • Voltage divider: v₂ = v·R₂/(R₁ + R₂). Current divider (two resistors): i₁ = i·R₂/(R₁ + R₂); the larger share goes through the smaller resistor.
  • Delta–wye (Δ–Y) conversion handles bridges that are neither series nor parallel: R_Y = (product of the two adjacent Δ resistors)/(sum of all three).
  • A voltmeter goes in parallel and should have very high resistance; an ammeter goes in series and should have very low resistance.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 Resistors of 100, 200 and 300 Ω are in series across 12 V. Find the current and the voltage across the 200 Ω resistor.

Show the steps
  1. R_eq = 600 Ω; i = 12/600 = 0.02 A.
  2. v = 0.02 × 200.

Answer: i = 20 mA, v₂₀₀ = 4 V.

Example 2.2 A 9 V source feeds a divider with R₁ = 10 kΩ on top and R₂ = 5 kΩ on the bottom. Find v_out across R₂.

Show the steps
  1. v_out = 9 × 5/(10 + 5).

Answer: v_out = 3 V.

Example 2.3 A 6 A current splits between 2 Ω and 4 Ω in parallel. Find each current.

Show the steps
  1. i₂Ω = 6 × 4/(2 + 4) = 4 A.
  2. i₄Ω = 6 × 2/6 = 2 A; check 4 + 2 = 6.

Answer: 4 A through 2 Ω and 2 A through 4 Ω.

Practice problems

  2. Three 30 Ω resistors are in parallel. Find R_eq.

    Show answer

  3. Find R_eq of 10 Ω in series with (20 Ω ∥ 20 Ω).

    Show answer

    10 + 10 = 20 Ω.

  4. Three currents enter a node: 2 A, 3 A and −1 A. What current leaves?

    Show answer

    4 A.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Parallel resistance is larger than the smallest resistor.

    Actually: It is always smaller than the smallest one.

  • Myth: A voltage divider works with any load.

    Actually: A load across R₂ is in parallel with it and lowers v_out unless its resistance is much larger.

  • Myth: An ammeter goes across the element.

    Actually: Across a low-resistance ammeter is nearly a short circuit; it goes in series.

How this helps save the planet

Solar panels are series and parallel circuits. The U.S. Energy Information Administration explains that one PV cell can only produce 1 or 2 watts, so cells are electrically connected in weather-tight panels, panels are connected into arrays of two to hundreds of panels, and the number of panels sets how much electricity an array can generate. Cells make direct current, and inverters convert it to the alternating current used on the grid.

Series connection adds voltages and parallel connection adds currents. As a sample, cells making 0.5 V and 3 A (1.5 W, within EIA’s 1–2 W range) give 30 V at 3 A, or 90 W, when 60 are wired in series. KCL and KVL also explain why one shaded cell in a series string can drag down the whole string, which is why panels include bypass diodes.

Unit 3 · Weeks 4–5

Systematic Analysis: Node-Voltage and Mesh-Current Methods

Choosing a reference node, writing KCL in terms of node voltages, supernodes, mesh currents and KVL, supermeshes, circuits with dependent sources, and solving the resulting linear systems by hand and with matrices.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Write and solve node-voltage equations, including supernodes.
  • Write and solve mesh-current equations, including supermeshes.
  • Handle dependent sources in both methods.
  • Choose the method that gives fewer equations.

Key concepts

  • Node-voltage method: pick a ground node, name the other node voltages, write KCL at each non-reference node using Ohm’s law (current from a to b through R is (v_a − v_b)/R), and solve.
  • A voltage source between two non-reference nodes makes a supernode: write one KCL for the combined region plus the source equation v_a − v_b = V_s.
  • Mesh-current method (planar circuits): assign a clockwise current to each window, write KVL around each mesh; a shared resistor carries the difference of the two mesh currents.
  • A current source shared by two meshes makes a supermesh: one KVL around the combined loop plus the source equation.
  • An N-node circuit needs N − 1 node equations; a circuit with M meshes needs M mesh equations. Choose the smaller set. The equations form Gv = i or Ri = v, a linear system.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 A 10 V source feeds node 1 through 2 Ω. Node 1 has 4 Ω to ground and 2 Ω to node 2; node 2 has 4 Ω to ground and a 1 A source flowing into it. Find v₁ and v₂.

Show the steps
  1. Node 1 (×4): 2(v₁ − 10) + v₁ + 2(v₁ − v₂) = 0, so 5v₁ − 2v₂ = 20.
  2. Node 2 (×4): 2(v₂ − v₁) + v₂ − 4 = 0, so −2v₁ + 3v₂ = 4.
  3. det = 15 − 4 = 11; v₁ = (60 + 8)/11, v₂ = (20 + 40)/11.

Answer: v₁ = 6.182 V, v₂ = 5.455 V.

Example 3.2 Mesh 1 has a 10 V source and 2 Ω; mesh 2 has 6 Ω; the meshes share 4 Ω. Find the mesh currents and the current in the shared resistor.

Show the steps
  1. Mesh 1: 6i₁ − 4i₂ = 10. Mesh 2: −4i₁ + 10i₂ = 0.
  2. det = 60 − 16 = 44; i₁ = 100/44, i₂ = 40/44.

Answer: i₁ = 2.273 A, i₂ = 0.909 A; shared resistor carries 1.364 A.

Example 3.3 A 5 V source sits between nodes a and b (v_a − v_b = 5). Node a has 1 Ω to ground, node b has 4 Ω to ground, and 2 A is injected into the supernode. Find v_a and v_b.

Show the steps
  1. Supernode KCL: v_a/1 + v_b/4 = 2.
  2. Substitute v_a = v_b + 5: v_b + 5 + v_b/4 = 2, so 1.25v_b = −3.

Answer: v_b = −2.4 V, v_a = 2.6 V.

  3. Two meshes share a 3 Ω resistor; i₁ = 2 A and i₂ = 0.5 A, both clockwise. Find the current in the shared resistor.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: KCL can be written at the reference node too.

    Actually: It gives a dependent equation; use N − 1 nodes.

  • Myth: Mesh currents are the branch currents.

    Actually: Only on outer branches; shared branches carry differences.

  • Myth: A voltage source current is zero because it has no resistance.

    Actually: Its current is set by the rest of the circuit; that is why supernodes are used.

How this helps save the planet

The power grid is one enormous nodal-analysis problem. EIA explains that the U.S. grid is made up of thousands of miles of high-voltage lines and millions of miles of low-voltage lines connecting thousands of power plants to hundreds of millions of customers. At the start of the 20th century more than 4,000 isolated utilities operated; today the Lower 48 run as three main interconnections, Eastern, Western and ERCOT, linked to provide multiple paths for power to flow.

EIA notes that supply must match demand at every moment and that balancing authorities keep them matched. Grid operators do that with power-flow models whose equations are the AC version of the node-voltage method in this unit, solved for thousands of buses, and the same analysis tells planners where new lines are needed to connect wind and solar far from cities.

Unit 4 · Week 6

Circuit Theorems: Linearity, Superposition, Source Transformation, Thevenin, Norton and Maximum Power Transfer

Linearity and superposition, source transformations, Thevenin and Norton equivalent circuits, finding R_Th by deactivating sources or with a test source, and the maximum power transfer theorem.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use superposition to analyze circuits with several sources.
  • Convert between voltage and current sources.
  • Find Thevenin and Norton equivalents.
  • Choose a load for maximum power transfer and compute that power.

Key concepts

  • Linearity: in a circuit of resistors and sources, every voltage and current is a linear combination of the source values. Superposition: find the response to each independent source alone (other voltage sources shorted, current sources opened) and add.
  • Source transformation: a voltage source V in series with R is equivalent, at its terminals, to a current source V/R in parallel with R.
  • Thevenin’s theorem: any linear two-terminal network looks like a source V_Th in series with R_Th. V_Th is the open-circuit voltage; R_Th is the resistance seen with independent sources turned off (or V_oc/i_sc).
  • Norton’s theorem: the same network is a current source I_N = i_sc in parallel with R_N = R_Th; V_Th = I_N R_Th.
  • Maximum power transfer: a load receives the most power when R_L = R_Th, and then P_max = V_Th²/(4R_Th). The efficiency at that point is only 50%, so power systems are designed for R_L ≫ R_source instead.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 A 12 V source in series with 4 Ω feeds terminals a–b, with 12 Ω across a–b. Find the Thevenin and Norton equivalents.

Show the steps
  1. V_Th = 12 × 12/(4 + 12) = 9 V (divider).
  2. R_Th = 4 ∥ 12 = 48/16 = 3 Ω (source shorted).
  3. I_N = 9/3.

Answer: V_Th = 9 V, R_Th = 3 Ω; I_N = 3 A.

Example 4.2 For that circuit, find the power to a 6 Ω load and the maximum possible load power.

Show the steps
  1. i = 9/(3 + 6) = 1 A; P = 1² × 6 = 6 W.
  2. Maximum at R_L = 3 Ω: P_max = 9²/(4 × 3).

Answer: 6 W; P_max = 6.75 W at 3 Ω.

Example 4.3 A node connects to a 10 V source through 5 Ω, to ground through 5 Ω, and receives a 2 A current source. Find the node voltage by superposition.

Show the steps

Operational Amplifiers

The ideal op amp and its two rules, inverting and non-inverting amplifiers, voltage followers, summing and difference amplifiers, saturation, cascaded stages, and op amps in sensors and measurement.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Apply the ideal op-amp rules with negative feedback.
  • Analyze inverting, non-inverting, follower, summing and difference circuits.
  • Recognize saturation and its limits.
  • Design a simple amplifier for a sensor signal.

Key concepts

  • An op amp amplifies the difference between its inputs by a very large gain. With negative feedback, the ideal rules are: no current flows into either input, and the two input voltages are equal (v₊ = v₋).
  • Inverting amplifier: v_o = −(R_f/R_in)v_in. Non-inverting amplifier: v_o = (1 + R_f/R_g)v_in. Voltage follower (buffer): v_o = v_in with almost no current drawn from the source.
  • Summing amplifier: v_o = −R_f(v₁/R₁ + v₂/R₂ + …). Difference amplifier with matched ratios: v_o = (R₂/R₁)(v₂ − v₁).
  • The output cannot exceed the supply rails; beyond them the op amp saturates and the linear rules stop applying.
  • Cascaded stages multiply their gains, and because an op-amp output acts like an ideal voltage source, a stage is not loaded by the next.

  2. v_o = −10 × 0.2.

Show the steps

Capacitors and Inductors

Capacitance and inductance, their current–voltage relations, stored energy, continuity of capacitor voltage and inductor current, series and parallel combinations, DC steady-state behavior, and op-amp integrators and differentiators.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use i = C dv/dt and v = L di/dt.
  • Compute energy stored in capacitors and inductors.
  • Combine capacitors and inductors in series and parallel.
  • Find DC steady-state voltages and currents in circuits with C and L.

Key concepts

  • A capacitor stores energy in an electric field: q = Cv, i = C dv/dt, w = ½Cv². Its voltage cannot jump instantly; at DC steady state it acts as an open circuit.
  • An inductor stores energy in a magnetic field: v = L di/dt, w = ½Li². Its current cannot jump instantly; at DC steady state it acts as a short circuit.
  • Capacitors in parallel add; in series they combine like resistors in parallel. Inductors combine like resistors: series add, parallel combine as reciprocals.
  • An op amp with a capacitor as feedback integrates: v_o = −(1/RC)∫v_in dt. Swapping R and C gives a differentiator.
  • Ideal capacitors and inductors store and return energy without loss; real ones have small resistance and leakage.

First-Order Circuits: RC and RL Transients

Natural and step responses of RC and RL circuits, the time constant, the general solution x(t) = x(∞) + [x(0) − x(∞)]e^(−t/τ), switching problems, and energy in digital switching.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Find initial and final values across a switching event.
  • Compute time constants τ = RC and τ = L/R.
  • Write and use the general first-order solution.
  • Estimate switching energy and power in digital circuits.

Key concepts

  • A circuit with one capacitor or one inductor (plus resistors and DC sources) obeys a first-order differential equation. Its response moves exponentially from an initial value to a final value.
  • Time constant: τ = R_Th C for an RC circuit and τ = L/R_Th for an RL circuit, where R_Th is the resistance seen by the C or L.
  • General solution: x(t) = x(∞) + [x(0⁺) − x(∞)] e^(−t/τ). After one τ the change is 63.2% complete; after 5τ it is 99.3% complete.
  • Use continuity: v_C(0⁺) = v_C(0⁻) and i_L(0⁺) = i_L(0⁻). Find x(∞) from the DC steady state (C open, L short).
  • Charging a capacitance C to V through a resistor takes CV² from the source: ½CV² is stored and ½CV² is lost as heat. Digital chips charge tiny capacitances billions of times a second, so their dynamic power is about P = αCV²f.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

  2. v_C(τ) = 3(1 − e⁻¹) = 3 × 0.632.
  3. 99.3% takes 5τ.

Answer: τ = 10 s, v_C(τ) = 1.90 V, about 50 s.

Example 7.2 A 0.5 H inductor in series with 10 Ω is switched onto 20 V at t = 0. Find τ, the final current and i(0.1 s).

Show the steps
  1. τ = 0.5/10 = 0.05 s; i(∞) = 20/10 = 2 A.
  2. i(0.1) = 2(1 − e^(−0.1/0.05)) = 2(1 − e⁻²).

Answer: τ = 50 ms, i(∞) = 2 A, i(0.1 s) = 1.73 A.

Example 7.3 A charged capacitor discharges through a resistor with τ = 10 s. When does its voltage reach half its starting value?

Show the steps
  1. V₀e^(−t/τ) = V₀/2 gives t = τ ln 2.

Answer: t = 6.93 s.

Second-Order Circuits: RLC Natural and Step Responses

Series and parallel RLC circuits, the characteristic equation, the neper frequency α and resonant frequency ω₀, overdamped, critically damped and underdamped responses, quality factor, and LC energy exchange.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Write the second-order equation for series and parallel RLC circuits.
  • Compute α, ω₀ and ω_d and classify the damping.
  • Find the response form and its constants from initial conditions.
  • Relate Q to ringing and energy loss.

Key concepts

  • A circuit with an inductor and a capacitor obeys a second-order equation. For a series RLC: α = R/(2L), ω₀ = 1/√(LC). For a parallel RLC: α = 1/(2RC), same ω₀.
  • Roots s = −α ± √(α² − ω₀²). Overdamped (α > ω₀): two real roots, no oscillation. Critically damped (α = ω₀): fastest return without overshoot. Underdamped (α < ω₀): decaying oscillation at ω_d = √(ω₀² − α²).
  • An ideal LC circuit (R = 0) swaps energy between the capacitor’s electric field and the inductor’s magnetic field forever at ω₀.
  • Quality factor for a series RLC: Q = ω₀L/R = (1/R)√(L/C). High Q means sharp resonance and long ringing.
  • Critical resistance for a series RLC: R = 2√(L/C).

Key equations

Sinusoidal Steady State: Phasors and Impedance

Sinusoids, amplitude, frequency, phase and RMS values, complex numbers, phasors, impedance and admittance of R, L and C, AC circuit analysis with Ohm’s law, KCL, KVL, nodal and Thevenin methods in phasor form, and frequency response basics.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Convert between time-domain sinusoids and phasors.
  • Compute impedances of R, L and C at a given frequency.
  • Analyze AC circuits with phasor versions of the DC methods.
  • Describe how impedance changes with frequency in simple filters.

Key concepts

  • A sinusoid v(t) = V_m cos(ωt + φ) has amplitude V_m, angular frequency ω = 2πf and phase φ. Its RMS value is V_m/√2; U.S. household 120 V is an RMS value at 60 Hz, a peak of about 170 V.
  • A phasor is the complex number V = V_m∠φ that carries amplitude and phase. In sinusoidal steady state, d/dt becomes multiplication by jω.
  • Impedance Z = V/I: Z_R = R, Z_L = jωL, Z_C = 1/(jωC) = −j/(ωC). Inductors look like shorts at low frequency and opens at high frequency; capacitors do the opposite.
  • All the DC tools (series/parallel, dividers, nodal and mesh analysis, Thevenin and Norton) work with impedances and complex arithmetic.
  • An RC low-pass filter passes low frequencies and attenuates high ones; its cutoff (half-power) frequency is ω_c = 1/(RC).

Key equations

Answer: V_rms = 120.2 V at f = 60.0 Hz.

Unit 10 · Week 14

AC Power, Power Factor, Resonance, Transformers and Three-Phase Systems

Instantaneous and average power, real, reactive and apparent power, the power triangle, power factor and its correction, series resonance and bandwidth, ideal transformers, balanced three-phase systems, and why the grid transmits at high voltage.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute real, reactive and apparent power and the power factor.
  • Size a capacitor for power-factor correction.
  • Analyze ideal transformers and balanced three-phase loads.
  • Explain transmission losses with I²R.

Key concepts

  • Average (real) power P = V_rms I_rms cos θ in watts, where θ is the angle between voltage and current. Reactive power Q = V_rms I_rms sin θ in VAR. Apparent power S = V_rms I_rms in VA, and S² = P² + Q².
  • Power factor pf = cos θ = P/S. Motors are inductive (lagging pf). A parallel capacitor supplies reactive power locally, raising pf and cutting the current the utility must deliver: C = Q/(ωV²) for full correction.
  • At series resonance ω₀ = 1/√(LC) the reactances cancel, the impedance is just R and current is largest; the bandwidth is ω₀/Q.
  • An ideal transformer with turns ratio N₁:N₂ has V₂/V₁ = N₂/N₁ and I₂/I₁ = N₁/N₂, so power in equals power out.
  • Balanced three-phase: three sinusoids 120° apart. Total power P = √3 V_L I_L cos θ, constant in time. Line losses are I²R, so raising voltage by k for the same power cuts current by k and losses by k².

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 10.1 A motor draws 20 A RMS from 240 V RMS at a power factor of 0.8 lagging. Find S, P and Q.

Show the steps
  1. S = 240 × 20 = 4800 VA.
  2. P = 4800 × 0.8; sin θ = 0.6, so Q = 4800 × 0.6.

Answer: S = 4.8 kVA, P = 3.84 kW, Q = 2.88 kVAR.

Example 10.2 What capacitor in parallel would correct that motor to unity power factor at 60 Hz?

Show the steps
  1. The capacitor must supply Q = 2880 VAR.
  2. C = Q/(ωV²) = 2880/(377.0 × 240²).

Answer: C = 132.6 µF; the line current falls from 20 A to 16 A.

Example 10.3 A line with 5 Ω resistance delivers 10 MW. Compare the I²R loss at 100 kV and at 20 kV (unity power factor, single-phase sample).

Show the steps
  1. At 100 kV: I = 10⁷/10⁵ = 100 A, loss = 100² × 5 = 50 kW (0.5%).
  2. At 20 kV: I = 500 A, loss = 500² × 5 = 1.25 MW (12.5%).

Answer: 5 times the voltage cuts losses 25 times.

  • Myth: Three-phase power is three times single-phase with the line voltage.

How this helps save the planet

The grid moves power at high voltage to keep I²R losses down. EIA explains that high-voltage transmission lines carry electricity over long distances because higher voltage makes long-distance transmission more efficient and less expensive, lower-voltage distribution lines deliver it to customers, and transformers at substations step voltages up and down between the stages. EIA estimates that transmission and distribution losses averaged about 5% of the electricity transmitted and distributed in the United States in 2018 through 2022.

The worked example shows the physics: five times the voltage cuts losses twenty-five times. Power-factor correction, efficient transformers and new long-distance lines to reach wind and solar resources, which EIA lists among the grid’s challenges, all chip away at that 5%. AFDC notes that common U.S. three-phase voltages are 208/120 V and 480/277 V, common at commercial and industrial sites, and that DC fast chargers typically take a three-phase AC input.
