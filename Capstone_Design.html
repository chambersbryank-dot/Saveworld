Capstone Design (Aircraft or CubeSat)

A full design project from requirements to review: pick an electric drone or a 3U CubeSat track, write requirements, select a concept, size it, close power, link, structural and thermal budgets, plan cost, schedule and risk, test, and present at a design review, with six spreadsheet labs. Free, self-paced, and tied to NASA’s CubeSat Launch Initiative, the PREFIRE CubeSats and NASA’s Sustainable Flight National Partnership.

About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a one-semester senior aerospace capstone. It is modeled on the conceive-design-implement-operate approach of MIT 16.83X Space Systems Engineering (free on MIT OpenCourseWare) and on the NASA Systems Engineering Handbook, which we link where they help. Neither MIT nor NASA endorses this page.

What we add: two parallel tracks (a small electric drone or a 3U CubeSat), every unit tied to real work with a cited public source, every number on the page re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab in a free spreadsheet.

Work through it in order, alone or with a team. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Capstone teams build the satellites and aircraft that measure and protect the planet: student CubeSats fly through NASA’s CubeSat Launch Initiative, and small satellites like PREFIRE study how the Arctic sheds heat.

On the aircraft side, NASA’s Sustainable Flight National Partnership aims for 25–30% energy-efficiency gains. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample or textbook values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Avionics & Systems Engineering: requirements, reliability and verification.
  • Spacecraft Design: power, thermal and link budgets.
  • Aircraft Performance: lift, drag, range and climb.
  • Aerospace Structures: stress, safety factors and margins.

How to study

  • Keep one design notebook or spreadsheet that every number flows from.
  • Trace every design choice to a requirement.
  • Close budgets with margin before adding detail.
  • Hold short weekly reviews, even when working alone.
  • Write as you go: every unit adds a section to your final report.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer, and retake the quizzes a few days later.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • Spreadsheet software (any free one works), or Python.
  • The free NASA Systems Engineering Handbook in a web browser.
  • Free slide software for your design reviews.
  • A calculator.

Unit 1 · Week 1

Mission, Stakeholders and Requirements

Choosing a track, defining the mission, identifying stakeholders, writing verifiable requirements and setting allocations with margin.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Write a one-sentence mission statement.
  • List stakeholders and their needs.
  • Write verifiable requirements.
  • Compute margin against an allocation.

Key concepts

  • Pick one track: a small electric drone that carries a sensor, or a 3U CubeSat that observes Earth.
  • A mission statement says who needs what and why; requirements make it measurable.
  • Stakeholders include users, funders, launch or airspace authorities, and the team itself.
  • Each requirement has a number, a unit and a verification method (test, analysis, inspection or demonstration).
  • Allocations carry margin early, because estimates grow as designs mature.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 A 3U CubeSat has a 4.0 kg allocation (textbook value) and a 3.4 kg estimate. Margin?

Show the steps
  1. (4.0 − 3.4)/3.4.

Answer: 17.6%.

Example 1.2 A drone sensor estimate is 0.8 kg. Allocate with 25% margin (sample values).

Show the steps
  1. 0.8 × 1.25.

Answer: 1.0 kg.

Example 1.3 Write a verifiable requirement for drone endurance.

Show the steps
  1. Number, unit, method.

Answer: “The drone shall fly at least 30 minutes at 15 m/s, verified by flight test.”

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Margin if the estimate grows to 3.8 kg?

    Show answer

    5.26%.

  2. Three textbook 1.33 kg units?

    Show answer

    3.99 kg, about 4 kg.

  3. Name four stakeholders for a student CubeSat.

    Show answer

    For example: science users, the university, the launch provider and the radio regulator.

  4. Is “the CubeSat shall be reliable” verifiable?

    Show answer

    No; add a number and a method.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Requirements describe the design.

    Actually: They state needs, not solutions.

  • Myth: Margin can be added later.

    Actually: It is cheapest to hold early.

  • Myth: One stakeholder is enough.

    Actually: Missed stakeholders cause late surprises.

Flashcards

    Who needs what and why.

  • Stakeholder

    Anyone with a need or say.

  • Requirement

    Verifiable need.

  • Allocation

    Budgeted share.

  • Margin

    Reserve over estimate.

  • Track

    Drone or CubeSat project path.

Unit 2 · Week 2

Concept Generation and Selection

Brainstorming, morphological charts, Pugh matrices, weighted decision matrices and sensitivity to weights.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Generate several concepts.
  • Build a morphological chart.
  • Score concepts in a weighted matrix.
  • Test sensitivity to weights.

Key concepts

  • Generate many concepts before judging any; quantity first, quality later.
  • A morphological chart lists options for each function and combines them into concepts.
  • A Pugh matrix compares concepts to a datum with +, 0 and −.
  • A weighted decision matrix multiplies scores by weights and sums them.
  • If small weight changes flip the winner, the decision needs more data.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 Concept A scores 6 (cost), 9 (performance), 5 (risk); B scores 8, 6, 8. Weights 0.3, 0.5, 0.2. Winner?

Show the steps
  1. A = 1.8 + 4.5 + 1.0 = 7.3.
  2. B = 2.4 + 3.0 + 1.6 = 7.0.

Answer: A, 7.3 to 7.0.

Example 2.2 Change weights to 0.4, 0.3, 0.3.

Show the steps
  1. A = 2.4 + 2.7 + 1.5 = 6.6.
  2. B = 3.2 + 1.8 + 2.4 = 7.4.

Answer: B wins: the choice is sensitive to weights.

Example 2.3 A chart has 3 power options, 2 structures and 4 sensors. Combinations?

Show the steps
  1. 3 × 2 × 4.

Answer: 24.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Pugh: 4 pluses, 1 minus. Net?

    Show answer

    +3.

  2. Combinations for 2 × 3 × 3 × 2?

    Show answer

    36.

  3. Why test weight sensitivity?

    Show answer

    To see if the winner is robust.

  4. Why brainstorm before scoring?

    Show answer

    Early judging kills good ideas.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: The highest score is always right.

    Actually: Check sensitivity and assumptions.

  • Myth: Weights are objective.

    Actually: They encode judgment.

  • Myth: One concept is enough.

    Actually: Compare several.

    Options per function.

  • Pugh matrix

    Compare to a datum.

  • Datum

    Reference concept.

  • Weighted matrix

    Scores times weights.

  • Sensitivity

    How results change with inputs.

  • Down-select

    Choosing a concept.

Unit 3 · Weeks 3–4

Aircraft Track: Weight Sizing and Range

Takeoff-weight estimation from weight fractions, the Breguet range equation, and how efficiency gains change range and fuel.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Estimate takeoff weight from fractions.
  • Apply the Breguet range equation.
  • Show how L/D changes range.
  • Iterate a sizing loop.

Key concepts

  • Takeoff weight equals payload divided by one minus the empty and energy fractions.
  • If the empty plus energy fractions approach 1, the design does not close.
  • For jet aircraft, Breguet range is (V/c)(L/D) ln(W_i/W_f).
  • Range is proportional to L/D, so a 30% L/D gain gives 30% more range at the same fuel fraction.
  • Sizing is iterative: estimate, compute, update fractions, repeat until the weight converges.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 A drone carries 1 kg payload with empty fraction 0.5 and battery fraction 0.3 (sample values). Takeoff mass?

Show the steps
  1. 1/(1 − 0.5 − 0.3).

Answer: 5 kg.

Example 3.2 A transport flies at 230 m/s with c = 0.6 per hour, L/D = 17 and W_i/W_f = 1.25 (textbook values). Range?

Show the steps
  1. c = 1.667 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹.
  2. R = (230/1.667 × 10⁻⁴) × 17 × ln 1.25 = 1.38 × 10⁶ × 17 × 0.2231.

Answer: About 5235 km.

Example 3.3 Raise L/D by 30%.

Show the steps
  1. Range scales with L/D.

Answer: About 6805 km.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Takeoff mass if the battery fraction rises to 0.35?

    Show answer

    6.67 kg.

  2. Weight of the 5 kg drone?

    Show answer

    49.0 N.

  3. Why can’t fractions sum to 1?

    Show answer

    Nothing would be left for payload.

  4. ln 1.25?

    Show answer

    0.223.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: More battery always means more range.

    Actually: It also raises weight; the fraction matters.

  • Myth: Breguet fits electric aircraft.

    Actually: Batteries don’t get lighter; use an energy form.

  • Myth: One pass is enough.

    Actually: Iterate until weight converges.

Flashcards

  • Empty fraction

    Empty over takeoff weight.

  • Fuel fraction

    Fuel over takeoff weight.

  • Breguet

    Range equation.

  • L/D

    Lift-to-drag ratio.

  • TSFC

    Thrust-specific fuel consumption.

  • Sizing loop

    Iterate to convergence.

How this helps save the planet

NASA says the Sustainable Flight National Partnership aims to enable 25–30% energy-efficiency improvements in next-generation transports that can use 100% sustainable aviation fuel and fly optimal trajectories.

Its centerpiece is a full-scale X-plane demonstrator testing an ultra-efficient aerodynamic design, with first flight no earlier than late 2026; higher L/D is exactly the lever this unit’s Breguet equation rewards.

Unit 4 · Week 5

Aircraft Track: Wing Loading and Power

Constraint sizing: wing loading from stall speed, wing area, and climb power for a small electric aircraft.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Find wing loading from stall speed.
  • Size the wing area.
  • Compute climb power.
  • Draw a simple constraint diagram.

Key concepts

  • Stall speed sets the maximum wing loading: W/S = ½ρV_s²C_Lmax.
  • Wing area follows from weight divided by wing loading.
  • Shaft power for climb covers drag (W V/(L/D)) plus the climb term (W × rate of climb), divided by propulsive efficiency.
  • Each requirement draws a line on a W/S versus power-to-weight plot; the design point lies in the region meeting all.
  • Small drones fly at low Reynolds numbers, so check airfoil data for small scales.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 Stall at 12 m/s, C_Lmax = 1.2, sea level. Wing loading?

Show the steps
  1. ½ × 1.225 × 144 × 1.2.

Answer: 105.8 N/m².

Example 4.2 Wing area for the 5 kg drone?

Show the steps
  1. 49.03/105.84.

Answer: 0.463 m².

Example 4.3 Climb power at 15 m/s, L/D = 10, 2 m/s climb, η = 0.7?

Show the steps
  1. 49.03 × (1.5 + 2) = 171.6 W.
  2. 171.6/0.7.

Answer: 245 W.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Wing loading for a 10 m/s stall?

    Show answer

    73.5 N/m².

  2. Power without the climb term?

    Show answer

    105.1 W.

  3. Dynamic pressure at 15 m/s?

    Show answer

    137.8 Pa.

  4. Why does stall set a maximum W/S?

    Show answer

    Higher W/S needs higher stall speed.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Bigger wings are always better.

    Actually: They add weight and drag.

  • Myth: Climb power is small.

    Actually: It can dominate small drones.

  • Myth: η = 1.

    Actually: Propellers and motors lose energy.

Flashcards

  • Wing loading

    Weight per wing area.

  • C_Lmax

    Maximum lift coefficient.

  • Constraint diagram

    Requirements on one plot.

  • Rate of climb

    Vertical speed.

  • Propulsive efficiency

    Useful power fraction.

  • Stall speed

    Minimum steady speed.

Unit 5 · Week 6

CubeSat Track: Sizing and Orbit

CubeSat form factors, mass allowance, orbit period, passes per day and eclipse fraction for a low Earth orbit.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Size a 3U CubeSat mass budget.
  • Compute orbit period.
  • Compute orbits per day.
  • Compute the worst-case eclipse.

Key concepts

  • CubeSats come in 10 cm units (U); a 3U is about 10 × 10 × 34 cm (textbook value).
  • Mass allowance is roughly 1.33 kg per U (textbook value); confirm with the launch provider.
  • Orbit period depends only on the semi-major axis: T = 2π√(a³/μ).
  • In the worst case (Sun in the orbit plane), eclipse fraction is asin(R_E/a)/π.
  • Low orbits decay faster from drag; most student CubeSats re-enter within years.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 Period at 500 km altitude?

Show the steps
  1. a = 6878.1 km.
  2. T = 2π√(6878.1³/398,600.4).

Answer: 94.6 min.

Example 5.2 Orbits per day?

Show the steps
  1. 1440/94.6.

Answer: 15.2.

Example 5.3 Worst-case eclipse?

Show the steps
  1. asin(6378.1/6878.1)/π = 0.378.
  2. 0.378 × 94.6.

Answer: 35.8 min.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. 3U mass allowance at 1.33 kg/U?

    Show answer

    3.99 kg.

  2. Sunlit time per orbit?

    Show answer

    58.9 min.

  3. Does period depend on CubeSat mass?

    Show answer

    No.

  4. Why do low orbits decay?

    Show answer

    Atmospheric drag.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Heavier satellites orbit slower.

    Actually: Period depends only on a.

  • Myth: Eclipse is always 50%.

    Actually: It depends on altitude and Sun angle.

  • Myth: Use altitude in Kepler’s law.

    Actually: Use a = R_E + h.

Flashcards

    Standard small satellite in 10 cm units.

  • 3U

    Three-unit CubeSat.

  • Period

    Time per orbit.

  • Eclipse fraction

    Share of orbit in shadow.

  • Semi-major axis

    R_E + h for circular orbits.

  • Decay

    Orbit loss from drag.

How this helps save the planet

NASA says PREFIRE uses a pair of tiny satellites, CubeSats launched in May 2024, to measure a little-studied part of the radiant energy Earth emits, for clues about sea ice loss, ice-sheet melting and a warming Arctic.

NASA calls the Arctic the region that behaves like our planet’s thermostat, venting excess energy received in the tropics, which is why a pair of small satellites there matters.

Unit 6 · Week 7

CubeSat Track: Power and Link Budgets

Solar-array output, orbit-average power, battery sizing for eclipse, UHF link path loss and daily data volume.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute array output in sunlight.
  • Compute orbit-average power.
  • Size a battery for eclipse.
  • Estimate path loss and data per day.

Key concepts

  • Array power is solar flux × cell efficiency × area × cos(angle).
  • Orbit-average generation is reduced by the eclipse fraction (and by pointing in practice).
  • The battery covers the eclipse load; limiting depth of discharge extends cycle life.
  • Free-space path loss grows as 20 log₁₀(4πd/λ); UHF amateur bands are common for student CubeSats.
  • Data volume per day is bit rate × pass time × passes.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 6.1 Panels of 0.06 m² with 28% cells face the Sun (sample values). Output?

Show the steps
  1. 1361 × 0.28 × 0.06.

Answer: 22.9 W.

Example 6.2 Orbit average with Unit 5’s eclipse fraction of 0.378?

Show the steps
  1. 22.86 × 0.622.

Answer: 14.2 W.

Example 6.3 Battery for 8 W through a 35.8 min eclipse at 30% depth of discharge?

Show the steps
  1. 8 × 35.75/60 = 4.77 Wh.
  2. 4.77/0.3.

Answer: 15.9 Wh.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Path loss at 437 MHz over 1000 km?

    Show answer

    λ = 0.686 m; L = 145.3 dB.

  2. Data per day at 9600 bit/s, four 10-minute passes?

    Show answer

    23.04 Mbit = 2.88 MB.

  3. Output at a 60° Sun angle?

    Show answer

    11.4 W.

  4. Why limit depth of discharge?

    Show answer

    More cycles before the battery wears out.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Sun power is constant around the orbit.

    Actually: Eclipse and pointing cut it.

  • Myth: Battery = eclipse energy.

    Actually: Divide by DoD.

  • Myth: More bits per second is free.

    Actually: It needs more link margin.

Flashcards

  • Orbit-average power

    Generation over a full orbit.

  • DoD

    Depth of discharge.

  • Path loss

    Spreading loss.

  • UHF

    Ultra-high-frequency band.

  • Pass

    Time a ground station sees the satellite.

  • Data volume

    Bits downlinked per day.

How this helps save the planet

NASA’s small spacecraft report says that when its first edition was published in 2013, 247 CubeSats and 105 other small spacecraft under 50 kg had been launched worldwide, less than 2% of the mass launched to orbit.

Unit 7 · Week 8

Structural and Thermal Checks

Load factors, margins of safety, launch and flight loads, and a first thermal equilibrium estimate for both tracks.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute a design load from a load factor.
  • Compute a margin of safety.
  • Estimate equilibrium temperature.
  • Decide what needs detailed analysis.

Key concepts

  • Design loads are weight times a load factor (launch or maneuver), times a factor of safety.
  • Margin of safety MS = allowable/(FS × applied) − 1 must be positive.
  • Aluminum 6061-T6 yields near 276 MPa (textbook value), a common choice for both drones and CubeSat frames.
  • A first thermal estimate balances absorbed sunlight with emitted infrared; for α = ε a sunlit body near Earth sits near 278 K on average.
  • Hand checks find the critical cases; finite-element and thermal models then refine them.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 7.1 A 4 kg CubeSat sees 10 g (sample load factor). Load?

Show the steps
  1. 10 × 4 × 9.80665.

Answer: 392 N.

Example 7.2 A rail sees 80 MPa; yield is 276 MPa; FS = 1.25. MS?

Show the steps
  1. 276/(1.25 × 80) − 1.

Answer: 1.76, positive.

Example 7.3 Average equilibrium temperature with α = ε?

Show the steps
  1. (1361/(4 × 5.670374419 × 10⁻⁸))^¼.

Answer: 278 K.

Unit 8 · Weeks 9–10

Cost, Schedule and Risk

Work breakdown structures, critical-path schedules, earned value, risk matrices and mitigation plans.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Build a work breakdown structure.
  • Find the critical path.
  • Compute CPI, SPI and estimate at completion.
  • Score and mitigate risks.

Key concepts

  • A work breakdown structure splits the project into deliverable pieces that can be estimated and assigned.
  • The critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks; any delay on it delays the project.
  • Earned value compares planned value, earned value and actual cost.
  • CPI = EV/AC and SPI = EV/PV; values under 1 mean over budget or behind schedule.
  • Risks are scored likelihood × consequence and tracked with owners and mitigations.

Unit 9 · Weeks 11–13

Prototype, Test and Verification

Verification matrices, test planning, measurement statistics, comparing results with predictions, and closing requirements.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Build a verification matrix.
  • Compute mean and standard deviation.
  • Compare a measurement with an allocation.
  • Close or waive a requirement.

Key concepts

  • A verification matrix lists each requirement, its method, the test or analysis, and the result.
  • Repeat measurements to estimate the mean and spread; report both.
  • Compare results with predictions; large differences reveal model errors early.
  • Test as you fly: use flight-like configurations and procedures.
  • MIT’s 16.83X follows conceive, design, implement and operate phases, ending with testing in the intended environment.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 9.1 Five mass measurements: 3.38, 3.42, 3.40, 3.45, 3.35 kg. Mean?

Show the steps
  1. Sum 17.00/5.

Answer: 3.40 kg.

Example 9.2 Sample standard deviation?

Show the steps
  1. Deviations −0.02, 0.02, 0, 0.05, −0.05.
  2. √(0.0058/4).

Answer: 0.038 kg.

Example 9.3 Margin against the 4.0 kg allocation?

Show the steps
  1. (4.0 − 3.40)/3.40.
