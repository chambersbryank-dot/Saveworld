About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a one-semester junior engineering thermodynamics course. It is modeled on the topics and depth of MIT 16.050 Thermal Energy and the thermodynamics parts of MIT Unified Engineering (both free on MIT OpenCourseWare), and on courses such as Purdue ME 200 and Georgia Tech ME 3322, using the free OpenStax University Physics Volume 2 chapters on heat and the laws of thermodynamics and NIST’s free fluid property data, which we link where they help. None of those authors or schools endorses this page.

What we add: every unit ties to real work with a cited public source (power-plant efficiency, nuclear and concentrating solar plants, heat pumps, combined heat and power and geothermal energy), every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab has a free simulation or a safe home version.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Almost all electricity from fuel, uranium, concentrated sunlight or Earth’s heat comes from a thermodynamic cycle, and almost all heating and cooling runs on one too. The first law says energy is conserved; the second law says how much of it can become useful work. Between them they decide how much fuel the world burns.

Better cycles, recovered waste heat and heat pumps are some of the fastest ways to cut emissions. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Calculus II and III: integrals, partial derivatives and natural logarithms.
  • Physics I and General Chemistry I: energy, work, ideal gases and heat.
  • Differential Equations is helpful but not required.
  • Comfort with spreadsheets for tables and interpolation.

How to study

  • Start every problem by drawing the system or control volume and saying whether it is closed or open, steady or not.
  • List the known states and look up properties before writing any balance.
  • Write the energy balance in words first (energy in − energy out = change in stored energy), then cancel terms you can justify.
  • Keep units consistent: kJ, kPa, m³, kg and kelvin; never use °C inside a ratio of temperatures.
  • Sketch every process on a T–s or p–v diagram; cycles become much easier to follow.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer, and retake the quizzes a few days later.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer or tablet for the free PhET Gas Properties, Energy Forms and Changes and States of Matter simulations.
  • A kitchen thermometer (or a cheap digital probe), a measuring cup and a kitchen scale.
  • An electric kettle with a known power rating, ice cubes, an insulated mug or foam cup.
  • A phone stopwatch.
  • Spreadsheet software for the cycle labs.

Unit 1 · Week 1

Systems, Properties, Energy and Efficiency

Closed systems, control volumes and surroundings, properties and states, equilibrium and quasi-equilibrium processes, temperature scales and the zeroth law, pressure, forms of energy, energy units, and efficiency as useful output over required input.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Choose a system or control volume and its boundary.
  • Tell intensive from extensive properties, and state from path.
  • Convert between kJ, kWh and Btu, and between °C and K.
  • Compute an efficiency and a power plant’s efficiency from its heat rate.

Key concepts

  • A system is the region we study; a closed system exchanges energy but not mass with its surroundings, while a control volume (open system) lets mass cross its boundary.
  • Properties such as pressure p, temperature T and specific volume v = V/m describe the state. Intensive properties (p, T, v) do not depend on size; extensive ones (V, m, E) do. Two independent intensive properties fix the state of a simple compressible substance.
  • The zeroth law: two bodies each in thermal equilibrium with a third are in equilibrium with each other; it is why thermometers work. Absolute temperature T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15 must be used in every ratio and every gas law.
  • Energy is stored as internal energy U, kinetic energy ½mV² and potential energy mgz, and it crosses a boundary only as heat Q or work W.
  • Efficiency = useful output ÷ required input. For power plants, the U.S. EIA uses heat rate: Btu of fuel per kWh of electricity, with 1 kWh = 3412 Btu.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 EIA gives heat rates of 10,500 Btu/kWh and 7,500 Btu/kWh as examples. Find each efficiency.

Show the steps
  1. η = 3412/10,500.
  2. η = 3412/7500.

Answer: 32.5% and 45.5% (EIA rounds these to 33% and 45%).

Example 1.2 A tank holds 2 kg of gas in 0.5 m³. Find v and ρ.

Show the steps
  1. v = 0.5/2.
  2. ρ = 1/v.

Answer: v = 0.25 m³/kg, ρ = 4 kg/m³.

Example 1.3 A 1000 kg car at 30 m/s brakes to a stop. How much kinetic energy becomes internal energy of the brakes, in kJ and kWh?

Show the steps
  1. KE = ½ × 1000 × 30² = 450,000 J.
  2. 450 kJ ÷ 3600 kJ/kWh.

Answer: 450 kJ = 0.125 kWh.

  • Myth: Heat and temperature are the same thing.

    Actually: Temperature is a property; heat is energy in transit.

  • Myth: A system “contains heat.”

    Actually: It contains internal energy; heat exists only crossing a boundary.

  • Myth: Ratios can use °C.

    Actually: Gas laws and efficiencies need kelvin.

How this helps save the planet

The U.S. Energy Information Administration measures power-plant efficiency with the heat rate: the fuel energy a plant uses to deliver one kilowatthour to the grid, counting the electricity the plant itself uses for fuel feeding, boiler pumps, cooling and pollution control. Dividing 3,412 Btu (the energy in one kWh) by the heat rate gives efficiency; EIA’s examples are a 10,500 Btu plant at about 33% and a 7,500 Btu plant at about 45%.

That gap is the whole point of this course. For the same electricity, the 7,500 Btu plant burns 7,500/10,500 = 71.4% as much fuel, so about 28.6% less fuel and CO₂. The laws of thermodynamics explain where the rest of the energy goes and how engineers claw some of it back.

Unit 2 · Weeks 2–3

Pure Substances, Phase Change and Property Tables

Phases and phase change, saturation temperature and pressure, compressed liquid, saturated mixture and superheated vapor, quality, T–v and p–v diagrams, steam tables and interpolation, the ideal-gas equation and when it applies, and the compressibility factor.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Identify the phase from p and T using saturation data.
  • Use quality to find v, u and h of a saturated mixture.
  • Interpolate in property tables.
  • Use the ideal-gas law and know when it fails.

Key concepts

  • A pure substance has a fixed chemical makeup. At a given pressure, water boils at one saturation temperature (99.61 °C at 100 kPa); while it boils, p and T stay fixed and the mixture’s quality x = m_vapor/m_total grows from 0 to 1.
  • For a saturated mixture any specific property is y = y_f + x(y_g − y_f) = y_f + x·y_fg, using saturated-liquid (f) and saturated-vapor (g) values from the tables.
  • If T is below the saturation temperature at that pressure, the water is a compressed liquid (approximately saturated liquid at T); if above, it is superheated vapor, found in separate tables.
  • Linear interpolation: y = y₁ + (x − x₁)(y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁). NIST’s free WebBook gives the same data for water and many refrigerants.
  • At low pressure and high temperature relative to the critical point, gases obey pv = RT with R = R_u/M; for air R = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K). Near saturation (steam in a boiler) use the tables instead.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 At 100 kPa, h_f = 417.51 and h_fg = 2257.5 kJ/kg, v_f = 0.001043 and v_g = 1.6941 m³/kg (steam tables). Find h and v at quality 0.6.

Show the steps
  1. h = 417.51 + 0.6 × 2257.5.
  2. v = 0.001043 + 0.6 × (1.6941 − 0.001043).

Answer: h = 1772.0 kJ/kg; v = 1.0169 m³/kg.

Example 2.2 Find the mass of air in a 0.5 m³ tank at 100 kPa and 300 K.

Show the steps
  1. m = pV/(RT) = 100 × 0.5/(0.287 × 300).

Answer: m = 0.581 kg.

Example 2.3 Find the pressure of 2 kg of air in 0.5 m³ at 400 K.

Show the steps
  1. p = mRT/V = 2 × 0.287 × 400/0.5.

Answer: p = 459.2 kPa.

  • Myth: Steam in a boiler is an ideal gas.

    Actually: Near saturation it is not; use steam tables.

  • Myth: Quality applies to superheated vapor.

    Actually: Only to saturated mixtures, 0 ≤ x ≤ 1.

How this helps save the planet

The U.S. Department of Energy explains that nuclear reactors use fission heat to make steam that spins a turbine. All U.S. commercial reactors are light-water reactors: more than 65% are pressurized-water reactors, which keep core water under high pressure so it does not boil and make steam in a separate heat exchanger, and roughly a third are boiling-water reactors, which make steam directly in the vessel.

Both designs are exercises in this unit: raising the pressure raises the saturation temperature so water stays liquid, and the steam’s quality and enthalpy set how much work the turbine gets. DOE counts more than 400 commercial reactors worldwide, including 94 in the United States, all built on steam tables.

Unit 3 · Week 4

Energy Transfer by Work and Heat

Sign conventions, boundary work and its area on a p–V diagram, constant-pressure, isothermal and polytropic processes, shaft and electrical work, and the three modes of heat transfer: conduction, convection and radiation.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute boundary work for common processes.
  • Show that work and heat are path functions.
  • Compute shaft and electrical work and power.
  • Estimate conduction and convection heat rates.

Key concepts

  • Sign convention: heat into the system and work done by the system are positive (Q_in, W_out). Both are path functions: they depend on the process, not just the end states.
  • Boundary work W_b = ∫p dV is the area under the process curve on a p–V diagram. At constant pressure W_b = p(V₂ − V₁).
  • For an ideal gas at constant temperature, W_b = mRT ln(V₂/V₁). For a polytropic process pVⁿ = constant (n ≠ 1), W_b = (p₂V₂ − p₁V₁)/(1 − n).
  • Shaft work: power = torque × angular speed, Ẇ = 2πṅT. Electrical work: W = VIΔt = PΔt.
  • Heat moves by conduction (Q̇ = kAΔT/L through a wall), convection (Q̇ = hAΔT to a moving fluid) and radiation (Q̇ = εσA(T_s⁴ − T_surr⁴)). Insulation lowers k; this is why building envelopes matter.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 1 kg of air expands isothermally at 300 K to twice its volume. Find the work.

Show the steps
  1. W = 1 × 0.287 × 300 × ln 2.

Answer: W = 59.7 kJ (done by the gas).

Example 3.2 Air is compressed polytropically (n = 1.3) from 100 kPa and 0.2 m³ to 0.1 m³. Find p₂ and the work.

Show the steps
  1. p₂ = 100 × 2^1.3 = 246.2 kPa.
  2. W = (246.2 × 0.1 − 100 × 0.2)/(1 − 1.3).

Answer: p₂ = 246.2 kPa; W = −15.4 kJ (work done on the gas).

Example 3.3 A wall of area 10 m², thickness 0.1 m and k = 0.04 W/(m·K) (sample insulation) has 20 K across it. Find the heat loss.

Show the steps
  1. Q̇ = 0.04 × 10 × 20/0.1.

Answer: Q̇ = 80 W.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

    Show answer

    200 × 0.3 = 60 kJ.

  2. A shaft delivers 50 N·m at 3000 rpm. Power?

    Show answer

  3. A 1500 W heater runs 10 minutes. Electrical work?

    Show answer

  4. Convection: h = 10 W/(m²·K), A = 2 m², ΔT = 30 K. Heat rate?

    Show answer

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Work is a property of the state.

    Actually: It depends on the path.

  • Myth: A rigid tank can do boundary work.

    Actually: No; dV = 0.

  • Myth: Doubling insulation thickness halves the temperature.

    Actually: It halves the conduction heat rate at the same ΔT.

How this helps save the planet

The U.S. Department of Energy explains that concentrating solar-thermal power uses mirrors to concentrate sunlight onto a receiver, heating a high-temperature fluid. That heat spins a turbine or powers an engine to make electricity, and it can also serve industry, from desalination to food processing and chemical production.

DOE notes that utility-scale plants use power towers or rows of linear mirrors, while single dish/engine systems produce 5 to 25 kilowatts each. Every one of them is a heat-transfer and work problem: absorb concentrated radiation, limit conduction and convection losses from the receiver, and turn the heat into shaft work.

Unit 4 · Week 5

The First Law for Closed Systems

Energy balance for closed systems, internal energy and enthalpy, specific heats of ideal gases, liquids and solids, constant-volume and constant-pressure heating, latent heat, and energy balances for cycles.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Write and apply Q − W = ΔU + ΔKE + ΔPE.
  • Use c_v and c_p for ideal gases, and c for liquids and solids.
  • Include latent heat in phase-change problems.
  • Show that for a cycle the net heat equals the net work.

Key concepts

  • First law for a closed system: Q − W = ΔE = ΔU + ΔKE + ΔPE. Usually ΔKE and ΔPE are negligible, so Q − W = ΔU.
  • Enthalpy h = u + pv. At constant pressure, the heat added equals the enthalpy change: Q = m(h₂ − h₁), because the boundary work is already included.
  • For ideal gases u and h depend only on T: Δu = c_vΔT, Δh = c_pΔT, with c_p − c_v = R and k = c_p/c_v (1.4 for air).
  • For liquids and solids c_p ≈ c_v = c, so Q = mcΔT. Melting or boiling at constant T needs latent heat: Q = m h_if or m h_fg.
  • Over a full cycle the system returns to its starting state, so ΔU = 0 and Q_net = W_net. This is the basis of every engine and refrigerator.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 2 kg of air in a rigid tank is heated from 300 K to 400 K. Find Q.

Show the steps
  1. W = 0 (rigid).
  2. Q = m c_v ΔT = 2 × 0.718 × 100.

Answer: Q = 143.6 kJ.

Example 4.2 The same air is heated at constant pressure (100 kPa) from 300 to 400 K. Find Q and the boundary work.

Show the steps
  1. Q = m c_p ΔT = 2 × 1.005 × 100.
  2. W = mRΔT = 2 × 0.287 × 100.

Answer: Q = 201.0 kJ; W = 57.4 kJ; ΔU = 143.6 kJ either way.

Example 4.3 Find the heat to warm 1 kg of water from 20 °C to 100 °C, and then to boil 0.1 kg of it away.

Show the steps
  1. Q₁ = 1 × 4.18 × 80.
  2. Q₂ = 0.1 × 2257.

Answer: Q₁ = 334.4 kJ; Q₂ = 225.7 kJ.

  • Myth: Enthalpy is a form of heat.

    Actually: It is a property, u + pv.

How this helps save the planet

DOE reports that heat pumps are growing fast: as of 2020 over 17 million U.S. housing units had them, and they now account for more than half of all residential heating equipment sales in the United States. They work like a refrigerator, using electricity to move heat from a cool space to a warm one rather than making heat.

The first law is why that matters: an electric resistance heater turns each kWh of electricity into exactly one kWh of heat, but a heat pump delivers the electricity plus the heat it pulls from outside. DOE says modern air-source heat pumps can cut electricity use by 50% compared with furnaces and baseboard heaters, and that switching could save up to 7.6 tons of carbon emissions a year depending on the equipment replaced.

The Second Law: Heat Engines, Refrigerators and Carnot Limits

Thermal reservoirs, heat engines and thermal efficiency, refrigerators, heat pumps and COP, the Kelvin–Planck and Clausius statements, reversible and irreversible processes, the Carnot cycle, and the Carnot limits on efficiency and COP.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute thermal efficiency and COP.
  • State the Kelvin–Planck and Clausius statements.
  • Identify sources of irreversibility.
  • Use Carnot limits to judge whether a claimed device is possible.

Key concepts

  • A heat engine takes Q_H from a hot reservoir, rejects Q_L to a cold one, and produces W = Q_H − Q_L. Thermal efficiency η = W/Q_H = 1 − Q_L/Q_H.
  • Kelvin–Planck: no cycle can turn all the heat from a single reservoir into work, so η < 100%. Clausius: heat does not flow from cold to hot by itself; a refrigerator or heat pump needs work input.
  • COP_R = Q_L/W for a refrigerator; COP_HP = Q_H/W = COP_R + 1 for a heat pump. COP can be well above 1, which is why heat pumps beat resistance heaters.
  • Irreversibilities (friction, heat transfer across a finite ΔT, unrestrained expansion, mixing) reduce performance. A reversible cycle between two reservoirs, the Carnot cycle, is the best possible.
  • Carnot limits use absolute temperatures: η_Carnot = 1 − T_L/T_H, COP_R,Carnot = T_L/(T_H − T_L), COP_HP,Carnot = T_H/(T_H − T_L).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

  2. Q_L = 1000 − 350.

Answer: η = 35%; Q_L = 650 kJ.

Example 6.2 Find the Carnot efficiency between 800 K and 300 K, and between 1073 K and 293 K.

Show the steps
  1. 1 − 300/800.
  2. 1 − 293/1073.

Answer: 62.5% and 72.7%.

Example 6.3 A heat pump keeps a house at 294 K when it is 273 K outside. Find the Carnot COP, and the heat delivered by a real unit with COP = 3 (sample value) using 3 kWh.

Show the steps
  1. COP = 294/(294 − 273).
  2. Q_H = 3 × 3.

Answer: COP_Carnot = 14.0; the real unit delivers 9 kWh of heat.

Entropy and Isentropic Efficiency

The Clausius inequality, entropy as a property, the increase-of-entropy principle and entropy generation, the T ds relations, entropy change of liquids, solids and ideal gases, isentropic processes, T–s diagrams, and isentropic efficiencies of turbines, compressors and nozzles.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute entropy changes for liquids, solids and ideal gases.
  • Find entropy generation and decide if a process is possible.
  • Use isentropic relations for ideal gases.
  • Apply isentropic efficiencies to real devices.

Key concepts

  • Entropy S is a property defined by dS = δQ_rev/T. For any process, S₂ − S₁ ≥ ∫δQ/T; the difference is the entropy generated, S_gen ≥ 0 (zero only if reversible).
  • Increase-of-entropy principle: the entropy of an isolated system (system plus surroundings) never decreases. A process with S_gen < 0 is impossible.
  • T ds relations: T ds = du + p dv = dh − v dp. For liquids and solids Δs = c ln(T₂/T₁); for ideal gases Δs = c_p ln(T₂/T₁) − R ln(p₂/p₁).
  • An isentropic (reversible adiabatic) ideal-gas process with constant specific heats has T₂/T₁ = (p₂/p₁)^((k−1)/k). On a T–s diagram it is a vertical line, and the area under a reversible process is the heat transferred.
  • Real devices are compared with isentropic ones: η_T = (h₁ − h₂a)/(h₁ − h₂s) for turbines and η_C = (h₂s − h₁)/(h₂a − h₁) for compressors.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Show the steps

Answer: ΔS = 1.557 kJ/K.

Example 7.2 Air goes from 300 K and 100 kPa to 500 K and 400 kPa. Find Δs.

Show the steps
  1. Δs = 1.005 ln(500/300) − 0.287 ln(400/100).

Answer: Δs = 0.1155 kJ/(kg·K).

Example 7.3 Air at 300 K is compressed through a pressure ratio of 8. Find the isentropic exit temperature and the actual one if η_C = 0.85.

Show the steps
  1. T₂s = 300 × 8^(0.4/1.4) = 543.4 K.
  2. T₂a = 300 + (543.4 − 300)/0.85.

Answer: T₂s = 543.4 K; T₂a = 586.4 K.

Exergy: How Much Energy Is Useful

Dead state and surroundings, exergy (availability) of heat and of flowing streams, reversible work and irreversibility, exergy destruction I = T₀S_gen, second-law efficiency, and why matching energy quality to use matters.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute the exergy of heat from a reservoir.
  • Relate exergy destroyed to entropy generated.
  • Compute second-law efficiency.
  • Explain why combined heat and power and heat pumps use exergy well.

Key concepts

  • Exergy is the maximum useful work obtainable as a system comes to equilibrium with its surroundings (the dead state, T₀ and p₀). Energy is conserved; exergy is destroyed by irreversibility.
  • Heat Q from a reservoir at T has exergy X = Q(1 − T₀/T): heat at high temperature is valuable, heat near room temperature is almost worthless for work.
  • Exergy of a flowing stream per unit mass: ψ = (h − h₀) − T₀(s − s₀) + V²/2 + gz.
  • Exergy destroyed (irreversibility) I = T₀S_gen (Gouy–Stodola). Every irreversibility costs useful work.
  • Second-law efficiency η_II = η_th/η_rev for engines, or COP/COP_rev for heat pumps. Burning fuel at 2000 K just to heat water to 60 °C wastes most of the exergy; using the waste heat of a power plant for that job does not.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Show the steps
  1. X = 100 × (1 − 300/600).

Answer: 50 kJ.

Example 8.2 1000 kJ flows from 800 K to 300 K (T₀ = 300 K). Find the exergy destroyed.

Show the steps
  1. S_gen = 1000/300 − 1000/800 = 2.083 kJ/K.
  2. I = 300 × 2.083.

Answer: I = 625 kJ, all the exergy the heat had at 800 K.

Example 8.3 An engine with η = 35% runs between 800 K and 300 K. Find η_II.

Show the steps
  1. η_rev = 62.5%.
  2. η_II = 0.35/0.625.

Answer: η_II = 56%.

Gas Power Cycles: Otto, Diesel and Brayton

Air-standard assumptions, mean effective pressure, the Otto cycle (spark ignition), the Diesel cycle (compression ignition), the Brayton cycle for gas turbines and jet engines, back-work ratio, regeneration, intercooling and reheat, and combined cycles.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Analyze ideal Otto and Diesel cycles.
  • Analyze the ideal Brayton cycle, including back-work ratio.
  • Explain how compression ratio and pressure ratio raise efficiency.
  • Describe regeneration and combined cycles.

Key concepts

  • Air-standard assumptions: the working fluid is air, an ideal gas, in a closed cycle; combustion is replaced by heat addition and exhaust by heat rejection. Cold-air-standard adds constant specific heats at room temperature.
  • Otto cycle: isentropic compression, constant-volume heat addition, isentropic expansion, constant-volume heat rejection. η_Otto = 1 − 1/r^(k−1), with compression ratio r = V_max/V_min.
  • Diesel cycle: heat is added at constant pressure; with cutoff ratio r_c, η_Diesel = 1 − (1/r^(k−1))·(r_c^k − 1)/(k(r_c − 1)). Diesels run at higher r, so they are more efficient in practice.
  • Brayton cycle (gas turbines): isentropic compression, constant-pressure heat addition, isentropic expansion, constant-pressure heat rejection. η_Brayton = 1 − 1/r_p^((k−1)/k). A large share of turbine work drives the compressor (the back-work ratio).
  • Regeneration uses hot exhaust to preheat compressed air; intercooling and reheat move the cycle toward Carnot. A combined cycle feeds gas-turbine exhaust to a steam (Rankine) cycle.

Key concepts

  • Ideal Rankine cycle: pump (isentropic compression of liquid), boiler (constant-pressure heating to superheated steam), turbine (isentropic expansion), condenser (constant-pressure condensation).
  • Pump work is small because liquid has small v: w_p ≈ v(p₂ − p₁). Net work = w_turb − w_pump; η = w_net/q_in.
  • Efficiency rises with higher average heat-addition temperature (higher boiler pressure, more superheat, reheat) and lower condenser pressure. Regeneration with feedwater heaters preheats the water using steam bled from the turbine.
  • Vapor-compression refrigeration: compressor (to superheated vapor), condenser (rejects Q_H), expansion valve (throttle, h₃ = h₄), evaporator (absorbs Q_L). COP_R = (h₁ − h₄)/(h₂ − h₁).
  • A heat pump is the same cycle used for heating, with COP_HP = (h₂ − h₃)/(h₂ − h₁). Refrigerants are chosen for their saturation properties, safety and low global-warming potential.

Worked examples, step by step

Example 10.1 An ideal Rankine cycle runs between 3 MPa/350 °C and 75 kPa. Steam tables: h₁ = 384.44, v₁ = 0.001037 m³/kg, h₃ = 3116.1, h₄ = 2403.0 kJ/kg. Find w_p, q_in, w_net and η.

Show the steps
  1. w_p = 0.001037 × (3000 − 75) = 3.03 kJ/kg; h₂ = 387.47.
  2. q_in = 3116.1 − 387.47 = 2728.6 kJ/kg.
  3. w_net = (3116.1 − 2403.0) − 3.03 = 710.1 kJ/kg; η = 710.1/2728.6.

Answer: w_p = 3.03 kJ/kg, q_in = 2728.6 kJ/kg, w_net = 710.1 kJ/kg, η = 26.0%.

Example 10.2 A vapor-compression cycle has h₁ = 244.5, h₂ = 275.4 and h₃ = h₄ = 95.5 kJ/kg (sample values). Find q_L, w_in and COP_R.

Show the steps
  1. q_L = 244.5 − 95.5 = 149.0 kJ/kg.
  2. w = 275.4 − 244.5 = 30.9 kJ/kg.
  3. COP = 149.0/30.9.

Answer: q_L = 149.0 kJ/kg, w = 30.9 kJ/kg, COP_R = 4.82.

Example 10.3 For that cycle used as a heat pump, find COP_HP.

Show the steps
  1. q_H = 275.4 − 95.5 = 179.9 kJ/kg.
  2. COP_HP = 179.9/30.9.

Answer: COP_HP = 5.82 (= COP_R + 1).

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Pump work in a Rankine cycle is large.

    Actually: It is tiny because liquid is nearly incompressible.

  • Myth: The expansion valve does work.

    Actually: It is a throttle: h constant, no work.

  • Myth: More superheat always helps with no limit.

    Actually: Turbine materials limit temperature.

How this helps save the planet

DOE’s geothermal office explains that geothermal plants bring hot fluid up through wells and convert its heat to steam that drives turbines, a Rankine-family cycle with Earth as the boiler. Geothermal plants can run essentially 24 hours a day regardless of weather, and DOE’s analysis sees potential for 90 gigawatts of U.S. geothermal electricity by 2050 if enhanced geothermal costs fall.

Nuclear plants use the same Rankine cycle, and DOE’s heat pump article describes the vapor-compression cycle in reverse: moving heat into homes in winter and out in summer. Better Rankine and refrigeration cycles are where much of the world’s clean electricity and efficient heating will come from.
