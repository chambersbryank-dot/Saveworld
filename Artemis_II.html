Artemis II

Four astronauts, one loop around the Moon, and a new record for how far humans have traveled.

Image: NASA, Artemis II launch.

What you see: SI-generated visuals: the words 252,756 miles over stars, Earth through a spacecraft window, a spacecraft passing Earth, then the savingplanets.com end card.

What you hear:

This is the farthest any human has ever been from home.

On April 6, 2026, NASA's Artemis II crew reached 252,756 [unclear] Moon.

Every voyage outward teaches us how precious home is.

Last reviewed: October 2026 · 11 sources cited

Spotlight: Artemis III

NASA target: 2027 · Crewed demonstration in low Earth orbit

Artemis III will carry out a series of objectives in low Earth The curved path an object takes as it travels around a planet, moon or star, like a satellite circling Earth. Glossary designed to demonstrate critical systems needed for future lunar landings, beginning with Artemis IV. NASA's SLS rocket will launch four crew members aboard Orion from Kennedy Space Center in Florida.

  • Mission type: crewed demonstration; launch 2027 (NASA target, as of October 2026); crew of 4.
  • Objective: demonstrate critical systems for lunar landing.
  • Highlight: NASA will test one or both commercial human landing systems in Earth orbit, demonstrating rendezvous and docking with Orion.
  • Also planned: integrated checks of life support, communications and propulsion, and tests of the new xEVA spacesuits.
  • Crew: Randy Bresnik (NASA, commander), Luca Parmitano (ESA, pilot), Andre Douglas and Frank Rubio (NASA, mission specialists).

Source: NASA, Artemis III mission

Source: NASA, Artemis architecture update

Source: NASA, Artemis III crew

Artemis II: Mission Status

Flown. Four astronauts flew around the Moon, reached 252,756 miles from Earth on April 6, 2026, and splashed down off San Diego after a nearly 10-day, 695,081-mile journey.

Source: NASA, Artemis II crew back on Earth

Artemis III: Mission Status

NASA target: 2027. A crewed test flight in low Earth orbit: Orion will practice rendezvous and docking with one or both commercial lunar landers and test critical systems before any landing. No launch date set yet, so no countdown.

Source: NASA, Artemis III mission

Artemis IV: Mission Status

NASA target: early 2028. Humanity's return to the lunar surface: two of four crew will descend near the Moon's South Pole and spend about a week there. Crew not yet announced.

Source: NASA, Artemis IV

Artemis I · Flown

Goal: Test the rocket, spacecraft and ground systems without a crew.

Crew: None (uncrewed).

Hardware: SLS rocket and Orion spacecraft (uncrewed).

Source: NASA, Artemis I

Artemis II · Flown

Goal: Prove Orion and SLS with a crew aboard.

Crew: Reid Wiseman (NASA, Commander), Victor Glover (NASA, Pilot), Christina Koch (NASA, Mission Specialist), Jeremy Hansen (CSA, Mission Specialist)

Hardware: SLS rocket and Orion spacecraft.

Source: NASA, Artemis II crew back on Earth

Source: NASA, Artemis II distance record

Artemis III · NASA Target: 2027

Goal: Demonstrate Critical Systems for Lunar Landing

Crew: Randy Bresnik (NASA, Commander), Luca Parmitano (ESA, Pilot), Andre Douglas (NASA, Mission Specialist), Frank Rubio (NASA, Mission Specialist)

Hardware: SLS, Orion, and one or both human landing systems: SpaceX Starship and Blue Origin Blue Moon (company-built, NASA contracts).

Source: NASA, Artemis III mission

Source: NASA, Artemis III crew

Source: NASA, Artemis architecture update

Artemis IV · NASA Target: Early 2028

Goal: Land two astronauts near the lunar South Pole for about a week of science.

Crew: Not yet announced.

Hardware: SLS (standardized configuration from Artemis IV), Orion docking with a commercial human landing system in lunar orbit. Gateway is not listed in NASA's February 2026 plan update.

Source: NASA, Artemis IV

Source: NASA, Return to the lunar surface plan (Mar 2026)

Artemis V · NASA Target: Late 2028

Goal: An additional astronaut landing on the Moon.

Crew: Not yet announced.

Hardware: SLS, Orion and a commercial human landing system (per NASA's plan).

Source: NASA, Return to the lunar surface plan (Mar 2026)

💡 Explain Like I'm 10

  • What is it? A series of crewed missions on SLS and Orion: a lunar flyby (Artemis II, flown), an Earth-orbit lander test (Artemis III) and a return to the lunar surface (Artemis IV).
  • Why does it matter? To explore more of the Moon for scientific discovery and economic benefit, and to build a foundation for the first crewed missions to Mars.

The Five W's of the Artemis Program

Who

NASA leads, with partners ESA, CSA and JAXA. CSA's Jeremy Hansen flew on Artemis II and ESA's Luca Parmitano is Artemis III pilot. Landers are built by SpaceX (Starship) and Blue Origin (Blue Moon), companies under NASA contracts.

Source: NASA, Artemis program

Source: NASA, Artemis III crew

What

A series of crewed missions on SLS and Orion: a lunar flyby (Artemis II, flown), an Earth-orbit lander test (Artemis III) and a return to the lunar surface (Artemis IV).

Source: NASA, Artemis program

When

Artemis II flew April 1–10, 2026. NASA targets: Artemis III in 2027 and Artemis IV in early 2028, as of October 11, 2026.

Source: NASA, Artemis III mission

Source: NASA, Artemis IV

Where

Launches from Kennedy Space Center in Florida; Artemis III stays in low Earth orbit, Artemis IV lands near the Moon's South Pole.

Source: NASA, Artemis III mission

Source: NASA, Artemis IV

Why

To explore more of the Moon for scientific discovery and economic benefit, and to build a foundation for the first crewed missions to Mars.

Source: NASA, Artemis program

Impact Statement

Living tracker · Artemis II flown; Artemis III and IV ahead

This page gives anyone, anywhere in the world, one living place to follow the Artemis program: NASA's current target dates, who is flying, what each mission must prove, and the hardware carrying them. Every figure links back to NASA, and the schedule refreshes from a single data file as NASA updates its plans.

It also keeps the bigger story in view. Artemis II carried four people 252,756 miles from Earth, farther than humans had ever gone, and brought them home. Following each step outward is a way to see how small and precious home is, and why the work on Earth matters.

Source: NASA, Artemis program
