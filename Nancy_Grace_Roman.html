Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope (NASA)

NASA's Roman telescope launched Aug 30, 2026 to L2: Hubble-sharp images 100x wider, a census of planetary systems, first images by early 2027.

Image: NASA/Sydney Rohde. Roman lifts off on a Falcon Heavy, Aug 30, 2026.

Roman: Mission Status

Launched August 30, 2026 on a Falcon Heavy; on a three-month, million-mile trip to Sun-Earth L2 while it is commissioned. NASA expects the first images by early 2027. Dates per NASA; missions shift.

Meet Roman, Hubble's Wide-Eyed Cousin

Roman has a mirror the same size as Hubble's and images just as sharp, but each picture covers a patch of sky at least 100 times larger. NASA says one Roman image holds the detail of 100 Hubble pictures, and Roman can survey the sky about a thousand times faster than Hubble.

Source: NASA, Roman mission page; NASA, Roman launch release (Aug 30, 2026)

What Is a Lagrange Point?

A Lagrange point is a spot in space where the pull of two big bodies, like the Sun and Earth, balances out so a spacecraft can stay put with very little fuel. There are five: L1, L2 and L3 sit on the Sun-Earth line, and L4 and L5 lead and trail Earth in its orbit.

  • Why L2? It is close enough to talk to Earth easily, and it keeps the Sun, Earth and Moon behind the telescope, so it gets solar power and a clear, cold view of deep space.
  • Webb lives there too. Webb orbits L2 in a loop about the size of the Moon's orbit around Earth, and takes about 6 months to go around once.
  • Fun fact: L1 and L2 are a little wobbly, so spacecraft there make small course corrections, roughly every 23 days.

Source: NASA, What is a Lagrange Point?; NASA, Webb's orbit; NASA, Roman launch release (Aug 30, 2026)

Who Was Nancy Grace Roman?

Born to Look Up

Born May 16, 1925 in Nashville, Tennessee. By 6th grade she had started an astronomy club with her friends.

NASA's First Chief of Astronomy

She joined NASA in 1959, six months after it formed, and became its first Chief of Astronomy and its first woman executive.

Mother of Hubble

She planned NASA's space astronomy program and fought to get Hubble approved by Congress. Her colleague Ed Weiler named her the "mother of Hubble."

In Her Own Words

"If I hadn't been stubborn, I would've been talked out of it years earlier." She died December 25, 2018.

How This Saves People and the Planet

For Nature: How Rare Is Earth?

Roman will complete a statistical census of planetary systems in our galaxy. Knowing how common, or how rare, worlds like ours are shows how precious Earth's oceans, forests and air really are.

For Life: Finding Other Earths

Roman's Coronagraph tests the technology that future missions like NASA's Habitable Worlds Observatory concept could use to photograph Earth-like planets and search for life.

For People: Everyone Can Help

Machine learning, AI and citizen scientists will help sift Roman's 1.4 terabytes a day, so anyone can take part in discovery.

For Future Missions: Lighter and On Budget

Roman's mirror weighs 80% less than Hubble's, and NASA delivered the telescope ahead of schedule and on budget, a sign of how far telescope technology has come.

Source: NASA, Roman mission page; NASA, Roman launch release (Aug 30, 2026); NASA, Roman FAQ
