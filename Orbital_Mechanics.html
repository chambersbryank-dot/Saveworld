About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a one-semester junior orbital mechanics course. It is modeled on the topics and depth of MIT 16.346 Astrodynamics and the orbital parts of MIT 16.07 Dynamics (free on MIT OpenCourseWare), and on courses such as Purdue AAE 340 and Georgia Tech AE 3330, using NASA’s free Basics of Space Flight and OpenStax University Physics, which we link where they help. None of those authors, schools or agencies endorses this page.

What we add: every unit ties to real work with a cited public source (Landsat, GOES weather satellites, NASA’s DART planetary-defense test and orbital-debris rules), every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab has a free simulation or a spreadsheet.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Orbits are how we watch our planet: Earth-observing satellites in low orbits map forests, crops and water, and geostationary weather satellites watch storms around the clock. Choosing those orbits is pure orbital mechanics.

Orbits are also a shared, limited resource. Debris left in space threatens the satellites we rely on, so responsible mission design now includes a plan to leave orbit. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample or textbook values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Dynamics: Newton’s laws, angular momentum and energy.
  • Calculus III and Differential Equations: vectors, gradients and ODEs.
  • Linear Algebra: vectors, rotations and coordinate frames.
  • Physics I: universal gravitation.

How to study

  • Start every problem with a sketch of the orbit: central body, periapsis, apoapsis and the spacecraft’s position.
  • Write the vis-viva equation first; it answers most speed questions.
  • Keep units in metres and seconds, and μ in m³/s²; convert to km only at the end.
  • Draw each maneuver as a velocity triangle before computing Δv.
  • Check limits: a circular orbit has e = 0; escape has zero total energy.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer, and retake the quizzes a few days later.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer or tablet for the free PhET Gravity and Orbits, Kepler’s Laws and My Solar System simulations.
  • NASA’s Eyes on the Solar System in a web browser.
  • Spreadsheet software for the transfer, rocket-equation and ground-track labs.
  • A calculator with trigonometric functions.

Unit 1 · Week 1

Gravity, the Two-Body Problem and Circular Orbits

Newton’s law of gravitation, the gravitational parameter μ, the two-body equation of motion, circular orbit speed and period, escape speed, Kepler’s three laws, and low, medium and geostationary orbits.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Write the two-body equation of motion.
  • Compute circular orbit speed and period.
  • Compute escape speed.
  • Find the geostationary radius.

Key concepts

  • Newton’s gravity gives the two-body equation r̈ = −μr/r³, with μ = GM. For Earth μ = 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³/s².
  • A circular orbit has speed v = √(μ/r) and period T = 2π√(r³/μ): higher orbits are slower and take longer.
  • Escape speed is √2 times circular speed at the same radius: v_esc = √(2μ/r).
  • Kepler’s laws: orbits are ellipses with the central body at a focus; the radius sweeps equal areas in equal times; and T² is proportional to a³.
  • A geostationary orbit has a period of one sidereal day (86,164.1 s) and lies over the equator, so the satellite stays over the same spot.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 Find the speed and period of a circular orbit 400 km up.

Show the steps
  1. r = 6378 + 400 = 6778 km.
  2. v = √(3.986 × 10¹⁴/6.778 × 10⁶).
  3. T = 2π√(r³/μ).

Answer: v = 7.67 km/s; T = 92.6 min.

Example 1.2 Find escape speed from that altitude.

Show the steps
  1. v_esc = √2 × 7.669.

Answer: v_esc = 10.85 km/s.

Example 1.3 Find the geostationary radius and altitude.

Show the steps
  1. r = (μT²/(4π²))^(1/3) with T = 86,164.1 s.
  2. Altitude = 42,164 − 6378.

Answer: r = 42,164 km; altitude 35,786 km (22,236 miles); v = 3.07 km/s.

    Actually: They are slower.

  • Myth: Astronauts float because there is no gravity.

    Actually: They are in free fall around Earth.

  • Myth: A geostationary orbit can be over any city.

    Actually: It must be over the equator.

How this helps save the planet

NOAA explains that GOES weather satellites orbit 22,236 miles above Earth’s equator at speeds equal to Earth’s rotation, so they stay over the same regions and give continuous coverage. Together, GOES East and GOES West watch over more than half the globe, from the west coast of Africa to New Zealand.

The GOES-R series imager can image storms as often as every 30 seconds, and the series carries the first operational lightning mapper in geostationary orbit. The 22,236-mile altitude is exactly this unit’s geostationary radius minus Earth’s radius.

Unit 2 · Weeks 2–3

Conic Sections, Energy and Orbital Elements

Conservation of energy and angular momentum, the orbit equation, ellipses, parabolas and hyperbolas, eccentricity, the vis-viva equation, periapsis and apoapsis speeds, and the six classical orbital elements.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use vis-viva to find speeds.
  • Relate eccentricity to periapsis and apoapsis.
  • Compute specific energy and angular momentum.
  • Name and interpret the six orbital elements.

Key concepts

  • Specific energy ε = v²/2 − μ/r = −μ/(2a) is constant: negative for ellipses, zero for parabolas and positive for hyperbolas.
  • Specific angular momentum h = r × v is constant, so the orbit lies in a fixed plane.
  • The orbit equation r = (h²/μ)/(1 + e cos ν) gives the radius at true anomaly ν. Eccentricity e = (r_a − r_p)/(r_a + r_p) for an ellipse.
  • The vis-viva equation v² = μ(2/r − 1/a) gives the speed anywhere on any orbit.
  • Six classical elements fix an orbit: semi-major axis a, eccentricity e, inclination i, right ascension of the ascending node Ω, argument of periapsis ω and true anomaly ν.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 An orbit has perigee radius 6778 km and apogee radius 42,164 km. Find a and e.

Show the steps
  1. a = (6778 + 42,164)/2 = 24,471 km.
  2. e = (42,164 − 6778)/(42,164 + 6778).

Answer: a = 24,471 km; e = 0.723.

Example 2.2 Find its specific energy.

Show the steps
  1. ε = −3.986 × 10¹⁴/(2 × 2.4471 × 10⁷).

Answer: ε = −8.14 MJ/kg.

Example 2.3 Find the speeds at perigee and apogee.

Show the steps
  1. v_p = √(μ(2/r_p − 1/a)).
  2. v_a = √(μ(2/r_a − 1/a)).

Answer: v_p = 10.07 km/s; v_a = 1.62 km/s.

  2. What is e for a circle?

    Show answer

  4. Find the period of the orbit above.

    Show answer

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: The satellite moves at constant speed on an ellipse.

    Actually: Fastest at periapsis, slowest at apoapsis.

  • Myth: Inclination is measured from the North Pole.

    Actually: From the equator plane.

  • Myth: Energy depends on eccentricity.

    Actually: Only on a.

How this helps save the planet

NASA lists Landsat 9’s orbit as near-polar and sun-synchronous, at 705 km altitude and 98.2° inclination, with a period of 99 minutes, about 14.5 orbits a day, and a nominal equator crossing at 10:12 a.m. local time on the descending pass.

Those few numbers are orbital elements: a from the altitude, e near zero, i = 98.2°, and a node chosen to fix the local crossing time. The steady geometry is what lets Landsat compare images across more than five decades of its record.

Unit 3 · Week 4

Kepler’s Equation and Time of Flight

Mean motion, mean, eccentric and true anomaly, Kepler’s equation M = E − e sin E, solving it with Newton’s method, time of flight between two points, and position at a given time.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Relate mean, eccentric and true anomaly.
  • Solve Kepler’s equation iteratively.
  • Find the time to fly between two true anomalies.
  • Predict a satellite’s position at a later time.

Key concepts

  • Mean motion n = √(μ/a³) is the average angular rate. Mean anomaly M = n(t − t_p) grows uniformly with time.
  • Kepler’s equation links time to geometry: M = E − e sin E, where E is the eccentric anomaly.
  • True anomaly follows from tan(ν/2) = √((1 + e)/(1 − e)) tan(E/2).
  • Kepler’s equation has no closed-form solution for E; Newton’s method, E ← E − (E − e sin E − M)/(1 − e cos E), converges in a few steps.
  • Time of flight from ν₁ to ν₂: find E₁, E₂, then M₁, M₂, and divide the difference by n.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 For e = 0.3, find M when E = π/2.

Show the steps
  1. M = 1.5708 − 0.3 × 1.

Answer: M = 1.271 rad.

Example 3.2 For e = 0.3 and M = 1 rad, solve Kepler’s equation.

Show the steps
  1. Start E = 1; apply Newton’s method until it settles.

Answer: E = 1.288 rad.

Example 3.3 Find the true anomaly for that E.

Show the steps
  1. tan(ν/2) = √(1.3/0.7) × tan(0.644).

Answer: ν = 1.594 rad (91.3°).

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. How long does a LEO-to-GEO transfer take (half of a 10.58 h orbit)?

    Show answer

    5.29 h.

  2. For a circle, how are M, E and ν related?

    Show answer

    They are equal.

  3. Why can’t we solve for E directly?

    Show answer

    Kepler’s equation is transcendental.

  4. What is the mean motion of a 92.6 min orbit in rev/day?

    Show answer

    1440/92.56 = 15.56.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: True anomaly grows uniformly with time.

    Actually: Mean anomaly does.

  • Myth: Kepler’s equation applies to hyperbolas unchanged.

    Actually: Hyperbolas use a hyperbolic form.

  • Myth: Newton’s method needs many iterations.

    Actually: Usually 3–5.

How this helps save the planet

NASA reports that before DART’s impact, the moonlet Dimorphos took 11 hours and 55 minutes to orbit its parent asteroid Didymos, and that after the impact on September 26, 2022 the period was 11 hours and 23 minutes, a change of 32 minutes (uncertainty about ±2 minutes).

Astronomers measured that change by timing the moonlet’s orbit from telescopes on Earth: Kepler’s laws turn a small timing shift into proof that the orbit had changed.

Unit 4 · Weeks 5–6

Orbit Transfers: Hohmann and Beyond

Impulsive maneuvers and Δv, the Hohmann transfer between circular orbits, transfer time, bi-elliptic transfers, phasing and rendezvous, and raising and lowering orbits.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute Δv for a Hohmann transfer.
  • Find the transfer time.
  • Explain when bi-elliptic transfers save Δv.
  • Plan a simple phasing maneuver.

Key concepts

  • An impulsive burn changes velocity instantly by Δv; the orbit after the burn follows from vis-viva.
  • The Hohmann transfer uses an ellipse tangent to both circles: burn at the inner orbit to enter the ellipse, and burn again at apoapsis to circularize.
  • Transfer time is half the period of the transfer ellipse.
  • For very large radius ratios (above about 11.9), a three-burn bi-elliptic transfer can use less Δv, at the cost of much more time.
  • Phasing: to catch a target in the same orbit, enter a slightly smaller or larger orbit for one or more revolutions, then return.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 Find the Δv for a Hohmann transfer from a 400 km circular orbit to GEO (in the same plane).

Show the steps
  1. Δv₁ = 10.066 − 7.669 = 2.398 km/s.
  2. Δv₂ = 3.075 − 1.618 = 1.456 km/s.

Answer: Total Δv = 3.85 km/s.

Example 4.2 Find the transfer time.

Show the steps
  1. t = π√(24,471,000³/μ).

Answer: t = 5.29 h.

Example 4.3 A deorbit burn at 700 km lowers perigee to 200 km. Find Δv.

Show the steps
  1. v_c = √(μ/7078 km).
  2. v_a on the 7078 × 6578 km ellipse from vis-viva.

Answer: Δv = 139 m/s.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Which burn is larger in the LEO-to-GEO Hohmann?

    Show answer

  2. Above what radius ratio can bi-elliptic win?

    Show answer

  3. To catch up to a target ahead in the same orbit, go higher or lower?

    Show answer

    Lower (shorter period).

  4. Which way do you burn to lower an orbit?

    Show answer

    Retrograde.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: To speed up in orbit, burn forward and stay faster.

    Actually: Raising the orbit makes you slower overall.

  • Myth: Hohmann is always the cheapest.

    Actually: Not for very large ratios.

  • Myth: Transfer time is a full period.

    Actually: Half a period.

How this helps save the planet

The FCC adopted a rule in September 2022 requiring satellite operators in low Earth orbit to dispose of their satellites within 5 years of completing their missions (Report and Order FCC-22-74).

A deorbit burn is just a Hohmann-style half maneuver: lower the perigee into the upper atmosphere so drag finishes the job. From 700 km, lowering perigee to 200 km takes only about 139 m/s in this unit’s example.

Unit 5 · Week 7

Plane Changes and Combined Maneuvers

Inclination and node changes, the simple plane-change Δv, why plane changes are expensive in low orbit, combining a plane change with a Hohmann burn, and launch site latitude.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute a simple plane-change Δv.
  • Combine a plane change with a circularization burn.
  • Explain why plane changes are done at high altitude.
  • Relate launch latitude to the minimum inclination.

Key concepts

  • Rotating the orbit plane by Δi at speed v costs Δv = 2v sin(Δi/2), with no change in speed.
  • Because the cost scales with speed, plane changes are cheapest where the spacecraft is slowest: at apoapsis or in high orbits.
  • Combining a plane change with a speed change in one burn uses the law of cosines: Δv = √(v₁² + v₂² − 2v₁v₂ cos Δi), which is cheaper than doing them separately.
  • A direct launch reaches an inclination no lower than the launch site’s latitude, so GEO satellites launched from mid-latitudes must remove inclination later.
  • Node changes and combined inclination-and-node changes use the same velocity-triangle idea with spherical trigonometry.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 Find the Δv to remove 28.5° of inclination at GEO (v = 3.075 km/s).

Show the steps
  1. Δv = 2 × 3.075 × sin 14.25°.

Answer: Δv = 1.51 km/s.

Example 5.2 Combine that plane change with the GEO circularization burn (v_a = 1.618 km/s).

Show the steps
  1. Δv = √(1.618² + 3.075² − 2 × 1.618 × 3.075 × cos 28.5°).

Answer: Δv = 1.82 km/s, saving 1.15 km/s over separate burns (1.51 + 1.46).

Example 5.3 Find the cost of a 10° plane change in a 400 km orbit.

Show the steps
  1. Δv = 2 × 7.669 × sin 5°.

Answer: Δv = 1.34 km/s.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Total LEO-to-GEO Δv with the combined final burn?

    Show answer

    2.40 + 1.82 = 4.22 km/s.

  2. Where is the cheapest place to change planes on an ellipse?

    Show answer

    Apoapsis.

  3. A 60° plane change costs how many times v?

    Show answer

    2 sin 30° = 1 × v.

  4. Can a direct launch from 28.5° latitude reach 0° inclination?

    Show answer

    No.

Common misconceptions

The Tsiolkovsky rocket equation, specific impulse and exhaust speed, mass ratio and propellant fraction, thrust, staging, gravity and drag losses on ascent, and building a mission Δv budget.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Apply the rocket equation.
  • Relate specific impulse, exhaust speed and thrust.
  • Show why staging helps.
  • Build a simple Δv budget.

Key concepts

  • The rocket equation: Δv = I_sp g₀ ln(m₀/m_f), where m₀/m_f is the mass ratio. Exhaust speed v_e = I_sp g₀.
  • Thrust F = ṁ v_e = ṁ I_sp g₀.
  • Because Δv grows only with the log of the mass ratio, a single stage to orbit needs most of its mass to be propellant. Staging drops empty tanks and engines so later stages push less dead mass.
  • Reaching low orbit takes about 9.3–10 km/s of Δv in practice (a textbook range): about 7.7–7.8 km/s of orbital speed plus gravity and drag losses, less a boost from Earth’s rotation for eastward launches.
  • A mission Δv budget adds every maneuver: launch, transfers, plane changes, station-keeping and disposal, plus margin.

Unit 7 · Weeks 9–10

Perturbations: Earth’s Bulge, Drag and Sun-Synchronous Orbits

Why real orbits drift, Earth’s oblateness and J₂, regression of the node and rotation of periapsis, sun-synchronous orbits, frozen and critically inclined orbits, atmospheric drag and orbit decay, and third-body and solar pressure effects.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute nodal regression from J₂.
  • Find the inclination for a sun-synchronous orbit.
  • Explain orbit decay from drag.
  • Name other perturbations.

Key concepts

  • Earth bulges at the equator. Its J₂ term (1.0826 × 10⁻³) makes the orbit plane precess: dΩ/dt = −1.5 n J₂ (R/p)² cos i, with p = a(1 − e²).
  • Prograde orbits (i < 90°) regress westward; retrograde orbits (i > 90°) advance eastward.
  • A sun-synchronous orbit picks i so the plane advances 360° per year, about 0.9856° per day, keeping the same local solar time at each pass.
  • In low orbit, atmospheric drag slowly removes energy, lowering the orbit until the spacecraft reenters. Drag grows sharply when solar activity heats the upper atmosphere.
  • Other perturbations: Moon and Sun gravity (important for GEO), solar radiation pressure, and higher gravity harmonics.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 7.1 Find the inclination for a sun-synchronous circular orbit at 705 km.

Show the steps
  1. r = 7083 km; n = √(μ/r³).
  2. Required rate 0.9856°/day = 1.991 × 10⁻⁷ rad/s.
  3. cos i = −rate/(1.5nJ₂(R/r)²).

Answer: i = 98.2°, exactly Landsat 9’s inclination.

Example 7.2 Check the nodal rate for Landsat 9 (705 km, 98.2°).

Show the steps
  1. dΩ/dt = −1.5nJ₂(R/r)² cos 98.2°.

Answer: +0.985° per day, matching the 0.9856° per day the Sun moves.

Example 7.3 Find the nodal regression of a 400 km orbit at 51.6°.

Show the steps
  1. Same formula with r = 6778 km and cos 51.6°.

Answer: −5.00° per day (westward).

Interplanetary Transfers and Patched Conics

Heliocentric Hohmann transfers, transfer time to Mars, departure v∞ and C₃, the patched-conic method, spheres of influence, escape from a parking orbit, synodic periods and launch windows, and gravity assists.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute an Earth–Mars Hohmann transfer.
  • Find v∞ and C₃.
  • Find the Δv to depart from low Earth orbit.
  • Compute synodic periods and explain launch windows.

Key concepts

  • Far from planets, a spacecraft follows a heliocentric conic with μ_Sun = 1.327 × 10²⁰ m³/s². An Earth–Mars Hohmann transfer is an ellipse from 1 AU to 1.524 AU.
  • Patched conics: near each planet, use a hyperbola relative to the planet; far away, use the heliocentric ellipse; match velocities at the sphere of influence.
  • The departure hyperbolic excess speed v∞ is the heliocentric speed needed minus Earth’s orbital speed. C₃ = v∞² is the launch energy.
  • From a circular parking orbit, the departure burn is Δv = √(v∞² + 2μ/r) − √(μ/r), using the Oberth effect of burning deep in the gravity well.
  • Launch windows repeat every synodic period, 1/(1/T₁ − 1/T₂): about 2.1 years for Mars. Gravity assists change a spacecraft’s heliocentric speed for free by flying past a planet.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 9.1 Find the Earth–Mars Hohmann transfer time.

  2. t = π√(a³/μ_Sun).

Answer: t = 259 days.

Example 9.2 Find v∞ and C₃ at departure.

Show the steps
  1. Earth’s speed √(μ_Sun/1 AU) = 29.78 km/s.
  2. Transfer perihelion speed from vis-viva.

Answer: v∞ = 2.95 km/s; C₃ = 8.68 km²/s².

Show the steps

Key concepts

  • Low Earth orbit holds millions of pieces of debris: dead satellites, rocket parts, fragments and paint flecks. At orbital speeds even tiny pieces carry enormous energy.

Glossary

Apoapsis
Farthest point of an orbit.
Conjunction
Close approach between two space objects.
C₃
Launch energy, the square of v∞.
Eccentricity
How stretched an orbit is.
Geostationary orbit
Equatorial orbit with a one-sidereal-day period.
Ground track
Path of the point directly below a satellite.
Hohmann transfer
Two-burn transfer on a tangent ellipse.
Inclination
Tilt of the orbit plane from the equator.
J₂
Earth’s oblateness term.
Kepler’s equation
M = E − e sin E.
Kessler cascade
Chain reaction of debris collisions.
Mean anomaly
Angle that grows uniformly with time.
Patched conics
Joining planet-centered and Sun-centered conics.
Periapsis
Closest point of an orbit.
Specific impulse
Thrust per propellant weight flow, in seconds.
Sun-synchronous orbit
Orbit whose plane keeps a fixed angle to the Sun.
Synodic period
Time between repeat alignments of two orbiting bodies.
True anomaly
Angle from periapsis to the spacecraft.
Vis-viva equation
v² = μ(2/r − 1/a).
Δv
Change in velocity a maneuver requires.
