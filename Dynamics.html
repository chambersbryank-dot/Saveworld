About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a one-semester sophomore engineering dynamics course. It is modeled on the topics and depth of MIT 2.003 Engineering Dynamics and MIT 8.01 Classical Mechanics (both on MIT OpenCourseWare), Purdue ME 274 and Georgia Tech dynamics, and on the free OpenStax University Physics Volume 1, which we link throughout. None of those authors or schools endorses this page.

What we add: every unit ties to real clean-energy, Earth-observation or planetary-defense work with a cited public source (electric cars, the space station, GRACE-FO, pumped storage, NASA’s DART asteroid test, rockets, wind and hydro turbines, earthquake-safe buildings), every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab has a free simulation or a cheap home version.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Dynamics is the physics of every machine that moves energy around. Electric cars turn battery energy into motion and win some of it back, hydro and wind turbines are spinning rigid bodies, pumped-storage plants bank energy by lifting water, and satellites that watch Earth’s water and climate stay up because they keep falling around it.

It is also how we protect the planet: NASA’s DART mission used momentum to change an asteroid’s orbit, and engineers use vibration theory to keep buildings standing in earthquakes. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Statics: free-body diagrams, moments and centroids.
  • Physics I: Newton’s laws, energy and momentum.
  • Calculus I and II: derivatives, integrals and simple differential equations.
  • Differential Equations helps with Unit 10 (vibration) but is not required; the needed solutions are given.

How to study

  • Start with kinematics: what is known about position, velocity and acceleration, and in which coordinates?
  • Draw a free-body diagram and a kinetic diagram (ma and Iα) side by side.
  • Choose the method that fits: F = ma for accelerations, work-energy for speeds between two positions, impulse-momentum for forces over time and impacts.
  • Solve with symbols first, then put in SI numbers, and check units and limiting cases.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer.
  • Retake the quizzes a few days later; spaced practice beats one long session.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer or tablet for the free PhET simulations (they run in a browser).
  • A smartphone with a slow-motion video camera or a free sensor app (accelerometer, stopwatch).
  • A board or long book to make a ramp, a tape measure, and a ball, a can of soup, an empty can and a ring or hoop to roll.
  • String, a small weight (a nut or washers) and a ruler for a pendulum.
  • A few rubber bands or a soft spring and small masses (coins in a bag) for a spring-mass oscillator.
  • Spreadsheet software for graphs and fits.

Unit 1 · Weeks 1–2

Kinematics of Particles in a Straight Line

Position, velocity and acceleration as derivatives, constant acceleration, acceleration given as a function of time, speed or position, and distance versus displacement.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Relate x, v and a by differentiation and integration.
  • Use the constant-acceleration equations correctly.
  • Solve a(x) problems with v dv = a dx.
  • Tell total distance from displacement.

Key concepts

  • Velocity is v = dx/dt and acceleration is a = dv/dt = d²x/dt². Integrating goes the other way.
  • Only when a is constant do v = v₀ + at, x = x₀ + v₀t + ½at² and v² = v₀² + 2a(x − x₀) hold.
  • If a is given as a function of position, use a dx = v dv; if a function of speed, use dt = dv/a(v).
  • Distance traveled counts every leg; split the motion where v = 0 and add the lengths.
  • Signs matter: pick a positive direction and keep it; slowing down means a and v have opposite signs.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 A particle moves with x = t³ − 6t² + 9t (m, s). Find when it stops, v and a at t = 2 s, and the distance traveled from 0 to 4 s.

Show the steps
  1. v = 3t² − 12t + 9 = 0 at t = 1 s and t = 3 s; a = 6t − 12.
  2. At t = 2 s: v = −3 m/s, a = 0.
  3. x(0) = 0, x(1) = 4, x(3) = 0, x(4) = 4, so legs of 4 + 4 + 4 m.

Answer: Stops at 1 s and 3 s; v = −3 m/s, a = 0 at 2 s; 12 m traveled (displacement 4 m).

Example 1.2 A car starts from rest and accelerates at 3 m/s² for 8 s. Find its speed and distance.

Show the steps
  1. v = at = 3 × 8.
  2. x = ½at² = ½ × 3 × 64.

Answer: 24 m/s and 96 m.

Example 1.3 A car at 30 m/s brakes at 6 m/s². Find the stopping distance and time.

Show the steps
  1. x = v₀²/(2a) = 900/12.
  2. t = v₀/a.

Answer: 75 m in 5 s.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. A ball is thrown straight up at 15 m/s. Find its maximum height and the time to reach it.

    Show answer

    h = v²/(2g) = 11.47 m; t = v/g = 1.529 s.

  2. A particle starts at rest at x = 0 with a = 2x (m/s²). Find v at x = 3 m.

    Show answer

    v dv = 2x dx gives v² = 2x², v = √2 × 3 = 4.24 m/s.

  3. Sample values: an electric car goes from 0 to 27 m/s (about 100 km/h) in 6 s. Find its average acceleration.

    Show answer

    a = 27/6 = 4.5 m/s² (sample values).

  4. A stone dropped into a well hits the water after 2.5 s. How deep is the water surface (ignore sound travel time)?

    Show answer

    h = ½gt² = 30.7 m.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Zero velocity means zero acceleration.

    Actually: At the top of a throw v = 0 but a = −g.

  • Myth: Distance and displacement are the same.

    Actually: Not when the particle reverses; split at v = 0.

  • Myth: The constant-acceleration formulas always work.

    Actually: Only for constant a; otherwise integrate.

Flashcards

  • Velocity

    v = dx/dt.

  • Acceleration

    a = dv/dt.

  • Constant a

    v = v₀ + at; x = v₀t + ½at².

  • No-time equation

    v² = v₀² + 2aΔx.

  • a(x)

    a dx = v dv.

  • Distance

    Split the motion where v = 0.

How this helps save the planet

Every speed change costs energy, and where that energy comes from matters. FuelEconomy.gov (DOE/EPA) says electric vehicles convert over 77% of the electrical energy from the grid to power at the wheels, while conventional gasoline vehicles convert only about 12% to 30% of the energy in gasoline. The kinematics in this unit, how fast a car speeds up and how far it brakes, is the starting point for the drive-cycle models used to rate vehicle efficiency.

Unit 2 · Week 3

Curvilinear Motion: Projectiles, Normal–Tangential and Polar Coordinates

Motion in a plane: rectangular components and projectiles, normal and tangential components on curved paths, polar coordinates for rotating arms, and relative motion.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Solve projectile problems with independent x and y motions.
  • Find tangential and normal acceleration on a curved path.
  • Use polar components for motion along a rotating arm.
  • Add relative velocities.

Key concepts

  • A projectile has a_x = 0 and a_y = −g (no air drag); time links the two directions.
  • On a curved path, a_t = dv/dt changes the speed and a_n = v²/ρ points toward the center of curvature and changes the direction.
  • In polar coordinates, a_r = r̈ − rθ̇² and a_θ = rθ̈ + 2ṙθ̇; the 2ṙθ̇ term is the Coriolis part.
  • Relative motion: v_A = v_B + v_A/B, and the same for accelerations (for translating frames).
  • A satellite in a circular orbit has only normal acceleration: it falls toward Earth at v²/r while moving sideways fast enough to miss it.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 A ball is launched at 20 m/s and 30° above level ground. Find the range, maximum height and flight time.

Show the steps
  1. v_x = 17.32 m/s, v_y = 10 m/s.
  2. Time = 2v_y/g; height = v_y²/(2g); R = v₀² sin 60°/g.

Answer: R = 35.3 m, h = 5.10 m, t = 2.04 s.

Example 2.2 A car at 25 m/s on a curve of radius 100 m is slowing at 2 m/s². Find its total acceleration.

Show the steps
  1. a_n = 25²/100 = 6.25 m/s²; a_t = −2 m/s².
  2. |a| = √(6.25² + 2²).

Answer: 6.56 m/s².

Example 2.3 A collar slides out along an arm at 0.3 m/s (constant) while the arm turns at a constant 2 rad/s. Find its acceleration when r = 0.5 m.

Show the steps
  1. a_r = 0 − 0.5 × 2² = −2.0 m/s².
  2. a_θ = 0 + 2 × 0.3 × 2 = 1.2 m/s².

Answer: |a| = 2.33 m/s².

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. NASA says the station orbits Earth about every 90 minutes. Using a sample orbit radius of 6,791 km (420 km altitude), find its speed and normal acceleration.

    Show answer

    v = 2πr/T = 7,902 m/s (NASA quotes about 5 miles per second, 8.0 km/s); a_n = v²/r = 9.19 m/s².

  2. A boat heads north at 4 m/s in a current flowing east at 3 m/s. Find its velocity over the ground.

    Show answer

    5 m/s at 36.9° east of north.

  3. Which launch angle gives the longest range on level ground (no drag)?

    Show answer

    45°, where sin 2θ = 1.

  4. Find the normal acceleration of a car at 15 m/s on a curve of radius 50 m.

    Show answer

    a_n = 225/50 = 4.5 m/s².

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: A body moving at constant speed has no acceleration.

    Actually: On a curve it has a_n = v²/ρ.

  • Myth: Horizontal speed of a projectile drops during flight.

    Actually: Without drag, v_x stays constant.

  • Myth: Astronauts float because there is no gravity in orbit.

    Actually: Gravity there is still strong: (6371/6791)² = 88% of surface gravity at a sample 420 km altitude. They float because they are in free fall with the station.

Flashcards

  • Projectile

    a_x = 0, a_y = −g.

  • Range

    R = v₀² sin 2θ/g.

  • Tangential

    a_t = dv/dt.

  • Normal

    a_n = v²/ρ.

  • Polar

    a_r = r̈ − rθ̇², a_θ = rθ̈ + 2ṙθ̇.

  • Relative velocity

    v_A = v_B + v_A/B.

How this helps save the planet

The space station shows curvilinear motion at its most extreme. NASA says it travels at five miles per second, orbiting Earth about every 90 minutes and making 16 orbits in 24 hours, and that its path takes it over 90 percent of Earth’s population; astronauts have taken millions of images of the planet below. The same normal-acceleration balance keeps every Earth-observing satellite in orbit.

Unit 3 · Weeks 4–5

Kinetics of Particles: Newton’s Second Law

ΣF = ma in rectangular, normal–tangential and polar coordinates, friction and connected bodies, banked curves, and orbits.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Draw free-body and kinetic diagrams.
  • Apply ΣF = ma with friction and connected bodies.
  • Solve curve and banking problems with normal–tangential components.
  • Find circular-orbit speeds.

Key concepts

  • Newton’s second law: ΣF = ma, in an inertial frame. Write it in the coordinates that match the motion.
  • Kinetic friction while sliding is μ_kN, opposite to the sliding direction.
  • Connected bodies share accelerations through constraints (a rope of fixed length, a pulley); write one equation per body.
  • On a curve, the net normal force supplies mv²/ρ; banking lets the normal force provide it without friction.
  • For a circular orbit, gravity supplies the normal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r, so v = √(GM/r).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 A 1200 kg car skids to a stop from 25 m/s with μ_k = 0.7. Find the deceleration and stopping distance.

Show the steps
  1. a = μ_k g = 6.87 m/s² (mass cancels).
  2. x = v²/(2a) = 625/13.73.

Answer: 6.87 m/s²; 45.5 m.

Example 3.2 Masses of 5 kg and 3 kg hang on either side of a light, frictionless pulley. Find the acceleration and rope tension.

Show the steps
  1. a = (5 − 3)g/(5 + 3).
  2. T = 2m₁m₂g/(m₁ + m₂).

Answer: a = 2.45 m/s²; T = 36.8 N.

Example 3.3 A curve of radius 80 m is banked at 15°. At what speed is no friction needed?

Show the steps
  1. v = √(gρ tan 15°) = √(9.81 × 80 × 0.268).

Answer: 14.5 m/s (52 km/h).

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. A 10 kg block is pushed with 50 N along a floor with μ_k = 0.3. Find its acceleration.

    Show answer

    a = (50 − 29.4)/10 = 2.06 m/s².

  2. A 70 kg person stands in an elevator accelerating upward at 1.5 m/s². Find the floor force.

    Show answer

    N = m(g + a) = 792 N.

  3. Find the circular-orbit speed at a sample radius of 6,791 km (GM = 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³/s²).

    Show answer

    v = √(GM/r) = 7,661 m/s.

  4. Find the top speed on a flat curve of radius 50 m with μ_s = 0.8.

    Show answer

    v = √(μ_s g r) = 19.8 m/s.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: A moving object needs a net force to keep moving.

    Actually: Only to change its velocity.

  • Myth: ma is a force on the free-body diagram.

    Actually: ma is the result, drawn on the kinetic diagram.

  • Myth: Centrifugal force pushes you out on a curve.

    Actually: In an inertial frame, the net inward force makes you turn; nothing pushes outward.

  • Circular orbit

How this helps save the planet

GRACE-FO weighs Earth’s water with Newton’s second law. NASA JPL explains that the twin satellites fly about 220 km apart; areas of slightly stronger gravity pull the lead satellite ahead first, and a microwave ranging system measures the tiny change in distance. An accelerometer at each satellite’s center of mass measures non-gravitational accelerations such as atmospheric drag so that only gravity is counted. The result is monthly maps showing how water moves in aquifers, lakes, ice sheets and oceans.

Unit 4 · Week 6

Work, Energy and Power

Work of forces (gravity, springs, friction), the work–energy principle, conservative forces and potential energy, conservation of energy, power and efficiency.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute the work of gravity, springs and friction.
  • Use the work–energy principle to find speeds between two positions.
  • Use conservation of mechanical energy when only conservative forces do work.
  • Compute power and efficiency.

Key concepts

  • Work is U = ∫F·dr. Gravity does −mgΔh, a spring does −½k(x₂² − x₁²), and kinetic friction does −μ_kN × distance.
  • Work–energy principle: T₁ + ΣU₁→₂ = T₂, with kinetic energy T = ½mv².
  • For conservative forces, work equals the loss of potential energy, so T₁ + V₁ = T₂ + V₂ when only they do work.
  • Power is the rate of work: P = F·v. Efficiency is output power divided by input power.
  • Energy methods give speeds directly without finding accelerations, which is why they are so useful.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 A 2 kg block slides from rest down a smooth ramp, dropping 3 m. Find its speed at the bottom.

Show the steps
  1. mgh = ½mv².
  2. v = √(2gh).

Answer: 7.67 m/s (mass does not matter).

Example 4.2 A spring (k = 500 N/m) compressed 0.2 m launches a 0.5 kg block on a smooth floor. Find the launch speed.

Show the steps
  1. ½kx² = ½mv².
  2. v = x√(k/m).

Answer: 6.32 m/s.

Example 4.3 A 1500 kg car climbs a 5% grade at a steady 20 m/s. Ignoring drag and rolling losses, what power lifts it?

Show the steps
  1. θ = tan⁻¹ 0.05 = 2.86°.
  2. P = mg sin θ × v.

Answer: 14.7 kW.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Sample values: a pumped-storage plant raises 1 million m³ of water by 300 m. How much energy does it store?

    Show answer

    E = mgh = 10⁹ × 9.81 × 300 = 2.94 × 10¹² J ≈ 818 MWh (sample values).

  2. A motor with 85% efficiency delivers 10 kW. What power does it draw?

    Show answer

    P_in = 10/0.85 = 11.8 kW.

  3. A 20 kg box is pushed 5 m across a floor with μ_k = 0.4. How much work does friction do?

    Show answer

    U = −0.4 × 196.2 × 5 = −392 J.

  4. A pendulum bob is released 0.5 m above its lowest point. Find its speed there.

    Show answer

    v = √(2g × 0.5) = 3.13 m/s.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Work is force times distance in every case.

    Actually: Only the component along the motion does work.

  • Myth: The normal force does work on a sliding block.

    Actually: It is perpendicular to the motion, so its work is zero.

  • Myth: Energy is lost to friction.

    Actually: It is converted to heat; total energy is conserved, mechanical energy is not.

Impulse, Momentum and Impact

Linear impulse and momentum, conservation of momentum, direct central impact and the coefficient of restitution, and angular momentum of a particle.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use the impulse–momentum principle to find average forces.
  • Apply conservation of momentum to collisions and recoil.
  • Solve impacts with the coefficient of restitution.
  • Use conservation of angular momentum for central-force motion.

Key concepts

  • Linear momentum is L = mv. Impulse–momentum: mv₁ + ∫F dt = mv₂.
  • If no external impulse acts on a system, its total momentum is conserved, even when kinetic energy is not.
  • The coefficient of restitution e = (v₂′ − v₁′)/(v₁ − v₂) ranges from 0 (perfectly plastic) to 1 (perfectly elastic).
  • Angular momentum about O is H_O = r × mv. If all forces pass through O (a central force like gravity), H_O is conserved.
  • In orbit, conservation of angular momentum means a satellite moves fastest at periapsis: r₁v₁ = r₂v₂ at the apsides.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Show the steps

  2. F = impulse/Δt.

Answer: 10.5 N·s; 5,250 N.

Example 5.2 A 1500 kg car at 20 m/s hits a stopped 1000 kg car and they lock together. Find the common speed and kinetic energy lost.

Show the steps
  1. 1500 × 20 = 2500v.
  2. T₁ = 300 kJ; T₂ = ½ × 2500 × 12².

Answer: 12 m/s; 120 kJ (40%) lost.

Example 5.3 A 2 kg ball at 5 m/s hits a 3 kg ball at rest head-on with e = 0.8. Find both final velocities.

Show the steps
  1. Momentum: 10 = 2v₁′ + 3v₂′.
  2. Restitution: v₂′ − v₁′ = 0.8 × 5 = 4.

Answer: v₁′ = −0.4 m/s (bounces back), v₂′ = 3.6 m/s.

Rotation about a fixed axis, angular velocity and acceleration, gear trains, relative velocity and acceleration in rigid bodies, instantaneous centers, and rolling without slipping.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

Key concepts

  • For rotation about a fixed axis, a point at radius r has v = rω, a_t = rα and a_n = rω².

Key concepts

  • A mass on a spring obeys mẍ + kx = 0 and vibrates at ω_n = √(k/m); the period is T = 2π/ω_n. A simple pendulum has ω_n = √(g/L) for small swings.
  • With viscous damping, mẍ + cẋ + kx = 0. The damping ratio ζ = c/(2√(km)) sets the behavior: ζ < 1 oscillates and decays, ζ = 1 is critical, ζ > 1 creeps back.
  • The logarithmic decrement δ = ln(x₁/x₂) between successive peaks gives ζ ≈ δ/(2π) for light damping.
  • A harmonic force F₀ sin ωt drives a steady response of amplitude X = (F₀/k)/√((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²), with r = ω/ω_n.
  • At resonance (r = 1) the magnification is 1/(2ζ): with light damping, a small push repeated at the right rhythm builds a large motion.

Show the steps

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: A heavier mass vibrates faster.

    Actually: ω_n = √(k/m): more mass, lower frequency.

  • Myth: More damping always means more safety.

    Actually: It lowers resonant peaks but also passes more force at high frequency.

  • Myth: A pendulum’s period depends on how heavy the bob is.

    Actually: For small swings, only on L and g.

How this helps save the planet

Resonance decides which buildings survive earthquakes. IRIS (now EarthScope) explains that every building has a natural period, the time it takes to sway back and forth, and that when ground motion matches it the building undergoes its largest oscillations and greatest damage. A USGS study of 41 tall buildings found their damping ratios clearly lower than 3%, and the magnification formula in this unit shows why low damping makes resonance dangerous. Engineers tune periods and add damping so homes, hospitals and infrastructure stay standing.
