Find them, then move them

Planetary defense has two halves. First, find and track asteroids and comets early. Then, if one is ever on course for Earth, have a tested way to push it aside. Humanity has now done both: NASA's DART spacecraft hit the asteroid moonlet Dimorphos on September 26, 2022 and shortened its 11-hour-55-minute orbit by 33 minutes and 15 seconds.

Source: NASA, Asteroid's orbit, shape changed after DART impact

This hub collects the missions that make that defense real, plus the natural one that has been doing it for billions of years.

Planetary defense solutions

Hera (ESA)

Europe's planetary defender is flying to the only asteroid whose orbit humans have measurably changed. It arrives at Didymos and its moon Dimorphos in November 2026 to measure what NASA's DART impact really did.

NEO Surveyor (NASA)

The first space telescope built specifically to hunt hazardous asteroids and comets, including the dark ones and the ones hiding in the Sun's glare. Launch no earlier than September 2027.

Webb and asteroid 2024 YR4

In February 2026 the James Webb Space Telescope tracked a faint 60-meter asteroid two years before ground telescopes could, and ruled out a 2032 impact on the Moon.

Jupiter (natural)

The solar system's biggest natural debris catcher. Comet impacts are thought to happen over 2,000 times more often on Jupiter than on Earth, with honest trade-offs for asteroids.

A note on Roman

NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope launched on August 30, 2026. NASA describes it as a mission for dark energy, dark matter, exoplanets and galaxy surveys. It is not a near-Earth-object mission. The dedicated asteroid hunter is NEO Surveyor.
