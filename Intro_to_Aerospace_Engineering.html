About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows first-year aerospace engineering introductions such as MIT 16.00 and the opening of MIT’s Unified Engineering, the introductory aeronautics and astronautics courses at Purdue, Georgia Tech and Embry-Riddle, and the classic textbook Introduction to Flight by John D. Anderson Jr. We link to free materials throughout, especially NASA Glenn’s Beginner’s Guide to Aeronautics. None of these organizations endorse this page.

What we add: every unit connects to cleaner flight and to the spacecraft that watch our planet, every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and four labs, including a virtual wind tunnel and a glider you build, make the equations real.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Aerospace engineering builds the satellites that measure Earth’s climate, forests, ice and oceans, and it also has to shrink its own footprint: every kilogram of jet fuel burned makes about 3.16 kg of CO₂.

Each unit ends with a short, sourced section showing how its ideas help people and the planet.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • High-school algebra and trigonometry; logarithms and exponentials.
  • Physics I mechanics (forces, energy, momentum, circular motion) helps; take it alongside if you can.
  • Calculus I is useful for a few derivations but not required for the numbers.

How to study

  • Keep units in SI throughout and write them on every line.
  • Sketch the forces (lift, weight, thrust, drag) before writing any equation.
  • Estimate first; aerospace numbers have familiar sizes (sea-level air ≈ 1.2 kg/m³, low orbit ≈ 7.7 km/s).
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer.
  • Use the NASA Glenn simulators to check your intuition.
  • Retake the quizzes a few days later. Spaced practice beats one long session.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer with a web browser and a spreadsheet (FoilSim runs in the browser).
  • For the glider lab: card stock or foam board, a ruler, scissors, modeling clay or coins for nose weight, and a tape measure.
  • Optional: a smartphone stopwatch.

Unit 1 · Week 1

History of Flight & the Design Process

From the Wright brothers to Artemis: how aerospace engineers turn a mission into requirements, trade-offs and a design that flies.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Outline key milestones of aviation and spaceflight.
  • Describe the design cycle: requirements, concept, preliminary and detailed design, test.
  • Explain trade studies and why weight drives everything.
  • Name the four forces of flight.

Key concepts

  • On December 17, 1903, the Wright Flyer made a 12-second flight covering 36 m (120 ft); the best flight that day covered 260 m (852 ft) in 59 seconds. The Wrights succeeded by testing: they built gliders and their own wind tunnel before adding an engine.
  • Every design starts with a mission: payload, range or orbit, cost, safety. Requirements turn the mission into numbers the design must meet.
  • Design is iterative: concept sketch, sizing, analysis, test, redesign. A trade study compares options against weighted criteria.
  • Four forces act on an aircraft: lift, weight, thrust and drag. In steady level flight, lift = weight and thrust = drag.
  • Weight compounds: extra structure needs more lift, a bigger wing, more drag, more fuel, and more weight again.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 A 1,100 kg light airplane cruises level. How much lift must its wing produce?

Show the steps
  1. Level flight: L = W = mg.
  2. L = 1,100 × 9.81.

Answer: 10,791 N ≈ 10.8 kN.

Example 1.2 Two wing concepts are scored out of 10 on cost (weight 0.3), efficiency (0.5) and ease of building (0.2). Concept A scores 8, 6, 9; B scores 5, 9, 6. Which wins?

Show the steps
  1. A: 0.3(8) + 0.5(6) + 0.2(9) = 7.2.
  2. B: 0.3(5) + 0.5(9) + 0.2(6) = 7.2.

Answer: A tie at 7.2: the team should test both or revisit the weights it trusts least.

Example 1.3 The Wright Flyer’s first flight covered 36 m in 12 s. What was its average ground speed?

Show the steps
  1. v = 36/12.

Answer: 3 m/s over the ground. It flew into a strong headwind, so its airspeed was much higher.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. An airliner of mass 70,000 kg cruises level. Find its lift.

    Show answer

    L = 70,000 × 9.81 = 686,700 N ≈ 687 kN.

  2. In level cruise an aircraft’s engines give 30 kN of thrust. What is its drag?

    Show answer

    30 kN, since thrust balances drag in steady flight.

  3. Why did the Wright brothers build a wind tunnel?

    Show answer

    Published lift data were unreliable, so they measured many wing shapes themselves: testing before building is the heart of the design process.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Engineers design the aircraft first and then find a mission for it.

    Actually: The mission and its requirements come first.

  • Myth: In level flight thrust must balance weight.

    Actually: Thrust balances drag; lift balances weight.

  • Myth: Shaving a little weight saves only that weight.

    Actually: Weight savings compound through wing size, drag and fuel.

Flashcards

  • Four forces

    Lift, weight, thrust, drag.

  • Requirements

    Measurable targets a design must meet.

  • Trade study

    Weighted comparison of design options.

  • Design cycle

    Concept → preliminary → detailed design → test → iterate.

  • Wright Flyer

    First powered, controlled airplane flights, Dec. 17, 1903.

  • Weight spiral

    Extra weight grows the whole design.

Show answer

How this helps save the planet

Design priorities change with the times. Requirements for new aircraft now include fuel burn and emissions, so the same trade-study tools weigh climate alongside cost and performance.

NASA lists three main ways to make aviation sustainable: efficient aircraft technology, sustainable aviation fuel, and better operations and infrastructure.

Unit 2 · Week 2

The Atmosphere

The air an aircraft flies through: how temperature, pressure, density and the speed of sound change with altitude.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use a standard atmosphere model to find T, p and ρ at altitude.
  • Apply the ideal-gas law to air.
  • Compute the speed of sound and Mach number.
  • Explain why airliners cruise near 11 km.

Key concepts

  • A standard atmosphere is an average model fitted to measurements. In NASA’s model the troposphere runs from the ground to 11,000 m, and temperature falls about 6.5 °C per kilometer.
  • From 11 to 25 km (lower stratosphere) the model’s temperature is constant at −56.46 °C, while pressure keeps falling exponentially.
  • Density follows from the ideal-gas law, ρ = p/(RT). At 11 km air is about 30% as dense as at sea level.
  • The speed of sound is a = √(γRT), so it depends only on temperature. Mach number M = V/a.
  • Thin air means less drag at a given speed, which is why jets cruise high, but wings and engines still need enough air to work.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 Find T, p and ρ at 5,000 m with NASA’s model.

Show the steps
  1. T = 15.04 − 0.00649 × 5,000 = −17.41 °C.
  2. p = 101.29 × (255.69/288.08)^5.256 = 54.11 kPa.
  3. ρ = 54.11/(0.2869 × 255.69).

Answer: T ≈ −17.4 °C, p ≈ 54.1 kPa, ρ ≈ 0.738 kg/m³ (60% of sea level).

Example 2.2 Find the speed of sound at sea level (15 °C) and at 11 km (−56.5 °C).

Show the steps
  1. a = √(1.4 × 287 × 288.15) = 340.3 m/s.
  2. a = √(1.4 × 287 × 216.65) = 295.0 m/s.

Answer: 340 m/s and 295 m/s.

Example 2.3 An airliner flies at 230 m/s true airspeed at 11 km. What is its Mach number?

Show the steps
  1. M = 230/295.0.

Answer: M ≈ 0.78.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Find the air density at 11,000 m with the NASA model (T = −56.46 °C, p = 22.72 kPa).

    Show answer

    ρ = 22.72/(0.2869 × 216.64) ≈ 0.366 kg/m³, about 30% of sea level.

  2. Find the temperature at 1,500 m in the NASA model.

    Show answer

    T = 15.04 − 9.74 = 5.3 °C.

  3. What is Mach 2 at sea level in m/s?

    Show answer

    2 × 340 = 680 m/s.

  4. Find the sea-level density from p = 101.40 kPa and T = 15.04 °C.

    Show answer

    ρ = 101.40/(0.2869 × 288.14) ≈ 1.227 kg/m³.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: The speed of sound depends on pressure.

    Actually: For an ideal gas it depends only on temperature.

  • Myth: It gets colder all the way up.

    Actually: Temperature levels off above 11 km and rises again higher in the stratosphere.

  • Myth: Thinner air always helps.

    Actually: It cuts drag but also lift and engine thrust, so wings must fly faster or at higher lift coefficients.

Flashcards

  • Standard atmosphere

    Average model of T, p, ρ versus altitude.

  • Troposphere

    Ground to ~11 km; temperature falls with height.

  • Lapse rate

    ≈ 6.5 °C per km in the troposphere.

  • Ideal gas law

    p = ρRT.

  • Speed of sound

    a = √(γRT).

  • Mach number

    M = V/a.

How this helps save the planet

Aircraft fly where weather and climate happen. NASA studies aircraft emissions through computer modeling, combustion laboratory testing and flight testing, because what engines release at altitude matters for the atmosphere.

The same atmosphere physics underlies how satellites turn the radiation they measure into profiles of the air’s temperature.

Unit 3 · Weeks 3–4

Basic Aerodynamics: Lift & Drag

Where lift and drag come from, and the two equations that size every wing: L = ½ρV²SC_L and D = ½ρV²SC_D.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Explain lift as the result of turning a flow, with pressure differences described by Bernoulli’s equation.
  • Use the lift and drag equations.
  • Compute dynamic pressure, stall speed and Reynolds number.
  • Distinguish skin-friction, pressure and induced drag.

Key concepts

  • A wing turns the airflow downward; by Newton’s third law the air pushes the wing up. Equivalently, faster flow over the top means lower pressure there (Bernoulli). Both describe one flow.
  • Dynamic pressure q = ½ρV² sets the scale of every aerodynamic force. Lift and drag coefficients C_L and C_D capture shape and angle of attack.
  • Lift grows with V², so halving speed needs four times the lift coefficient or wing area.
  • Drag comes from skin friction, pressure (form) drag and induced drag, the cost of making lift with a finite wing.
  • Reynolds number Re = ρVc/μ compares inertial and viscous effects; wind-tunnel models must account for it to predict full-size behavior.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 A 1,100 kg airplane with a 16.2 m² wing flies level at 55 m/s at sea level (ρ = 1.227 kg/m³). Find C_L.

Show the steps
  1. L = W = 10,791 N.
  2. q = ½ × 1.227 × 55² = 1,855 Pa.
  3. C_L = 10,791/(1,855 × 16.2).

Answer: C_L ≈ 0.36.

Example 3.2 With C_L,max = 1.6, find this airplane’s stall speed at sea level.

Show the steps
  1. V_s = √(2W/(ρSC_L,max)).
  2. = √(2 × 10,791/(1.227 × 16.2 × 1.6)).

Answer: V_s ≈ 26.1 m/s (about 51 knots).

Example 3.3 Find the Reynolds number of a 1.5 m chord at 55 m/s at sea level (μ = 1.789 × 10⁻⁵ kg/(m·s)).

Show the steps
  1. Re = 1.227 × 55 × 1.5/(1.789 × 10⁻⁵).

Answer: Re ≈ 5.7 million.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. If speed doubles at the same C_L, how does lift change?

    Show answer

    Lift ∝ V², so it quadruples.

  2. Find the dynamic pressure at 230 m/s at 11 km (ρ = 0.366 kg/m³).

    Show answer

    q = ½ × 0.366 × 230² ≈ 9,680 Pa.

  3. Find the drag of the Example 3.1 airplane if C_D = 0.032.

    Show answer

    D = 1,855 × 16.2 × 0.032 ≈ 962 N.

  4. How much faster is the stall speed at 1,500 m (ρ = 1.060 kg/m³) than at sea level?

    Show answer

    V_s ∝ 1/√ρ: √(1.227/1.060) = 1.076, so about 7.6% faster (≈ 28.0 m/s true airspeed).

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Air over the top must meet air under the bottom at the trailing edge (equal transit time).

    Actually: It does not; the upper flow actually arrives first. NASA Glenn lists equal transit as an incorrect theory of lift.

  • Myth: Lift is only Bernoulli, or only Newton.

    Actually: Both describe the same flow; pressure differences and flow turning go together.

  • Myth: Drag is just air friction.

    Actually: Pressure drag and induced drag are often larger.

Flashcards

  • Dynamic pressure

    q = ½ρV².

  • Lift equation

    L = qSC_L.

  • Drag equation

    D = qSC_D.

  • Stall speed

    V_s = √(2W/(ρSC_L,max)).

  • Reynolds number

    ρVc/μ.

  • Induced drag

    Drag due to lift on a finite wing.

Self-check quiz

1. Lift is proportional to…

Show answer

V². Through dynamic pressure ½ρV².

Through dynamic pressure ½ρV².

2. Doubling wing area at the same speed and C_L…

Show answer

doubles lift. L ∝ S.

L ∝ S.

3. Reynolds number compares…

Show answer

inertial and viscous effects. ρVc/μ.

ρVc/μ.

4. Induced drag is caused by…

Show answer

Wingtip vortices tilt the lift backward.

How this helps save the planet

Drag is fuel. In cruise, engine thrust equals drag, so every newton of drag removed is fuel not burned and CO₂ not emitted. Needing less energy to fly is the first of NASA’s three paths to sustainable aviation.

The same lift physics drives wind-turbine blades, which turn the wind’s aerodynamic force into clean electricity.

Unit 4 · Week 5

Airfoils & Wings

Airfoil geometry, the lift curve, stall, and why long, slender wings make less drag.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Name airfoil geometry: chord, camber, thickness, angle of attack.
  • Use thin-airfoil theory: c_l ≈ 2πα.
  • Correct the lift slope for a finite wing of aspect ratio AR.
  • Use the drag polar C_D = C_D0 + C_L²/(πeAR) to find the best lift-to-drag ratio.

Key concepts

  • An airfoil is a wing’s cross-section. Camber lets it lift at zero angle of attack; thickness adds strength and room for fuel.
  • Thin-airfoil theory predicts a lift slope of 2π per radian (about 0.11 per degree) until the flow separates and the wing stalls.
  • A finite wing loses lift slope to its tip vortices: a = a₀/(1 + a₀/(πeAR)), with aspect ratio AR = b²/S.
  • Total drag C_D = C_D0 + KC_L² with K = 1/(πeAR). Maximum L/D occurs where induced drag equals parasite drag.
  • High aspect ratio (sailplanes, albatrosses, modern airliner wings) means less induced drag and higher L/D.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 Thin-airfoil theory: find c_l at α = 5°.

Show the steps
  1. α = 5π/180 = 0.0873 rad.
  2. c_l = 2π × 0.0873.

Answer: c_l ≈ 0.548.

Example 4.2 Find the lift slope of a wing with AR = 8 and e = 0.9.

Show the steps
  1. a = 2π/(1 + 2π/(π × 0.9 × 8)) = 6.283/1.278.

Answer: 4.92 per rad ≈ 0.086 per degree, 22% below the 2D value.

Example 4.3 With C_D0 = 0.025, e = 0.8 and AR = 7.5, find (L/D)max and the C_L where it occurs.

Show the steps
  1. K = 1/(π × 0.8 × 7.5) = 0.0531.
  2. (L/D)max = 1/(2√(0.025 × 0.0531)).
  3. C_L* = √(0.025/0.0531).

Answer: (L/D)max ≈ 13.7 at C_L ≈ 0.69.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Find the aspect ratio of a wing with span 11 m and area 16.2 m².

    Show answer

    AR = 121/16.2 ≈ 7.5.

  2. Using c_l = 2πα, what angle gives c_l = 0.5?

    Show answer

    α = 0.5/(2π) = 0.0796 rad ≈ 4.6°.

  3. For the Example 4.3 airplane, find C_D at C_L = 0.36.

    Show answer

    C_D = 0.025 + 0.0531 × 0.1296 ≈ 0.0319.

  4. If AR rises from 7.5 to 15 (same e and C_D0), how does (L/D)max change?

    Show answer

    K halves, so (L/D)max grows by √2 to ≈ 19.4.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: More angle of attack always means more lift.

    Actually: Past the stall angle the flow separates and lift drops.

  • Myth: Symmetric airfoils cannot lift.

    Actually: They lift at a positive angle of attack.

  • Myth: Wingtip devices make induced drag zero.

    Actually: They reduce it; only an infinitely long wing would have none.

Flashcards

  • Chord

    Leading edge to trailing edge.

  • Camber

    Curvature of the mean line.

  • Angle of attack

    Angle between chord and oncoming flow.

  • Stall

    Flow separation; lift drops.

  • Aspect ratio

    b²/S.

  • Drag polar

    C_D = C_D0 + KC_L².

Self-check quiz

1. Thin-airfoil lift slope is…

Show answer

2π per rad. c_l = 2πα.

Show answer

b²/S. Span squared over area.

3. At maximum L/D…

Show answer

How this helps save the planet

Long, slender wings are one of the most direct ways to cut fuel burn, because induced drag falls as aspect ratio rises. That is exactly the “efficient aircraft technology” path in NASA’s sustainable-aviation work.

The albatross, which glides for hours over the ocean, uses the same physics: a long, thin wing with a high lift-to-drag ratio.

Unit 5 · Weeks 6–7

Aircraft Performance

How far, how long, how fast and how steep: gliding, climbing, range and endurance.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Relate glide ratio to L/D.
  • Find thrust and power required in level flight.
  • Compute climb rate from excess power.
  • Use the Breguet range equation.

Key concepts

  • A glider with lift-to-drag ratio L/D travels L/D meters forward for every meter of height lost: tan γ = D/L.
  • Thrust required in level flight equals drag; power required is drag × speed. Excess power becomes climb: rate of climb = (P_avail − P_req)/W.
  • For a jet, range follows the Breguet equation: R = (V/c)(L/D) ln(W_i/W_f). Three levers: engine efficiency (V/c), aerodynamics (L/D) and structures (weight fraction).
  • Endurance (time aloft) is maximized at a different speed than range.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 A sailplane with L/D = 20 is 1,000 m up. How far can it glide in still air, and at what angle?

Show the steps
  1. Distance = 1,000 × 20.
  2. γ = arctan(1/20).

Answer: 20 km, at a glide angle of about 2.9°.

Example 5.2 A jet cruises at 230 m/s with L/D = 17 and thrust-specific fuel consumption c = 0.6 per hour. Burning fuel lowers its weight from W_i to W_i/1.25. Find its range.

Show the steps
  1. c = 0.6/3,600 = 1.667 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹.
  2. R = (230/1.667 × 10⁻⁴) × 17 × ln 1.25.

Answer: ≈ 5,235 km.

Example 5.3 The Example 3.1 airplane, with the Example 4.3 drag polar, has about 957 N of drag at 55 m/s. What power does level flight require?

Show the steps
  1. P = DV = 957 × 55.

Answer: ≈ 52.6 kW (about 71 hp).

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

Unit 6 · Week 8

Stability & Control

Why a well-designed aircraft returns to steady flight by itself, and how pilots and computers control it.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Name the three axes, pitch, roll and yaw, and their control surfaces.
  • Explain static and dynamic stability.
  • Locate the center of gravity and the neutral point; compute static margin.
  • Explain why a tail stabilizes an aircraft.

Key concepts

  • An aircraft rotates about its center of gravity: pitch (elevator), roll (ailerons), yaw (rudder).
  • Static stability: after a disturbance, the aircraft initially tends to return. Dynamic stability: the motion actually dies out over time.
  • Pitch stability needs the center of gravity ahead of the neutral point. Static margin = (x_np − x_cg)/c̄ must be positive.
  • A tail far behind the CG works like the feathers on an arrow: a nose-up disturbance raises the tail’s lift and pushes the nose back down.
  • Too much stability makes an aircraft sluggish and costs trim drag; some modern fighters are designed unstable and flown by computer.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 6.1 The neutral point is at 40% of the mean chord and the CG at 25%. Find the static margin.

Show the steps
  1. SM = 0.40 − 0.25.

Answer: 0.15, or 15%: stable.

Example 6.2 A glider has a 50 g wing at x = 10 cm, a 10 g tail at x = 40 cm and 20 g of nose weight at x = 0. Find the CG.

Show the steps
  1. Σmx = 50(10) + 10(40) + 20(0) = 900 g·cm.
  2. Σm = 80 g.

Answer: x_cg = 11.25 cm from the nose.

Example 6.3 For the Example 6.2 glider (mean chord 8 cm), the neutral point is at 13 cm. Is it stable?

Show the steps
  1. SM = (13 − 11.25)/8.

Answer: SM ≈ 0.22: stable. Remove nose weight and the CG moves aft toward instability.

    Actually: That is dynamic stability; static stability is the initial tendency.

  • Myth: More stability is always better.

    Actually: Too much makes control heavy and wastes trim drag.

  • Myth: The CG can be anywhere as long as the wing lifts.

    Actually: An aft CG makes the aircraft unstable.

Unit 7 · Week 9

Propulsion Basics

How propellers, jets and rockets make thrust by throwing mass backward, and why bigger, slower jets are more efficient.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use the momentum thrust equation.
  • Compute propulsive efficiency.
  • Compare turbojets, turbofans and propellers.
  • Define specific impulse and thrust-specific fuel consumption.

Key concepts

  • Every engine makes thrust by accelerating a mass flow: F = ṁ(v_e − v₀) for an air-breathing engine (ignoring fuel mass and pressure terms).
  • Propulsive efficiency η_p = 2/(1 + v_e/v₀): moving a lot of air a little is more efficient than moving a little air a lot. That is why turbofans have huge fans.
  • Turbojet: all air passes through the core. Turbofan: most air bypasses the core through the fan. Turboprop: a propeller driven by a gas turbine.
  • Specific impulse I_sp = F/(ṁ_fuel g₀) measures thrust per fuel weight flow, in seconds.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 7.1 A turbofan moves 500 kg/s of air from 250 m/s to 320 m/s. Find thrust and propulsive efficiency.

Show the steps
  1. F = 500 × 70 = 35,000 N.
  2. η_p = 2/(1 + 320/250) = 2/2.28.

Answer: F = 35 kN, η_p ≈ 0.88.

Example 7.2 A turbojet makes the same 35 kN with 100 kg/s of air at v₀ = 250 m/s. What exhaust speed does it need, and what is η_p?

Show the steps
  1. v_e = 250 + 35,000/100 = 600 m/s.
  2. η_p = 2/(1 + 2.4).

Answer: v_e = 600 m/s, η_p ≈ 0.59: much less efficient.

Example 7.3 An engine makes 100 kN while burning 2.5 kg/s of fuel. Find its I_sp.

Show the steps
  1. I_sp = 100,000/(2.5 × 9.80665).

Answer: ≈ 4,079 s. Air-breathers beat rockets on this measure because they do not carry their oxidizer.

    Show answer

  3. Why do airliners use high-bypass turbofans?

    Show answer

    They move a large mass of air slowly, which raises propulsive efficiency and cuts fuel burn and noise.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Jets push against the air behind them.

    Actually: They accelerate air through the engine; the reaction to that is thrust.

  • Myth: Faster exhaust always means a better engine.

    Actually: For efficiency, exhaust speed should be close to flight speed.

  • Myth: Specific impulse is a speed.

    Actually: It is measured in seconds; I_sp × g₀ is an effective exhaust speed.

    Bypass air ÷ core air.

    F/(ṁ g₀), in seconds.

  • TSFC

    Fuel flow per unit thrust.

Unit 8 · Weeks 10–11

Rockets & the Rocket Equation

Why reaching orbit is so hard: Tsiolkovsky’s rocket equation, mass ratios and staging.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use Δv = I_sp g₀ ln(m₀/m_f).
  • Compute required mass ratios.
  • Explain why staging works.
  • Compute rocket thrust-to-weight ratio.

Key concepts

  • A rocket carries all its propellant, fuel and oxidizer. Its thrust is F = ṁv_e + (p_e − p_a)A_e.
  • The ideal rocket equation: Δv = v_e ln(m₀/m_f), with v_e = I_sp g₀. Speed grows only with the logarithm of the mass ratio.
  • Reaching low Earth orbit takes roughly 9.4 km/s of Δv once gravity and drag losses are included, so most of a launch vehicle’s mass is propellant.
  • Staging drops empty tanks and engines so later stages do not carry dead weight.
  • Thrust-to-weight ratio must exceed 1 to lift off.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 8.1 An engine with I_sp = 450 s fires until m₀/m_f = 5. Find Δv.

Show the steps
  1. v_e = 450 × 9.80665 = 4,413 m/s.
  2. Δv = 4,413 × ln 5.

Answer: ≈ 7,102 m/s.

Example 8.2 What mass ratio does a single stage need for 9.4 km/s with I_sp = 300 s? With 450 s?

Show the steps
  1. 300 s: e^(9,400/2,942) = e^3.195.
  2. 450 s: e^(9,400/4,413) = e^2.130.

Answer: ≈ 24.4 versus ≈ 8.4: about 96% versus 88% of the rocket must be propellant.

Example 8.3 A two-stage rocket: each stage has I_sp = 300 s and mass ratio 4. Find the total Δv.

Show the steps
  1. Each stage: 2,942 × ln 4 = 4,078 m/s.
  2. Add the stages.

Answer: ≈ 8,157 m/s, twice what one stage with mass ratio 4 gives.

    Show answer

  3. What I_sp gives Δv = 3,000 m/s with mass ratio 2?

    Show answer

    3,000/(9.80665 × ln 2) ≈ 441 s.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Rockets push against the air.

    Actually: They work best in vacuum; they push on their own exhaust.

  • Myth: Double the propellant, double the Δv.

    Actually: Δv grows with the logarithm of the mass ratio.

  • Myth: Orbit needs only 7.7 km/s of Δv.

    Actually: Gravity and drag losses push the total to roughly 9.4 km/s.

Unit 9 · Weeks 12–13

Orbits & Space Missions

Circular and elliptical orbits, periods, geostationary orbit and the Hohmann transfer, plus how Earth-observing missions choose their orbits.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute circular orbital speed and period.
  • Find the geostationary radius.
  • Compute a Hohmann transfer Δv and transfer time.
  • Describe common Earth orbits and their uses.

Key concepts

  • In a circular orbit gravity supplies the centripetal force: v = √(μ/r), with μ = GM.
  • Kepler’s third law: T = 2π√(a³/μ). Higher orbits are slower and take longer.
  • A geostationary orbit has a period of one sidereal day (86,164 s) and sits over the equator at a radius of about 42,164 km.
  • A Hohmann transfer uses two burns along an ellipse that touches both circular orbits; it is the most efficient two-burn transfer between coplanar circular orbits.
  • Low Earth orbit (about 400 km for the ISS), sun-synchronous polar orbits for imaging, and geostationary orbit for weather each serve different missions.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 9.1 Find the speed and period of a circular orbit 400 km up.

Show the steps
  1. r = 6,771 km.
  2. v = √(3.986 × 10¹⁴/6.771 × 10⁶) = 7,673 m/s.
  3. T = 2π√(r³/μ) = 5,545 s.

Answer: 7.67 km/s and ≈ 92.4 minutes.

Example 9.2 Find the geostationary radius.

Show the steps
  1. r = (μT²/4π²)^(1/3) with T = 86,164 s.

Answer: ≈ 42,164 km from Earth’s center (about 35,786 km above the equator).

Example 9.3 Find the Hohmann Δv from a 300 km circular orbit to geostationary radius.

Show the steps
  1. r₁ = 6,671 km, r₂ = 42,164 km.
  2. Δv₁ = 2,428 m/s; Δv₂ = 1,468 m/s.
  3. Transfer time = half the transfer ellipse’s period.

Answer: Total ≈ 3.90 km/s; transfer ≈ 5.27 hours (ignoring any plane change).

  • Myth: Higher orbits need more speed.

    Actually: Circular speed falls with height, though reaching higher orbits takes more total Δv.

Lab C: Design, build and fly a glider

Week 8

Goal: Design a card-stock or foam glider, set its CG for a positive static margin, then measure its glide ratio.

Procedure

  1. Sketch a design: pick a wing span and chord for an aspect ratio between 5 and 8, a tail about 20% of the wing area, and a fuselage stick.
  2. Build it. Find the CG by balancing it on a finger; add nose weight until the CG is about 25% of the wing chord behind the wing’s leading edge.
  3. Launch gently and level from a measured height in still air, at least five times; record each distance.
  4. Compute L/D. Then move the CG aft in small steps and record what happens.

Midterm practice set (Units 1–5)

Glossary

Angle of attack
Angle between the chord line and the oncoming air.
Aspect ratio
Span squared divided by wing area.
Breguet range equation
Range of a jet from speed, fuel use, L/D and weight fraction.
Camber
Curvature of an airfoil’s mean line.
Center of gravity
Point where the weight acts.
Drag polar
C_D as a function of C_L.
Dynamic pressure
½ρV².
Geostationary orbit
Equatorial orbit with a one-sidereal-day period.
Hohmann transfer
Two-burn elliptical transfer between circular orbits.
Induced drag
Drag caused by producing lift with a finite wing.
Mach number
Speed divided by the local speed of sound.
Neutral point
CG position giving neutral pitch stability.
Propulsive efficiency
Fraction of jet power that becomes useful thrust power.
Reynolds number
Ratio of inertial to viscous effects, ρVc/μ.
Rocket equation
Δv = I_sp g₀ ln(m₀/m_f).
Specific impulse
Thrust per weight flow of propellant, in seconds.
Stall
Loss of lift when flow separates from the wing.
Standard atmosphere
Average model of air properties versus altitude.
Static margin
Distance from CG to neutral point in mean chords.
Sustainable aviation fuel
Jet fuel made from wastes or biomass.
