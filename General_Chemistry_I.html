About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows the first semester of college general chemistry as taught in open materials such as OpenStax Chemistry 2e and MIT OpenCourseWare, which we link to throughout. Those organizations do not endorse this page.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

Each unit ends with a short, sourced section showing how its ideas help both people and nature.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Algebra: solving for one unknown, rearranging formulas, ratios and percentages.
  • Scientific notation and logarithms (base 10). You will use log for pH and absorbance.
  • Units: converting grams to kilograms, mL to L, °C to K.
  • A scientific calculator. A phone calculator in scientific mode works.

How to study

  • Read the unit, then close it and try the worked examples yourself before checking each step.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer. Getting it wrong first and fixing it is how the method sticks.
  • Always carry units through a calculation. If the units do not cancel to the answer you want, the setup is wrong.
  • Check significant figures: your answer should not be more precise than your least precise measurement.
  • Use the flashcards for 10 minutes a day (spaced practice beats one long cram).
  • Take the unit quiz only when you think you are ready. Below 80 percent, review the misconceptions and try again tomorrow.
  • Explain one idea out loud each day, as if teaching it. If you get stuck, that is the part to review.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • Digital balance (to 0.001 g)
  • pH meter or probe
  • Spectrophotometer or colorimeter with cuvettes
  • Burette, volumetric pipets and flasks
  • Crucible, lid, clay triangle and burner
  • Safety goggles, gloves and lab coat

Unit 1 · Weeks 1–3

Atomic Theory & Quantum Numbers

What atoms are made of, how electrons are arranged, and why each element gives off its own colors of light.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Find protons, neutrons and electrons from an isotope symbol, and compute an average atomic mass.
  • Use c = λν and E = hν to move between wavelength, frequency and photon energy.
  • Find the wavelength of light given off when a hydrogen electron drops between levels.
  • List the allowed quantum numbers (n, l, mₗ, mₛ) and spot impossible sets.
  • Write electron configurations and orbital diagrams, including ions and noble-gas shorthand.
  • Explain periodic trends in atomic radius and ionization energy.

Key concepts

  • An atom has a tiny nucleus of protons and neutrons with electrons around it. The atomic number Z counts protons; the mass number A counts protons plus neutrons.
  • Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. The atomic mass on the periodic table is a weighted average of the isotopes found in nature.
  • Light behaves as a wave (wavelength λ, frequency ν) and as particles called photons, each carrying energy E = hν.
  • Electrons in an atom can only have certain energies. When an electron drops to a lower level it gives off a photon whose energy equals the gap, so every element has its own line spectrum.
  • Four quantum numbers describe each electron: n (shell, 1, 2, 3…), l (subshell shape, 0 to n − 1, named s, p, d, f), mₗ (orientation, −l to +l) and mₛ (spin, +½ or −½).
  • Pauli exclusion principle: no two electrons in an atom share all four quantum numbers, so an orbital holds at most two electrons, with opposite spins.
  • Aufbau principle: fill the lowest-energy orbitals first (1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p…). Hund’s rule: in equal-energy orbitals, place electrons singly with parallel spins before pairing.
  • Across a period, atoms get smaller and ionization energy generally rises because the nuclear charge felt by the outer electrons grows. Down a group, atoms get larger and ionization energy falls.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 What wavelength of light does a hydrogen atom give off when its electron drops from n = 3 to n = 2?

Show the steps
  1. Energy gap: |ΔE| = k(1/2² − 1/3²) = 2.179 × 10⁻¹⁸ J × (1/4 − 1/9) = 2.179 × 10⁻¹⁸ J × 5/36 = 3.026 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  2. Rearrange E = hc/λ to λ = hc/E.
  3. λ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s × 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s) ÷ 3.026 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 6.564 × 10⁻⁷ m.
  4. Convert: 6.564 × 10⁻⁷ m = 656 nm.

Answer: 656 nm, red light. This is the bright red line in the hydrogen spectrum that astronomers use to spot hydrogen gas in space.

Example 1.2 Write the electron configuration of iron (Fe, Z = 26) and of the Fe²⁺ ion. How many unpaired electrons does Fe have?

Show the steps
  1. Fill in order with 26 electrons: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d⁶.
  2. Shorthand: argon has 18 electrons, so Fe = [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶.
  3. Hund’s rule for 3d⁶: five d orbitals get one electron each, then one pairs up. That leaves 4 unpaired electrons.
  4. Transition metals lose their 4s electrons first when they form ions, so Fe²⁺ = [Ar] 3d⁶.

Answer: Fe = [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶ with 4 unpaired electrons; Fe²⁺ = [Ar] 3d⁶.

Example 1.3 Chlorine is 75.76% chlorine-35 (34.969 u) and 24.24% chlorine-37 (36.966 u). What is its average atomic mass?

Show the steps
  1. Convert percentages to fractions: 0.7576 and 0.2424.
  2. Multiply each mass by its fraction: 0.7576 × 34.969 u = 26.49 u; 0.2424 × 36.966 u = 8.961 u.
  3. Add: 26.49 u + 8.961 u = 35.45 u.

Answer: 35.45 u, which matches the periodic table value for chlorine.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in one atom of carbon-14?

    Show answer

    6 protons, 8 neutrons (14 − 6) and 6 electrons.

  2. A green laser has a wavelength of 532 nm. Find its frequency and the energy of one photon.

    Show answer

    ν = c/λ = (2.998 × 10⁸ m/s) ÷ (532 × 10⁻⁹ m) = 5.64 × 10¹⁴ Hz. E = hν = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s × 5.64 × 10¹⁴ Hz = 3.73 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

  3. List the allowed values of mₗ when l = 2.

    Show answer

    −2, −1, 0, +1, +2 (five d orbitals).

  4. Which set of quantum numbers is impossible: (a) n = 2, l = 1, mₗ = −1; (b) n = 2, l = 2, mₗ = 0; (c) n = 3, l = 0, mₗ = 0?

    Show answer

    (b). l must be less than n, so n = 2 allows only l = 0 or 1.

  5. What is the maximum number of electrons in the n = 3 shell?

    Show answer

    2n² = 2 × 3² = 18 (3s holds 2, 3p holds 6, 3d holds 10).

  6. Write the configuration of sulfur (Z = 16) and count its unpaired electrons.

    Show answer

    [Ne] 3s² 3p⁴. The four 3p electrons fill as ↑↓ ↑ ↑, so 2 are unpaired.

  7. Which is larger, a sodium atom or a chlorine atom? Why?

    Show answer

    Sodium. Both are in period 3, but chlorine has 6 more protons pulling the same shell of electrons closer.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Electrons orbit the nucleus in neat circles, like planets.

    Actually: An orbital is a region where an electron is likely to be found, not a path. The Bohr model’s circles work only for hydrogen’s energy levels.

  • Myth: The 4s electrons stay put when a transition metal forms an ion, because 4s filled first.

    Actually: Transition metals lose 4s electrons before 3d ones. Fe²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶, not [Ar] 4s² 3d⁴.

  • Myth: Higher-frequency light has a longer wavelength.

    Actually: Frequency and wavelength are inversely related (c = λν). Higher frequency means shorter wavelength and more energy per photon.

  • Myth: The atomic mass on the periodic table is the mass of every atom of that element.

    Actually: The table shows a weighted average of the natural isotopes. Individual atoms have the mass of their own isotope.

Flashcards

  • Atomic number (Z)

    Number of protons in the nucleus; it defines the element.

  • Isotope

    Atoms of one element with different numbers of neutrons.

  • E = hν

    Energy of one photon equals Planck’s constant times its frequency.

  • Pauli exclusion principle

    No two electrons in an atom have the same four quantum numbers; max two per orbital.

  • Hund’s rule

    Fill equal-energy orbitals singly, spins parallel, before pairing.

  • Allowed l values

    0 up to n − 1 (s = 0, p = 1, d = 2, f = 3).

  • Ionization energy

    Energy needed to remove an electron from a gaseous atom; rises across a period.

  • Line spectrum

    The set of wavelengths an element gives off; a fingerprint used to identify it.

How this helps save the planet

Every gas absorbs and gives off light at its own wavelengths, the idea behind atomic line spectra. Satellite spectrometers use those fingerprints to measure gases from orbit.

NASA’s EMIT instrument on the International Space Station uses imaging spectroscopy to find large methane plumes, which helps people find and fix leaks. That protects the climate for people and nature alike.

NASA’s OCO-2 satellite measures carbon dioxide by how much sunlight CO₂ absorbs at specific wavelengths.

Unit 2 · Weeks 4–6

Stoichiometry

The math of chemical reactions: counting atoms by weighing, balancing equations, and predicting how much product you get. You will use this later for combustion and rocket propulsion.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Convert between mass, moles and number of particles using molar mass and Avogadro’s number.
  • Balance chemical equations by inspection, including combustion reactions.
  • Use mole ratios to find the mass of a product or reactant.
  • Identify the limiting reactant and compute theoretical and percent yield.
  • Compute molarity and solve dilution problems.

Key concepts

  • A mole is 6.02214076 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro’s number, exact by definition). The molar mass in g/mol equals the formula mass in atomic mass units.
  • A balanced equation has the same number of each kind of atom on both sides. Change only the coefficients, never the subscripts.
  • Coefficients give mole ratios, not mass ratios. Always convert grams to moles before using them.
  • The limiting reactant runs out first and sets the maximum amount of product (the theoretical yield). Percent yield compares what you actually got with that maximum.
  • Molarity (M) is moles of solute per liter of solution. Diluting keeps the moles of solute the same, so M₁V₁ = M₂V₂.
  • In complete combustion, a hydrocarbon reacts with oxygen to give carbon dioxide and water. The oxygen comes from the air, which is why the CO₂ produced weighs more than the fuel burned.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 Methane burns: CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. Balance it, then find the mass of CO₂ made by burning 1.00 kg of methane.

Show the steps
  1. Balance C: 1 CH₄ gives 1 CO₂. Balance H: 4 H need 2 H₂O. Balance O: 2 (in CO₂) + 2 (in 2 H₂O) = 4 O atoms, so 2 O₂.
  2. Balanced: CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O.
  3. Moles of CH₄: 1000 g ÷ 16.04 g/mol = 62.34 mol.
  4. Mole ratio CO₂ : CH₄ = 1 : 1, so 62.34 mol CO₂.
  5. Mass of CO₂: 62.34 mol × 44.01 g/mol = 2,744 g.

Answer: About 2.74 kg of CO₂ from 1.00 kg of methane. The extra mass is oxygen taken from the air.

Example 2.2 Hydrogen–oxygen rocket engines run on 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O. If 10.0 g of H₂ reacts with 64.0 g of O₂, which is limiting, how much water forms, and what is left over?

Show the steps
  1. Moles: H₂ = 10.0 g ÷ 2.016 g/mol = 4.960 mol; O₂ = 64.0 g ÷ 32.00 g/mol = 2.000 mol.
  2. To use all 2.000 mol O₂ you need 2 × 2.000 = 4.000 mol H₂. You have 4.960 mol, so O₂ runs out first: O₂ is limiting.
  3. Water: 2 mol H₂O per mol O₂, so 4.000 mol H₂O × 18.02 g/mol = 72.1 g.
  4. Left over: 4.960 − 4.000 = 0.960 mol H₂ × 2.016 g/mol = 1.94 g H₂.
  5. Check: 72.1 g + 1.94 g = 74.0 g, the same as 10.0 g + 64.0 g. Mass is conserved.

Answer: O₂ is limiting; 72.1 g of water forms and 1.94 g of H₂ is left over.

Example 2.3 How do you make 250.0 mL of 0.100 M NaOH from a 1.00 M stock solution?

Show the steps
  1. Use M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ with M₁ = 1.00 M, M₂ = 0.100 M, V₂ = 250.0 mL.
  2. V₁ = (0.100 M × 250.0 mL) ÷ 1.00 M = 25.0 mL.
  3. Measure 25.0 mL of stock with a volumetric pipet into a 250.0 mL volumetric flask and add water to the mark.

Answer: 25.0 mL of stock, diluted to 250.0 mL.

  4. How many molecules are in 18.02 g of water?

    Show answer

  5. What is the molarity of 5.844 g of NaCl dissolved to make 250.0 mL of solution?

    Show answer

  6. 25.0 mL of 6.00 M HCl is diluted to 150.0 mL. What is the new concentration?

  7. A reaction’s theoretical yield is 72.1 g, but you collect 65.0 g. What is the percent yield?

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: The reactant with the smaller mass is the limiting reactant.

    Actually: Compare moles against the mole ratio. In the rocket example, H₂ had less mass but O₂ was limiting.

  • Myth: You can balance an equation by changing subscripts, like writing H₂O₂ instead of H₂O.

    Actually: Subscripts define the substance. Changing them makes a different chemical. Change only coefficients.

  • Myth: Burning 1 kg of fuel cannot make more than 1 kg of products.

    Actually: Oxygen from the air joins the fuel, so the products weigh more than the fuel alone (mass is still conserved overall).

  • Myth: Molarity uses the volume of water added.

    Actually: Molarity uses the final volume of the solution, which is why you fill a volumetric flask to the mark.

Unit 3 · Weeks 7–9

Molecular Geometry & Bonding

Why atoms bond, how to draw molecules, what shapes they take, and the forces between molecules that decide whether something is a gas, liquid or solid.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Classify bonds as ionic, polar covalent or nonpolar covalent using electronegativity.
  • Draw Lewis structures and check them with formal charge.
  • Predict electron-pair and molecular geometry with VSEPR theory.
  • Decide whether a molecule is polar from its bonds and shape.
  • Rank substances by boiling point using intermolecular forces.

Key concepts

  • Ionic bonds form when electrons transfer between atoms with very different electronegativities (for example Na and Cl). Covalent bonds share electron pairs.
  • Electronegativity is an atom’s pull on shared electrons. Unequal sharing makes a polar bond with partial charges δ+ and δ−.
  • Lewis structures show valence electrons as bonds and lone pairs. Most main-group atoms end with eight electrons around them (the octet rule); hydrogen ends with two.
  • Formal charge helps choose the best Lewis structure: the best one has formal charges as close to zero as possible.
  • VSEPR: electron groups (bonds and lone pairs) around a central atom spread out as far as possible. 2 groups: linear, 180°. 3: trigonal planar, 120°. 4: tetrahedral, 109.5°. 5: trigonal bipyramidal. 6: octahedral.
  • Lone pairs take up more room than bonds, so they squeeze bond angles: water is bent at about 104.5°, ammonia is trigonal pyramidal at about 107°.
  • A molecule is polar if its bond dipoles do not cancel. CO₂ has polar bonds but is linear, so it is nonpolar overall; H₂O is bent, so it is polar.
  • Intermolecular forces, weakest to strongest for similar sizes: London dispersion (all molecules, larger with more electrons), dipole–dipole (polar molecules), hydrogen bonding (H on N, O or F). Stronger forces mean higher boiling points.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 Draw the Lewis structure of CO₂ and predict its shape and polarity.

Show the steps
  1. Count valence electrons: C has 4, each O has 6: 4 + 2 × 6 = 16.
  2. Put C in the center and connect each O with a double bond: O=C=O uses 8 electrons.
  3. Place the remaining 8 as two lone pairs on each O. Every atom now has an octet.
  4. Formal charges: C = 4 − 0 − 8/2 = 0; each O = 6 − 4 − 4/2 = 0. A good structure.
  5. Two electron groups on C and no lone pairs: linear, 180°. The two C=O dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel.

Answer: O=C=O, linear and nonpolar overall, even though each bond is polar.

Example 3.2 Predict the shape, bond angle and polarity of water.

Show the steps
  1. Valence electrons: 2 × 1 + 6 = 8.
  2. Two O–H bonds use 4 electrons; the other 4 are two lone pairs on O.
  3. Four electron groups: tetrahedral electron geometry. With two lone pairs, the molecular shape is bent.
  4. Lone pairs push the bonds closer: angle about 104.5°, less than 109.5°.
  5. The bond dipoles do not cancel in a bent shape, so water is polar and can hydrogen bond.

Answer: Bent, about 104.5°, polar.

Example 3.3 Which has the higher boiling point: methane (CH₄) or octane (C₈H₁₈)? Why?

Show the steps
  1. Both are nonpolar hydrocarbons, so the main force is London dispersion.
  2. Octane has many more electrons and a longer shape, so its dispersion forces are much stronger.

Answer: Octane. That is why octane is a liquid at room temperature while methane is a gas.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: A molecule with polar bonds must be polar.

    Actually: Shape matters. CO₂ and CCl₄ have polar bonds but symmetric shapes, so they are nonpolar.

  • Myth: Boiling breaks the bonds inside molecules.

    Actually: Boiling water separates H₂O molecules from each other. The O–H bonds inside each molecule stay intact.

  • Myth: Lone pairs do not affect shape because you cannot see them in the formula.

    Actually: Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonds and bend the shape, as in H₂O and NH₃.

  • Myth: Hydrogen bonds are bonds to hydrogen atoms in any molecule.

    Actually: They need H bonded to N, O or F, attracted to a lone pair on another N, O or F.

How this helps save the planet

Molecular shape explains the greenhouse effect. Molecules such as CO₂, CH₄ and H₂O can bend or stretch in ways that change their dipole, so they absorb infrared heat radiating from Earth. N₂ and O₂, which make up most of the air, cannot.

Hydrogen bonding makes ice less dense than liquid water, so ice floats. Lakes freeze from the top down, and fish and other life survive the winter in the water below.

Intermolecular forces also guide cleanups: oil is nonpolar and does not mix with water, which is why spills spread as surface slicks.

Unit 4 · Weeks 10–12

States of Matter: Gases & Solutions

How gases respond to pressure and temperature, how gas mixtures work, and how substances (including oxygen and CO₂) dissolve in water.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use Boyle’s, Charles’s and the combined gas law, always in kelvin.
  • Solve problems with the ideal gas law, PV = nRT, including molar mass from density.
  • Use Dalton’s law of partial pressures.
  • Explain gas behavior with kinetic-molecular theory.
  • Describe solubility, and use Henry’s law for gases dissolving in liquids.

Key concepts

  • Gas laws need absolute temperature: T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15.
  • At constant temperature, pressure and volume are inversely related (Boyle). At constant pressure, volume is proportional to kelvin temperature (Charles).
  • The ideal gas law, PV = nRT, ties pressure, volume, moles and temperature together. At 0 °C and 1 atm, one mole of an ideal gas takes up 22.4 L.
  • In a gas mixture, each gas exerts its own partial pressure, and they add up to the total (Dalton’s law). Dry air is about 20.95% oxygen by volume.
  • Kinetic-molecular theory: gas particles move fast and randomly, collide elastically, and their average kinetic energy is proportional to kelvin temperature.
  • A solution is a uniform mixture. “Like dissolves like”: polar solvents such as water dissolve polar and ionic substances.
  • The amount of a gas that dissolves in a liquid rises with that gas’s partial pressure (Henry’s law) and usually falls as the water warms.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 What volume does 1.00 mol of an ideal gas occupy at 25.0 °C and 1.00 atm?

Show the steps
  1. Convert temperature: 25.0 + 273.15 = 298.2 K.
  2. V = nRT/P = 1.00 mol × 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K) × 298.2 K ÷ 1.00 atm.
  3. V = 24.5 L.

Answer: 24.5 L, a bit more than the 22.4 L at 0 °C because warmer gas expands.

Example 4.2 A weather balloon holds 2.00 L of helium at 1.00 atm and 300 K. It rises to where the pressure is 0.500 atm and the temperature is 250 K. What is its new volume?

Show the steps
  1. Combined gas law solved for V₂: V₂ = V₁ × (P₁/P₂) × (T₂/T₁).
  2. V₂ = 2.00 L × (1.00/0.500) × (250/300).
  3. V₂ = 2.00 L × 2 × 0.8333 = 3.33 L.

Answer: 3.33 L. Lower pressure lets it expand more than the cold shrinks it.

Example 4.3 Estimate how much oxygen dissolves in water at 25 °C open to air at 1.00 atm. Henry’s constant for O₂ is about 1.3 × 10⁻³ mol/(L·atm).

Show the steps
  1. Partial pressure of O₂: 0.2095 × 1.00 atm = 0.21 atm.
  2. C = k_H × P = 1.3 × 10⁻³ mol/(L·atm) × 0.21 atm = 2.7 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L.
  3. Convert to mass: 2.7 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L × 32.00 g/mol = 8.7 × 10⁻³ g/L = 8.7 mg/L.

Answer: About 9 mg of oxygen per liter: a small amount, and all that fish and other aquatic life have to breathe.

    Actually: Always use kelvin. At 0 °C the volume is not zero; at 0 K it would be.

Unit 5 · Weeks 13–14

Thermodynamics

Chemical energy: heat, enthalpy, why some reactions release energy, and a first look at entropy and Gibbs free energy.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use q = mcΔT and calorimetry to find heat flow and specific heat.
  • Classify processes as exothermic or endothermic and give the sign of ΔH.
  • Use Hess’s law and standard enthalpies of formation to find ΔH of a reaction.
  • State the first law of thermodynamics with the chemistry sign convention.
  • Use ΔG = ΔH − TΔS to judge whether a process is spontaneous.

Key concepts

  • Energy is conserved (the first law): ΔU = q + w. In chemistry, q is heat added to the system and w is work done on the system.
  • Exothermic reactions release heat to the surroundings (ΔH < 0); endothermic reactions absorb heat (ΔH > 0).
  • Specific heat c is the heat needed to warm 1 g by 1 °C. Water’s is high, 4.184 J/(g·°C), which is why oceans and lakes warm and cool slowly.
  • Enthalpy is a state function, so ΔH depends only on start and end. That is why Hess’s law works: you can add reactions and add their ΔH values.
  • Standard enthalpy of formation (ΔH°f) is the enthalpy change to make 1 mol of a compound from its elements in their standard states. For an element in its standard state, ΔH°f = 0.
  • Entropy (S) measures how spread out energy and matter are. The Gibbs free energy change, ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, is negative for a spontaneous process at constant temperature and pressure.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 How much heat does it take to warm 250.0 g of water from 20.0 °C to 100.0 °C?

Show the steps
  1. ΔT = 100.0 − 20.0 = 80.0 °C.
  2. q = mcΔT = 250.0 g × 4.184 J/(g·°C) × 80.0 °C = 83,680 J.
  3. In kilojoules: 83.7 kJ.

Answer: 83.7 kJ.

Example 5.2 Find ΔH° for burning methane, CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l), using ΔH°f values: CH₄(g) −74.6, CO₂(g) −393.5, H₂O(l) −285.8 kJ/mol; O₂(g) 0.

Show the steps
  1. Products: −393.5 + 2 × (−285.8) = −965.1 kJ.
  2. Reactants: −74.6 + 2 × 0 = −74.6 kJ.
  3. ΔH° = −965.1 − (−74.6) = −890.5 kJ.

Answer: About −890 kJ per mole of methane: strongly exothermic.

Example 5.3 Compare the heat released per gram by methane and by hydrogen. For H₂(g) + ½ O₂(g) → H₂O(l), ΔH° = −285.8 kJ.

Show the steps
  1. Methane: 890.5 kJ ÷ 16.04 g = 55.5 kJ/g.
  2. Hydrogen: 285.8 kJ ÷ 2.016 g = 141.8 kJ/g.
  3. Products: methane makes CO₂; hydrogen makes only water.

Answer: Hydrogen releases about 2.6 times more heat per gram, and burning it makes no CO₂. Whether hydrogen is clean overall depends on how it was produced.

Example 5.4 A reaction has ΔH = +40.0 kJ and ΔS = +120 J/K. Is it spontaneous at 298 K? Above what temperature does it become spontaneous?

Show the steps
  1. Use the same units: ΔS = 0.120 kJ/K.
  2. At 298 K: ΔG = 40.0 − 298 × 0.120 = 40.0 − 35.8 = +4.2 kJ. Positive, so not spontaneous.
  3. Set ΔG = 0: T = ΔH/ΔS = 40.0 ÷ 0.120 = 333 K.

Answer: Not spontaneous at 298 K; spontaneous above about 333 K (60 °C).

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Heat and temperature are the same thing.

    Actually: Temperature measures average particle energy; heat is energy transferred. A bathtub of warm water holds more heat than a cup of boiling water.

  • Myth: Exothermic reactions always happen on their own.

    Actually: Spontaneity depends on ΔG, which includes entropy. Some exothermic reactions are not spontaneous at high temperatures.

  • Myth: A spontaneous reaction is a fast one.

    Actually: Spontaneous tells you the direction, not the speed. Diamond turning into graphite is spontaneous but far too slow to notice.

  • Myth: Breaking bonds releases energy.

    Actually: Breaking bonds always takes energy. Energy is released when new, stronger bonds form.

    Show full solution
    1. The simple filling order predicts [Ar] 4s² 3d⁹, but a full 3d subshell is more stable.
    2. One 4s electron moves to 3d.

    Answer: [Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰, a known exception.

  3. How much CO₂ is produced by burning 1.00 kg of octane (C₈H₁₈, 114.23 g/mol)?

    Show full solution
    1. Balanced: 2 C₈H₁₈ + 25 O₂ → 16 CO₂ + 18 H₂O, so 8 mol CO₂ per mol octane.
    2. Moles octane = 1000 g ÷ 114.23 g/mol = 8.754 mol.
    3. CO₂ = 8 × 8.754 = 70.04 mol × 44.01 g/mol = 3,082 g.

    Answer: About 3.08 kg of CO₂.

  4. 28.0 g of N₂ reacts with 9.00 g of H₂: N₂ + 3 H₂ → 2 NH₃. Find the limiting reactant and the mass of NH₃.

    Show full solution
    1. N₂: 28.0 ÷ 28.02 = 0.999 mol. H₂: 9.00 ÷ 2.016 = 4.46 mol.
    2. N₂ needs 3 × 0.999 = 3.00 mol H₂; 4.46 mol is available, so N₂ is limiting.
    3. NH₃ = 2 × 0.999 = 2.00 mol × 17.03 g/mol = 34.0 g.

    Answer: N₂ is limiting; 34.0 g NH₃.

  5. Give the shape and polarity of SO₂ and of CCl₄.

    Show full solution
    1. SO₂: S has two bonding groups and one lone pair, so it is bent and the dipoles do not cancel.
    2. CCl₄: four identical bonds, tetrahedral, dipoles cancel.

    Answer: SO₂ bent and polar; CCl₄ tetrahedral and nonpolar.

  6. Rank by boiling point, lowest first: H₂O, He, CH₄.

    Show full solution
    1. He: only weak dispersion, very few electrons.
    2. CH₄: dispersion forces, more electrons than He.
    3. H₂O: hydrogen bonding.

    Answer: He < CH₄ < H₂O.

Final practice set (all units)

  1. What volume does 88.0 g of CO₂ occupy at 25.0 °C and 1.00 atm?

    Show full solution
    1. n = 88.0 ÷ 44.01 = 2.00 mol.
    2. V = nRT/P = 2.00 × 0.08206 × 298.15 ÷ 1.00.

    Answer: 48.9 L.

  2. How many grams of methane must burn (ΔH = −890.5 kJ/mol) to warm 500.0 g of water from 15.0 °C to 85.0 °C, if all the heat goes into the water?

    Show full solution
    1. q = 500.0 × 4.184 × 70.0 = 146,440 J = 146.4 kJ.
    2. Moles CH₄ = 146.4 ÷ 890.5 = 0.1644 mol.
    3. Mass = 0.1644 × 16.04 = 2.64 g.

    Answer: 2.64 g of methane.

  3. 20.00 mL of sulfuric acid is neutralized by 31.60 mL of 0.1000 M NaOH. Find the acid’s molarity.

    Show full solution
    1. H₂SO₄ + 2 NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2 H₂O (two acidic hydrogens).
    2. Moles NaOH = 0.1000 × 0.03160 = 3.160 × 10⁻³ mol, so moles H₂SO₄ = 1.580 × 10⁻³ mol.
    3. M = 1.580 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.02000 L.

    Answer: 0.07900 M. A common mistake is to forget the 1 : 2 ratio and get 0.1580 M.

  4. A dye has ε = 1.20 × 10⁴ M⁻¹ cm⁻¹. A sample in a 1.00 cm cuvette reads A = 0.300. Find its concentration.

    Show full solution
    1. c = A ÷ (εb) = 0.300 ÷ (1.20 × 10⁴ × 1.00).

    Answer: 2.50 × 10⁻⁵ M.

  5. A compound is 40.0% C, 6.71% H and 53.3% O by mass, with a molar mass of 180.16 g/mol. Find its empirical and molecular formulas.

    Show full solution
    1. In 100 g: C 40.0 ÷ 12.011 = 3.33 mol; H 6.71 ÷ 1.008 = 6.66 mol; O 53.3 ÷ 16.00 = 3.33 mol.
    2. Divide by 3.33: 1 : 2 : 1, so CH₂O (30.03 g/mol).
    3. 180.16 ÷ 30.03 = 6.

    Answer: Empirical CH₂O; molecular C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose).

  6. Use Hess’s law to find ΔH for C(s) + ½ O₂ → CO, given C + O₂ → CO₂ (−393.5 kJ) and CO + ½ O₂ → CO₂ (−283.0 kJ).

    Show full solution
    1. Keep the first reaction; reverse the second (+283.0 kJ).
    2. Add them; CO₂ cancels.

    Answer: −110.5 kJ.

  7. For a process with ΔH = −10.0 kJ and ΔS = −50.0 J/K, find the temperature below which it is spontaneous.

    Show full solution
    1. ΔG < 0 when ΔH − TΔS < 0. Both are negative, so it works at low T.
    2. T = ΔH/ΔS = −10.0 kJ ÷ −0.0500 kJ/K = 200 K.

    Answer: Spontaneous below 200 K.

Glossary

Absorbance
How much light a sample absorbs; A = −log₁₀T.
Avogadro’s number
6.02214076 × 10²³, the number of particles in one mole.
Calorimetry
Measuring heat flow from temperature changes.
Electron configuration
The arrangement of an atom’s electrons in orbitals.
Empirical formula
The simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound.
Endpoint
The point in a titration where the indicator changes color.
Enthalpy (H)
Heat content of a system at constant pressure.
Entropy (S)
A measure of how spread out energy and matter are.
Equivalence point
The point where exactly enough titrant has been added to react with the sample.
Gibbs free energy (G)
G = H − TS; its change predicts spontaneity.
Hydrogen bond
Strong attraction between H on N, O or F and a lone pair on another N, O or F.
Ideal gas
A model gas whose particles have no volume and no attractions.
Isotope
Atoms of one element with different neutron numbers.
Limiting reactant
The reactant that runs out first.
Molarity
Moles of solute per liter of solution.
Mole
The SI unit for amount of substance.
Orbital
A region where an electron is likely to be found.
Partial pressure
The pressure one gas in a mixture would exert alone.
Percent yield
Actual yield ÷ theoretical yield × 100%.
Photon
A particle of light with energy E = hν.
Quantum numbers
n, l, mₗ, mₛ: the four values that describe an electron.
Specific heat
Heat needed to raise 1 g of a substance by 1 °C.
Stoichiometry
The quantitative relationships between amounts in reactions.
Titration
Adding a solution of known concentration to find the concentration of another.
VSEPR
Valence-shell electron-pair repulsion: electron groups spread out to set molecular shape.
