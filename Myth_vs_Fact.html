Myth vs Fact

Common myths about Earth and space, checked against NASA, NOAA, ESA and EPA.

NASA
  1. Home
  2. Myth vs Fact

Why This Page Exists

Myths spread faster than the data behind them. Each one below is checked against a NASA, NOAA, ESA or EPA source, linked so you can read it yourself. Where the news is good, we say so. Where the problem isn't solved, we say that too.

Myth

The ozone hole can't be fixed.

Fact

It is healing. NOAA and NASA ranked the 2025 Antarctic ozone hole the fifth smallest since 1992, when the Montreal Protocol began to take effect, and the ozone layer remains on track to recover fully later this century.

It's slow: banned chemicals still leak from old products, so scientists expect the Antarctic hole to recover around the late 2060s.

Source: NOAA, 2025 ozone hole is 5th smallest since 1992

Myth

If an asteroid were coming, there would be nothing we could do.

Fact

Humanity has already moved one. NASA's DART spacecraft hit the asteroid moonlet Dimorphos in 2022 and shortened its orbit by 33 minutes and 15 seconds.

DART was a test on a small asteroid that posed no threat. Finding asteroids early gives time to act, which is why NASA is building NEO Surveyor.

Source: NASA, Asteroid's orbit, shape changed after DART impact

Myth

Asteroid Apophis will hit Earth in 2029.

Fact

It won't. Apophis will safely pass close to Earth on April 13, 2029, and NASA is confident there is no risk of it impacting our planet for at least 100 years.

It really will pass close, about 20,000 miles away, which is why NASA's OSIRIS-APEX is going to study it.

Source: NASA, Asteroid Apophis

Myth

Asteroid 2024 YR4 is going to hit us in 2032.

Fact

In early 2025 it briefly had a small chance of hitting Earth. More observations showed it poses no significant risk to Earth, and in 2026 NASA ruled out a Moon impact too.

Early impact odds often rise before they fall to zero. That is the tracking system working, not failing.

Source: NASA, observations eliminate chance of 2032 lunar impact

Myth

Bleached coral is dead coral.

Fact

NOAA says it plainly: when a coral bleaches, it is not dead. If the stress is not severe, corals have been known to recover.

If the heat stress lasts too long, bleached coral does die. That's why restoration groups replant reefs.

Source: NOAA, Is coral bleaching the same as coral death?

Myth

Solar storms hit without any warning.

Fact

NOAA's SOLAR-1 watches the Sun from about a million miles away and is a cornerstone of the U.S. space weather early warning system, delivering storm imagery to forecasters within 30 minutes of capture.

Forecasts aren't perfect, and warning time varies from storm to storm. ESA's Vigil, planned for 2031, aims to add up to four to five days' notice of some effects.

Source: NOAA, SOLAR-1 enters new era of space weather monitoring

Myth

Nobody is doing anything about space junk.

Fact

Agencies are acting. ESA guided its Aeolus satellite down in 2023 in the first assisted reentry of its kind, and ESA's Zero Debris approach aims to sharply limit new debris from its missions by 2030.

The problem is still growing: ESA's 2025 report says better rule-following is not yet enough to stop debris from increasing. Removal missions are the next step.

Source: ESA, Space Environment Report 2025

Myth

You can see the Great Wall of China from space with your own eyes.

Fact

NASA says the wall isn't visible from the Moon, and is difficult or impossible to see from Earth orbit without high-powered lenses.

Astronaut Leroy Chiao did photograph it from the International Space Station, but only with a high-powered lens.

Source: NASA, Great Wall

Myth

Wings lift because air over the top has to race to meet the air underneath at the back.

Fact

NASA calls this "equal transit time" idea the most popular incorrect theory of lift. Air over the top of a lifting wing really moves much faster than that idea predicts.

Part of it is right: faster air over the top does mean lower pressure there. NASA explains lift as the wing turning the air, with both the top and bottom surfaces helping.

Source: NASA Glenn Research Center, Bernoulli and Newton

Myth

Astronauts float because there's no gravity in space.

Fact

At the space station's height, Earth's gravity is still about 90 percent as strong as on the ground. Astronauts float because they and their spacecraft are falling around Earth together.

Scientists call this feeling of weightlessness microgravity.

Source: NASA, What Is Microgravity?

Myth

It's summer when Earth is closest to the Sun.

Fact

Earth's tilted axis causes the seasons. In the Northern Hemisphere, winter actually comes when Earth is closest to the Sun.

Earth's orbit is a bit lopsided, but the change in distance is small compared with how far away the Sun is. The two hemispheres have opposite seasons at the same time.

Source: NASA Space Place, What Causes the Seasons?

Myth

Shooting stars are stars falling from the sky.

Fact

NASA says "shooting stars" are meteors: bits of space rock that burn up as they enter Earth's atmosphere at high speed.

If a piece survives the trip and lands on the ground, it's called a meteorite.

Source: NASA, Meteors and Meteorites

Myth

The Great Pacific Garbage Patch is a giant floating island of trash.

Fact

NOAA says it isn't a solid island. Much of the debris is small pieces of floating plastic that are hard to see, spread out by wind and waves.

It's still a real problem. Ships can sail through and see very little, but sea animals can swallow those small pieces.

Source: NOAA Ocean Service, What is the Great Pacific Garbage Patch?

Myth

The ocean is blue because it reflects the sky.

Fact

NOAA says the ocean is blue because water absorbs the red part of sunlight, leaving the blue part for us to see.

Floating particles can make it look green or red, and below about 3,280 feet (1,000 m) no sunlight reaches at all.

Source: NOAA Ocean Service, Why is the ocean blue?

Myth

The far side of the Moon is always dark.

Fact

NASA says calling it the "dark side" is misleading. As the Moon orbits Earth, different parts are in sunlight or darkness at different times.

We really do always see the same side, because the Moon spins at the same rate it orbits Earth. People first saw the far side in 1959, from a Soviet spacecraft.

Source: NASA, Earth's Moon: Facts

Myth

Lightning never strikes the same place twice.

Fact

NOAA's National Weather Service says lightning often strikes the same place again and again, especially tall, pointy, isolated objects. The Empire State Building is hit about 23 times a year.

Safety tip from the same page: if you hear thunder, get inside a real building or a hard-topped car right away.

Source: NOAA National Weather Service, Lightning Myths and Facts

Myth

If it isn't raining, lightning can't hit you.

Fact

Lightning often strikes more than three miles (5 km) from the center of a storm, and "bolts from the blue" can strike 10 to 15 miles (16 to 24 km) away.

You aren't safe anywhere outdoors in a thunderstorm, so head inside as soon as you hear thunder.

Source: NOAA National Weather Service, Lightning Myths and Facts

Myth

If plastic has the recycling arrows and a number on it, it can always be recycled.

Fact

The U.S. EPA says a plastic resin code on a product does not guarantee it can be recycled where you live.

The number tells you what kind of plastic it is. Check what your local recycling service accepts, because the rules change from place to place.

Source: U.S. EPA, Frequent Questions on Recycling

Myth

Almost all the oxygen we breathe comes from trees.

Fact

NOAA says about half of Earth's oxygen comes from the ocean, mostly made by tiny drifting plankton.

Ocean life also uses up about as much oxygen as the ocean makes, so healthy forests and a healthy ocean both matter.

Source: NOAA Ocean Service, How much oxygen comes from the ocean?

Myth

You can only see the northern lights near the North Pole.

Fact

NOAA says auroras form in two ovals, around the north and south magnetic poles. During big geomagnetic storms they can be seen over most of the United States.

The best places are still far north or far south, between about 60 and 75 degrees latitude.

Source: NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center, Aurora

Myth

Scientists have already explored the whole ocean.

Fact

NOAA says explorers have seen less than 0.001% of the deep-ocean floor, and as of April 2026 only 28.7% of the seafloor had been mapped in high detail.

More than 90% of the ocean is deep ocean, so most of it is still waiting to be explored.

Source: NOAA Ocean Exploration, How much of the ocean has been explored?
