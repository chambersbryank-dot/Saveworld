About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a one-semester sophomore engineering statics course. It is modeled on the topics and depth of MIT 2.001 (the statics half of Mechanics and Materials I, on MIT OpenCourseWare), Purdue ME 270 and Georgia Tech COE 2001, and on the free open textbook Engineering Statics: Open and Interactive and OpenStax University Physics Volume 1 (Chapter 12), which we link throughout. None of those authors or schools endorses this page.

What we add: every unit ties to real clean-energy, infrastructure or space work with a cited public source (wind turbines, floating offshore wind, bridges, Hoover Dam, the space station, landslides, building materials), every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab has a free simulation or a cheap home version.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Clean energy and resilient infrastructure stand on statics. A wind turbine tower must resist the overturning moment of the wind on its rotor, a floating offshore platform is held in place by mooring lines in balance, a dam turns water pressure into forces its concrete can carry, and every bridge is checked by summing forces and moments.

Statics is also how we build lighter and waste less: a well-designed truss or frame uses less steel, concrete and wood for the same job, and structures that last longer keep materials out of landfills. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Physics I: forces, Newton’s laws, torque and free-body diagrams.
  • Calculus I and II: derivatives and integrals (for distributed loads, centroids and moment diagrams).
  • Vectors: components, dot and cross products (reviewed in Unit 1).
  • Calculus III and Linear Algebra help with 3D problems but are not required.

How to study

  • Draw a free-body diagram first, every time. Most statics mistakes are a missing or extra force on the diagram.
  • Pick axes and a moment point that remove as many unknowns as possible.
  • Solve with symbols first, then put in numbers in SI units, and check units and signs at the end.
  • Count unknowns and independent equations before solving, so you know whether the problem can be solved by statics alone.
  • Check the answer with an equation you did not use (for example, a second moment point).
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer, and retake the quizzes a few days later.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer or tablet for the free PhET simulations (they run in a browser).
  • A digital luggage scale or a kitchen scale, string, a few known masses (water bottles or bags of rice with the mass on the label work).
  • A 30 cm ruler, a pencil, coins or washers, and a smartphone protractor or inclinometer app.
  • Craft sticks or stiff card, a glue gun or white glue, and pins.
  • A sheet of corrugated cardboard, scissors, a pushpin and a thread with a small weight (a plumb line).
  • A hardcover book, a small box, and a smooth mug or round post for the rope-friction test.

Unit 1 · Weeks 1–2

Forces and Vectors in 2D and 3D

What statics is, units, forces as vectors, components, resultants, unit vectors and position vectors in 3D, and the dot product for angles and projections.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Resolve a force into components in 2D and 3D.
  • Add forces to find a resultant and its direction.
  • Write a force along a line from two points.
  • Use the dot product to find angles and projections.

Key concepts

  • Statics studies bodies whose acceleration is zero, so the forces on them balance: ΣF = 0 and ΣM = 0.
  • A force has magnitude, direction and a point (or line) of action. In SI it is measured in newtons; 1 kg weighs 9.81 N.
  • In 2D, a force F at angle θ from the x axis has components F cos θ and F sin θ. Resultants add component by component.
  • In 3D, write F = F û, where û is a unit vector. Along a line from A to B, û = (r_B − r_A)/|r_B − r_A|.
  • The dot product A·B = |A||B| cos θ gives the angle between two vectors and the component of a force along any line.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 A 500 N force acts at 30° above the +x axis. Find its components.

Show the steps
  1. F_x = 500 cos 30° = 433 N.
  2. F_y = 500 sin 30° = 250 N.

Answer: F = (433, 250) N.

Example 1.2 Add F₁ = 300 N along +x and F₂ = 400 N at 120° from +x.

Show the steps
  1. F₂ = (400 cos 120°, 400 sin 120°) = (−200, 346.4) N.
  2. R = (100, 346.4) N, so |R| = √(100² + 346.4²).
  3. θ = tan⁻¹(346.4/100).

Answer: 360.6 N at 73.9° from +x.

Example 1.3 A cable pulls with 1.2 kN from A(0, 0, 0) toward B(2, 3, 6) m. Write the force as a vector.

Show the steps
  1. r_AB = (2, 3, 6) m, length √(4 + 9 + 36) = 7 m.
  2. û = (2/7, 3/7, 6/7).
  3. F = 1200 û.

Answer: F = (343, 514, 1029) N.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Find the angle between A = (3, 4, 0) and B = (4, 0, 3).

    Show answer

    A·B = 12, |A| = |B| = 5, cos θ = 0.48, θ = 61.3°.

  2. Find the component of F = (100, 200, −50) N along û = (0.6, 0.8, 0).

    Show answer

    F·û = 60 + 160 = 220 N.

  3. Find the direction angles of F = (200, −300, 600) N.

    Show answer

    |F| = 700 N; α = cos⁻¹(2/7) = 73.4°, β = cos⁻¹(−3/7) = 115.4°, γ = cos⁻¹(6/7) = 31.0°.

  4. Sample values: a 50 kN force on a structure acts 20° above horizontal. Find its horizontal and vertical parts.

    Show answer

    F_x = 50 cos 20° = 47.0 kN; F_y = 50 sin 20° = 17.1 kN.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Forces add like numbers: 300 N + 400 N = 700 N.

    Actually: They add as vectors; 300 N and 400 N at right angles give 500 N.

  • Myth: A unit vector has units of newtons.

    Actually: A unit vector is dimensionless with length 1; the magnitude carries the units.

  • Myth: Mass and weight are the same thing.

    Actually: Mass is in kg; weight is a force, mg, in newtons.

Flashcards

  • Statics

    Bodies at rest or constant velocity: ΣF = 0, ΣM = 0.

  • Components

    F_x = F cos θ, F_y = F sin θ.

  • Unit vector

    û = r/|r|, length 1.

  • Force along a line

    F = F (r_B − r_A)/|r_B − r_A|.

  • Dot product

    A·B = |A||B| cos θ.

  • Weight

    W = mg, 9.81 N per kg.

Show answer

How this helps save the planet

A wind turbine blade is a lesson in force vectors. The U.S. Department of Energy explains that wind flowing across a blade creates both lift and drag, and that the lift is stronger than the drag, which makes the rotor spin. Engineers resolve those forces into components along and across the rotor to size the blades, the hub and the tower.

Unit 2 · Week 3

Equilibrium of a Particle

Free-body diagrams, equilibrium of concurrent forces in 2D and 3D, cables, pulleys and springs.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Draw a complete free-body diagram of a particle.
  • Solve 2D equilibrium for two unknowns.
  • Solve 3D equilibrium with cable forces written as vectors.
  • Model springs with F = kδ.

Key concepts

  • A particle is a body small enough that all forces act at one point, so only ΣF = 0 matters (two equations in 2D, three in 3D).
  • A free-body diagram (FBD) isolates the body and shows every external force on it, with known directions and labeled unknowns.
  • A cable or rope can only pull, along its own line. Over an ideal pulley the tension is the same on both sides.
  • A linear spring pulls or pushes with F = kδ, where δ is the change from its unstretched length.
  • If you get a negative tension, the assumed direction was wrong; a cable cannot push, so the setup must change.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 A 100 kg mass hangs from two cables, each 30° above horizontal. Find the tension.

Show the steps
  1. W = 100 × 9.81 = 981 N.
  2. Vertical: 2T sin 30° = 981.

Answer: T = 981 N in each cable.

Example 2.2 A 50 kg mass hangs from cable B at 30° and cable C at 45° above horizontal, on opposite sides. Find both tensions.

Show the steps
  1. W = 490.5 N.
  2. Horizontal: T_B cos 30° = T_C cos 45°.
  3. Vertical: T_B sin 30° + T_C sin 45° = 490.5, so T_B (0.5 + 0.866) = 490.5.

Answer: T_B = 359.1 N, T_C = 439.8 N.

Example 2.3 A spring with k = 2000 N/m holds a 20 kg mass at rest. How far does it stretch?

Show the steps
  1. kδ = mg = 196.2 N.
  2. δ = 196.2/2000.

Answer: 0.0981 m (98.1 mm).

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. A 10 kg block rests on a smooth 25° incline, held by a cable parallel to the slope. Find the tension and normal force.

    Show answer

    T = mg sin 25° = 41.5 N; N = mg cos 25° = 88.9 N.

  2. A 600 N load hangs from three cables to points (2, 0, 4), (−1, 1.732, 4) and (−1, −1.732, 4) m. Find each tension.

    Show answer

    Each cable is √20 = 4.47 m long with vertical share 4/4.47 = 0.894; by symmetry 3T(0.894) = 600, so T = 223.6 N.

  3. Two slings, each 60° above horizontal, lift a 2,000 kg load. Find the sling tension.

    Show answer

    T = 19,620/(2 sin 60°) = 11,327 N ≈ 11.3 kN.

  4. Why do sling tensions grow as the sling angle gets flatter?

    Show answer

    T = W/(2 sin θ); as θ → 0, sin θ → 0 and T grows without limit.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: The tension in a cable equals the weight it holds.

    Actually: Only when the cable is vertical; at an angle it must be larger.

  • Myth: Forces from the body on its supports go on its FBD.

    Actually: Only forces acting on the body go on its FBD.

  • Myth: A heavier object on a pulley rope makes tension different on each side.

    Actually: An ideal, frictionless pulley keeps the tension the same on both sides.

Flashcards

  • Particle

    All forces meet at one point; only ΣF = 0.

  • Free-body diagram

    The isolated body with every external force on it.

  • Cable

    Pulls along its own line only.

  • Ideal pulley

    Same tension on both sides.

  • Spring

    F = kδ.

  • Negative answer

    The assumed direction was wrong.

Self-check quiz

1. In 3D, a particle in equilibrium gives how many independent equations?

Show answer

3. ΣF_x = ΣF_y = ΣF_z = 0.

ΣF_x = ΣF_y = ΣF_z = 0.

2. A cable can…

Show answer

pull. Cables act only in tension.

Cables act only in tension.

3. Two cables hold a weight at 30° each. Flattening them to 15° makes the tension…

Show answer

larger. T = W/(2 sin θ).

T = W/(2 sin θ).

4. The first step in any statics problem is…

Show answer

draw a free-body diagram. The FBD shows what to sum.

The FBD shows what to sum.

How this helps save the planet

The U.S. Department of Energy notes that much offshore wind energy potential exists over waters too deep for fixed-bottom foundations secured to the sea floor, so turbines there need floating platforms. A floating platform stays in place because its mooring lines, buoyancy and weight balance, which is particle and rigid-body equilibrium on a giant scale.

Unit 3 · Weeks 4–5

Moments, Couples and Equivalent Systems

Moment of a force in 2D and 3D, the cross product, Varignon’s theorem, moment about an axis, couples, equivalent force systems, and resultants of distributed loads.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Find the moment of a force about a point in 2D (scalar) and 3D (cross product).
  • Find the moment of a force about an axis.
  • Work with couples and replace a force system by an equivalent force and couple.
  • Replace a distributed load by its resultant.

Key concepts

  • The moment of a force about a point O is M_O = r × F, where r runs from O to any point on the force’s line. In 2D its size is F times the perpendicular distance d.
  • Varignon’s theorem: the moment of a force equals the sum of the moments of its components, so M_z = xF_y − yF_x.
  • The moment about an axis is the component of M_O along that axis: M_a = û_a·(r × F).
  • A couple is two equal, opposite, parallel forces. Its moment Fd is the same about every point, so it is a free vector.
  • Any force system can be replaced by one force and one couple at any point. A distributed load w(x) is equivalent to a force equal to its area, acting at its centroid.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 A 200 N pull acts at the end of a 0.40 m wrench, at 60° to the handle. Find the moment about the bolt.

Show the steps
  1. Perpendicular part of the force: 200 sin 60°.
  2. M = 0.40 × 200 sin 60°.

Answer: 69.3 N·m.

Example 3.2 Find M_O for r = (2, 1, 0) m and F = (0, 0, 500) N.

Show the steps
  1. r × F = (1 × 500 − 0, 0 − 2 × 500, 0).
  2. |M| = √(500² + 1000²).

Answer: M_O = (500, −1000, 0) N·m, size 1118 N·m.

Example 3.3 A beam carries a uniform 4 kN/m over 5 m and, elsewhere, a triangular load rising from 0 to 6 kN/m over 3 m. Find each resultant.

Show the steps
  1. Uniform: area 4 × 5 at the middle.
  2. Triangle: area ½ × 6 × 3 at two-thirds of the way from the zero end.

Answer: 20 kN at 2.5 m; 9 kN at 2 m from the zero end.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Two 150 N forces form a couple with arms 0.25 m apart. Find the couple moment.

    Show answer

    M = Fd = 37.5 N·m, the same about any point.

  2. F = (300, 400) N acts at (2, 1.5) m. Find M about the origin.

    Show answer

    M_z = xF_y − yF_x = 800 − 450 = 350 N·m, counterclockwise.

  3. Sample values: wind pushes a turbine rotor with 400 kN at a hub 100 m above the ground. Find the overturning moment at the base.

    Show answer

    M = 400 kN × 100 m = 40 MN·m (sample values).

  4. Replace 2 kN at x = 3 m and 4 kN at x = 6 m (both downward) by one force.

    Show answer

    F_R = 6 kN at x̄ = (2 × 3 + 4 × 6)/6 = 5 m.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Moment = force × distance to the point of application.

    Actually: Use the perpendicular distance to the line of action.

  • Myth: A couple tends to move a body sideways.

    Actually: A couple has zero net force; it only rotates.

  • Myth: r × F = F × r.

    Actually: The cross product changes sign when the order flips.

Flashcards

  • Moment

    M_O = r × F; size Fd.

  • Varignon

    Moment of a force = sum of the moments of its components.

  • Moment about an axis

    M_a = û·(r × F).

  • Couple

    Equal, opposite, parallel forces; moment Fd everywhere.

  • Equivalent system

    One force plus one couple at any point.

  • Distributed load

    Resultant = area, acting at the centroid.

Show answer

The perpendicular distance is unchanged.

2. The moment of a couple about different points is…

Show answer

the same. A couple is a free vector.

A couple is a free vector.

3. A uniform load w over length L has a resultant of…

Show answer

Show answer

How this helps save the planet

Tall towers mean big moments. The U.S. Department of Energy says offshore wind turbines tend to be massive and taller than the Statue of Liberty, and that larger turbines are more cost-effective. The wind’s push on a rotor high above the base makes a large overturning moment, and the tower, foundation or floating platform must supply an equal and opposite moment. A moment calculation like the sample one in the practice problems is where every tower design starts.

Unit 4 · Week 6

Rigid-Body Equilibrium in 2D

Supports and their reactions, free-body diagrams of rigid bodies, the three planar equilibrium equations, two- and three-force members, and statical determinacy.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Replace each support (pin, roller, fixed) by the right reactions.
  • Solve for support reactions with ΣF_x = ΣF_y = ΣM = 0.
  • Recognize two-force members.
  • Decide whether a body is statically determinate, indeterminate or improperly supported.

Key concepts

  • A rigid body needs both ΣF = 0 and ΣM = 0. In 2D that is three equations: ΣF_x = 0, ΣF_y = 0 and ΣM_A = 0 about any point A.
  • Supports: a roller gives one force normal to its surface, a pin gives two force components, and a fixed support gives two forces and a couple.
  • Take moments about the point where the most unknowns meet; that often gives one unknown per equation.
  • A two-force member (forces at only two points, no moment) carries equal and opposite forces along the line joining those points.
  • With more unknown reactions than equations a body is statically indeterminate; with reactions all parallel or all through one point it is improperly constrained.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 A 6 m simply supported beam (pin at A, roller at B) carries 12 kN at 2 m from A. Find the reactions.

Show the steps
  1. ΣM_A = 0: 6R_B = 12 × 2.
  2. ΣF_y = 0: R_A = 12 − R_B.

Answer: R_B = 4 kN, R_A = 8 kN.

Example 4.2 A 3 m cantilever carries 2 kN/m along its length plus 5 kN at the tip. Find the wall reactions.

Show the steps
  1. Distributed resultant 6 kN at 1.5 m.
  2. V = 6 + 5; M = 6 × 1.5 + 5 × 3.

Answer: V = 11 kN up, M = 24 kN·m at the wall.

Example 4.3 An 8 m beam has a pin at x = 0, a roller at x = 6 m, and a uniform 3 kN/m over the whole length. Find the reactions.

Show the steps
  1. Resultant 24 kN at x = 4 m.
  2. ΣM_A: 6R_B = 24 × 4.
  3. R_A = 24 − R_B.

Answer: R_B = 16 kN, R_A = 8 kN.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. A 4 m horizontal boom is pinned at a wall; a cable from its tip to the wall makes 30° with the boom. A 500 kg load hangs at the tip. Find the cable tension and pin reactions (ignore the boom’s weight).

    Show answer

    ΣM_pin: T sin 30° × 4 = 4905 × 4, T = 9810 N; A_x = T cos 30° = 8496 N; A_y = 4905 − 4905 = 0.

  2. A beam has a pin and two rollers. Is it determinate?

    Show answer

    Four unknown reactions, three equations: indeterminate to the first degree.

  3. Sample values: a 10 m bridge span carries an 80 kN truck axle group 4 m from support A. Find the reactions.

    Show answer

    R_B = 80 × 4/10 = 32 kN; R_A = 48 kN (sample values).

  4. Why is a beam on three rollers (all vertical) not safe?

    Show answer

    Nothing resists horizontal force: it is improperly constrained even though it has three reactions.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: A pin support gives a moment.

    Actually: A pin lets the body rotate; only a fixed support gives a couple.

  • Myth: Always take moments about the center.

    Actually: Pick the point that removes the most unknowns.

  • Myth: Three reactions always mean determinate.

    Actually: Not if they are all parallel or all meet at one point.

Flashcards

  • Roller

    One reaction, normal to the surface.

  • Pin

    Two force reactions.

  • Fixed support

    Two forces and a couple (2D).

  • Two-force member

    Equal, opposite forces along the line between its ends.

  • Statically indeterminate

    More unknowns than equilibrium equations.

  • Improper constraint

    Reactions all parallel or concurrent.

Unit 5 · Week 7

Rigid-Body Equilibrium in 3D

Supports in 3D (ball and socket, hinge, bearing, fixed), six equilibrium equations, moments about axes to isolate unknowns, and determinacy in 3D.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Model 3D supports with the right reactions.
  • Write and solve the six equations of 3D equilibrium.
  • Use moments about well-chosen axes to find one unknown at a time.
  • Count unknowns to judge 3D determinacy.

Key concepts

  • In 3D a rigid body gives six equations: ΣF_x = ΣF_y = ΣF_z = 0 and ΣM_x = ΣM_y = ΣM_z = 0.
  • A ball-and-socket joint gives three forces; a smooth surface or a cable gives one; a hinge or bearing gives up to five reactions; a fixed support gives three forces and three couples.
  • Taking moments about an axis that passes through several unknown forces removes them all from that equation.
  • A flat plate on three vertical supports with vertical loads uses three equations (ΣF_z, ΣM_x, ΣM_y); a fourth support makes it indeterminate.
  • In 3D, the cross product r × F is the reliable way to get moments; use a determinant and keep the order r then F.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 A 2 m × 1 m plate rests on supports at (0, 0), (2, 0) and (0, 1) m. An 800 N load acts at (0.8, 0.4) m. Find the three reactions.

Show the steps
  1. Moments about the x axis (y = 0 line): R₃ × 1 = 800 × 0.4.
  2. Moments about the y axis (x = 0 line): R₂ × 2 = 800 × 0.8.
  3. ΣF_z: R₁ = 800 − R₂ − R₃.

Answer: R₁ = 160 N, R₂ = 320 N, R₃ = 320 N.

Example 5.2 F = (0, 0, −200) N acts at (0.5, 0.3, 0) m. Find its moments about the x and y axes through the origin.

Show the steps
  1. M_x = yF_z − zF_y = 0.3 × (−200).
  2. M_y = zF_x − xF_z = −0.5 × (−200).

Answer: M_x = −60 N·m, M_y = +100 N·m.

Example 5.3 A symmetric three-legged stand carries 1.5 kN at its center. Find the force in each leg.

Show the steps
  1. By symmetry the three vertical reactions are equal.
  2. 3R = 1.5 kN.

Answer: 500 N per leg.

Unit 6 · Week 8

Trusses: Method of Joints and Method of Sections

Ideal trusses, tension and compression members, the method of joints, zero-force members, the method of sections, and truss determinacy.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • State the assumptions of an ideal truss.
  • Find member forces by the method of joints.
  • Spot zero-force members by inspection.
  • Find a few member forces quickly by the method of sections.

Key concepts

  • An ideal truss has straight members joined by frictionless pins and loaded only at the joints, so every member is a two-force member in tension (T) or compression (C).
  • Method of joints: each joint is a particle with two equations; start at a joint with no more than two unknowns.
  • Zero-force members: at an unloaded joint with two non-collinear members, both are zero; at an unloaded joint with two collinear members and a third, the third is zero.
  • Method of sections: cut through no more than three members with unknown forces and use the three equations of a rigid body on one side; take moments where two cut members meet.
  • A plane truss with m members, r reactions and j joints is determinate when m + r = 2j (and it is stable).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 6.1 A triangle truss spans 4 m with its apex 2 m high at mid-span; 10 kN hangs at the apex. Find the member forces.

Show the steps
  1. Reactions: 5 kN at each support.
  2. At A: F_AC sin 45° = 5, so F_AC = 7.07 kN.
  3. Horizontal at A: F_AB = F_AC cos 45°.

Answer: AC and BC: 7.07 kN (C); AB: 5 kN (T).

Example 6.2 A truss is 3 m deep. At a section, the moment of the loads and reactions on the left side about the bottom joint is 60 kN·m. Find the top chord force.

Show the steps
  1. Moments about the bottom joint where the other two cut members meet.
  2. F × 3 = 60.

Answer: 20 kN (C) in the top chord.

Example 6.3 In a bridge truss, an unloaded joint joins two collinear bottom-chord members and one vertical. Find the vertical’s force.

Show the steps
  1. Sum forces perpendicular to the chord at that joint: only the vertical has a component.

Answer: Zero: it is a zero-force member under this loading.

  3. Why keep zero-force members if they carry nothing?

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Members in a truss bend.

    Actually: In an ideal truss, members only stretch or shorten (pure tension or compression).

  • Myth: Zero-force members can be removed.

    Actually: They stabilize the truss and carry load under other cases.

  • Myth: A negative answer means a mistake.

    Actually: If you assumed tension, a negative answer means compression.

Unit 7 · Week 9

Frames and Machines

Structures with multiforce members: taking frames apart, pin forces on each member, mechanical advantage of machines, and pulley systems.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Tell a frame or machine from a truss.
  • Take a frame apart and apply Newton’s third law at shared pins.
  • Find the output force and mechanical advantage of simple machines.
  • Analyze ideal pulley systems.

Key concepts

  • Frames and machines contain at least one multiforce member: a member with forces at three or more points, or with forces and moments, so it can bend.
  • Take the structure apart. At each shared pin, the forces on the two members are equal and opposite.
  • Find two-force members first; their force directions are known, which cuts the unknowns.
  • A machine changes an input force into an output force; mechanical advantage MA = F_out/F_in (ideal, without friction).
  • In an ideal pulley system the load is shared by the rope segments that support it, so effort = load/n.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 7.1 A hand squeezes pliers with 100 N at 120 mm from the pin; the jaws grip 30 mm from the pin. Find the grip force.

Show the steps
  1. Moments about the pin on one handle: F_jaw × 30 = 100 × 120.

Answer: 400 N (MA = 4).

Example 7.2 A wheelbarrow load of 600 N sits 0.4 m from the wheel axle; the handles are 1.4 m from the axle. Find the lifting force.

Show the steps
  1. Moments about the axle: F × 1.4 = 600 × 0.4.

Answer: 171.4 N.

Example 7.3 An ideal pulley system has 4 rope segments supporting an 800 N load. Find the effort.

Show the steps
  1. Each segment carries the same tension T; 4T = 800.

Answer: 200 N.

Unit 8 · Weeks 10–11

Centroids, Distributed Loads, Fluid Pressure and Area Moments of Inertia

Center of gravity and centroids by integration and composite parts, distributed loads, hydrostatic pressure on submerged surfaces, and area moments of inertia with the parallel-axis theorem.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Find centroids of composite areas.
  • Find the resultant and line of action of hydrostatic pressure on a vertical wall or gate.
  • Compute area moments of inertia of rectangles and composite shapes.
  • Apply the parallel-axis theorem.

Key concepts

  • The center of gravity is where the total weight acts. For a uniform plate it equals the centroid of the area: x̄ = ΣxᵢAᵢ/ΣAᵢ.
  • Composite parts: split a shape into rectangles, triangles and circles; holes count as negative areas.
  • Water pressure grows linearly with depth, p = ρgh. On a vertical wall of width b and depth h, the resultant is ½ρgh²b, acting h/3 above the bottom.
  • The area moment of inertia I = ∫y² dA measures how far area sits from an axis; it controls how stiff a beam is in bending (next course).
  • Parallel-axis theorem: I = I_c + Ad², where I_c is about the centroidal axis and d the distance between axes.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 8.1 An L-shape is a 100 × 20 mm bar along the x axis plus a 20 × 80 mm bar standing on its left end (x from 0 to 20 mm, y from 20 to 100 mm). Find the centroid.

Show the steps
  1. A₁ = 2000 mm² at (50, 10); A₂ = 1600 mm² at (10, 60).
  2. x̄ = (2000 × 50 + 1600 × 10)/3600.
  3. ȳ = (2000 × 10 + 1600 × 60)/3600.

Answer: (32.2, 32.2) mm.

Example 8.2 A vertical gate 2 m wide holds water 3 m deep. Find the resultant force and where it acts.

Show the steps
  1. F = ½ρgh²b = ½ × 1000 × 9.81 × 3² × 2.
  2. Line of action: h/3 above the bottom.

Answer: 88.3 kN, 1 m above the bottom (2 m below the surface).

Example 8.3 Find I for a 100 mm wide, 200 mm deep rectangle about its centroidal axis and about its base.

Show the steps
  1. I_c = bh³/12 = 100 × 200³/12.
  2. I_base = I_c + A(100)² with A = 20,000 mm².

Answer: I_c = 66.7 × 10⁶ mm⁴; I_base = 266.7 × 10⁶ mm⁴ (= bh³/3).

Unit 9 · Weeks 12–13

Internal Forces: Shear and Bending-Moment Diagrams, and Cables

Internal normal force, shear and bending moment, sign conventions, shear and moment diagrams from equilibrium and from the load–shear–moment relations, and cables under distributed loads.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Find the internal forces at any section of a beam.
  • Draw shear and bending-moment diagrams for point and distributed loads.
  • Use dV/dx = −w and dM/dx = V to check diagrams.
  • Find the tension in a parabolic cable.

Key concepts

  • Cut a beam at x and the exposed face carries a normal force N, a shear force V and a bending moment M, found from equilibrium of either piece.
  • Sign convention used here: positive shear turns a segment clockwise; positive moment bends the beam into a smile (sagging).
  • Relations: dV/dx = −w (load per length, downward positive) and dM/dx = V. A point load makes a jump in V; a couple makes a jump in M.
  • The maximum moment occurs where V = 0 (or at a jump in V); it is the value that sizes the beam.
  • A cable under a uniform horizontal load hangs in a parabola; the horizontal tension is H = wL²/(8s) for span L and sag s, and the tension is largest at the supports.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 9.1 A 6 m simply supported beam carries 4 kN/m. Find V(x), M(x) and the maximum moment.

Show the steps
  1. Reactions: 12 kN each.
  2. V = 12 − 4x; M = 12x − 2x².
  3. V = 0 at x = 3 m.

Answer: M_max = wL²/8 = 18 kN·m at mid-span.

Example 9.2 The Unit 4 beam (6 m, 12 kN at 2 m from A). Draw V and M.

Show the steps
  1. R_A = 8 kN, R_B = 4 kN.
  2. V = +8 kN for 0 < x < 2, then −4 kN.
  3. M rises linearly to 8 × 2 at the load, then falls to 0 at B.

Answer: M_max = 16 kN·m under the load.

Example 9.3 A cable spans 100 m with a 10 m sag and carries 2 kN/m along the span. Find the horizontal and maximum tensions.

Show the steps
  1. H = wL²/(8s) = 2 × 100²/80.
  2. Vertical at each support = wL/2 = 100 kN.
  3. T_max = √(250² + 100²).

Answer: H = 250 kN; T_max = 269.3 kN.

Unit 10 · Week 14

Friction

Dry (Coulomb) friction, impending motion, angle of repose, sliding versus tipping, wedges, ladders, and belt or rope friction.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Decide whether a body slides, stays put or tips.
  • Solve impending-motion problems with F = μ_sN.
  • Use the angle of repose and the ladder problem.
  • Use the belt-friction equation T₂/T₁ = e^{μβ}.

Key concepts

  • Static friction adjusts to whatever is needed, up to a maximum F_max = μ_sN. Once sliding starts, kinetic friction is about μ_kN, usually a bit less.
  • A block on a slope stays put if tan θ ≤ μ_s; the steepest angle, tan⁻¹ μ_s, is the angle of repose.
  • Check both sliding and tipping: a tall box pushed high may tip before it slides.
  • Wedges and screws use friction to hold loads; solve each piece as a rigid body with friction opposing the impending motion.
  • A rope wrapped around a post through angle β (in radians) holds T₂ = T₁ e^{μβ}, so a few turns hold a large load with a small pull.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 10.1 A 50 kg crate sits on a 20° slope with μ_s = 0.4. Does it slip?

Show the steps
  1. tan 20° = 0.364 < 0.4, so it holds.
  2. Friction needed = mg sin 20° = 167.8 N; available = 0.4 × mg cos 20° = 184.4 N.

Answer: It stays put; friction needed 167.8 N of 184.4 N available.

Example 10.2 What force parallel to the slope starts the same crate moving up?

Show the steps
  1. Friction now acts down the slope at its maximum.
  2. P = mg sin 20° + 0.4 mg cos 20°.

Answer: 352.2 N.

Example 10.3 A rope makes 1.5 turns around a post (β = 3π) with μ = 0.3. What load can a 100 N pull hold?

Show the steps
  1. T₂/T₁ = e^{0.3 × 3π} = e^{2.83}.

Answer: Ratio 16.9, so about 1,690 N.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Find the angle of repose for μ_s = 0.6.

    Show answer

    tan⁻¹ 0.6 = 31.0°.

  2. A 5 m ladder weighing 200 N leans at 60° to the floor against a smooth wall. Find the smallest floor μ_s that holds it.

    Show answer

    Wall force N_w: N_w × 5 sin 60° = 200 × 2.5 cos 60°, N_w = 57.7 N; floor N = 200 N; μ_min = 57.7/200 = 0.289.

  3. A box 0.5 m wide and 1.5 m tall (μ_s = 0.4) is pushed at its top edge. Does it slide or tip first?

    Show answer

    Slides when P > 0.4W; tips when P × 1.5 > W × 0.25, P > 0.167W. It tips first.

  4. A rope makes 2 turns (β = 4π) with μ = 0.2. Find T₂/T₁.

    Show answer

    e^{0.8π} = 12.3.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Friction always equals μN.

    Actually: Only at impending or actual sliding; before that it is whatever balance needs.

  • Myth: Friction depends on contact area.

    Actually: In the simple Coulomb model it depends only on μ and N.

  • Myth: Friction always opposes motion, so it points down a slope.

    Actually: It opposes impending relative motion; on a block about to slide down, it points up the slope.

Flashcards

  • Static friction

    F ≤ μ_sN.

  • Kinetic friction

    F ≈ μ_kN, usually a bit smaller.

  • Angle of repose

    tan θ = μ_s.

  • Tipping check

    Compare moments about the tipping edge.

  • Capstan equation

    T₂/T₁ = e^{μβ}.

  • Impending motion

    Friction at its maximum, opposing the motion about to happen.

How this helps save the planet

A slope stays put for the same reason a block on a ramp does: friction and strength in the soil resist the pull of gravity down the slope. The USGS Landslide Hazards Program works to reduce long-term losses from landslides by understanding the causes of ground failure, and notes that wildfire can increase landslide susceptibility in mountainous terrain. Keeping slopes planted and stable protects both people and watersheds.

Lab F: Measuring friction on a slope and around a post

Glossary

Angle of repose
Steepest slope angle at which a body stays put, tan⁻¹ μ_s.
Bending moment
Internal moment at a beam section.
Centroid
Geometric center of an area or volume.
Couple
Two equal, opposite, parallel forces; a pure moment.
Equilibrant
The force that balances a set of forces.
Fixed support
Support that prevents translation and rotation.
Frame
Structure with at least one multiforce member.
Free-body diagram
Sketch of a body isolated with every external force on it.
Mechanical advantage
Output force divided by input force.
Method of joints
Truss analysis with ΣF = 0 at each joint.
Method of sections
Truss analysis with rigid-body equilibrium of a cut piece.
Moment
Turning effect of a force, r × F.
Parallel-axis theorem
I = I_c + Ad².
Pin support
Support that prevents translation but allows rotation.
Resultant
Single force equivalent to a set of forces.
Roller support
Support that gives one force normal to its surface.
Shear force
Internal force across a beam section.
Statically indeterminate
More unknowns than equilibrium equations.
Truss
Structure of two-force members pinned at joints.
Two-force member
Member loaded at two points only; forces lie along the line between them.
Zero-force member
Truss member carrying no force under a given loading.
