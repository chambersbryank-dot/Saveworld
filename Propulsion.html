About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a one-semester senior propulsion course for aerospace engineers. It is modeled on the topics and depth of MIT 16.50 Introduction to Propulsion Systems and MIT 16.512 Rocket Propulsion (free on MIT OpenCourseWare), using NASA Glenn’s free Beginner’s Guide to Propulsion, which we link where it helps. None of those authors, schools or agencies endorses this page.

What we add: every unit ties to real work with a cited public source (NASA’s RS-25 engines, NASA engine-core research and the U.S. Department of Energy’s sustainable aviation fuel work), every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab has a free simulation or a spreadsheet.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Propulsion is where aviation’s fuel burn and emissions are decided: every point of engine efficiency is fuel not burned, and new fuels can cut the carbon in what is burned.

Rockets carry Earth-observing satellites to orbit, and cleaner, more efficient engines make that access more sustainable. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample or textbook values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Thermodynamics: the first and second laws, ideal gases and cycles.
  • Fluid Mechanics: control volumes, compressible flow basics.
  • Aerodynamics I and Aircraft Performance: drag, thrust required and range.
  • Orbital Mechanics: the rocket equation and Δv.

How to study

  • Draw a control volume around the engine and mark inflow and outflow before writing any equation.
  • Keep temperatures in kelvin and use stagnation (total) values where the flow is fast.
  • Separate thermal efficiency (heat to kinetic energy) from propulsive efficiency (kinetic energy to useful thrust power).
  • Check every answer against a real engine’s order of magnitude.
  • Track units: thrust in newtons, mass flow in kg/s, specific impulse in seconds.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer, and retake the quizzes a few days later.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer or tablet for the free PhET Gas Properties and Energy Skate Park simulations.
  • Spreadsheet software (any free one works), or Python.
  • NASA Glenn’s Beginner’s Guide to Propulsion pages in a web browser.
  • A calculator.

Unit 1 · Week 1

Thrust, Momentum and Propulsive Efficiency

The control-volume thrust equation, pressure thrust, specific thrust, thrust power, jet power, propulsive efficiency, and why big slow jets are efficient.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Derive thrust from momentum flux.
  • Compute specific thrust and thrust power.
  • Compute propulsive efficiency.
  • Explain why high bypass saves fuel.

Key concepts

  • Thrust is the net momentum flux out of the engine plus a pressure term: F = ṁ_eV_e − ṁ_0V_0 + (p_e − p_0)A_e.
  • For an air-breathing engine with small fuel flow and a matched nozzle, F ≈ ṁ(V_e − V_0).
  • Specific thrust F/ṁ (N·s/kg) tells you how much thrust each kilogram per second of air gives.
  • Propulsive efficiency η_p = 2/(1 + V_e/V_0) is the share of jet kinetic power that becomes useful thrust power F·V_0.
  • Accelerating a lot of air a little (V_e close to V_0) is efficient but needs a big engine; that is the turbofan’s trade.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 An engine moves 100 kg/s of air from 250 m/s to 600 m/s (sample values). Find the thrust.

Show the steps
  1. F = 100 × (600 − 250).

Answer: F = 35 kN.

Example 1.2 Find its propulsive efficiency.

Show the steps
  1. η_p = 2/(1 + 600/250).

Answer: η_p = 0.588.

Example 1.3 Check that efficiency with powers.

Show the steps
  1. Thrust power = 35,000 × 250 = 8.75 MW.
  2. Jet power = ½ × 100 × (600² − 250²) = 14.875 MW.

Answer: 8.75/14.875 = 0.588.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Specific thrust of that engine?

    Show answer

    350 N·s/kg.

  2. η_p if V_e = 300 m/s at V₀ = 250 m/s?

    Show answer

    0.909.

  3. Why do fighters use small, fast jets?

    Show answer

    High specific thrust at the cost of efficiency.

  4. When does pressure thrust matter?

    Show answer

    When the nozzle exit pressure differs from ambient, as in many rockets.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: More exhaust speed always means more efficiency.

    Actually: Propulsive efficiency falls as V_e/V₀ grows.

  • Myth: Thrust pushes on the air behind.

    Actually: It is the reaction to accelerating the flow.

  • Myth: Pressure thrust is always zero.

    Actually: Only for a perfectly expanded nozzle.

Flashcards

  • Thrust

    Net momentum flux + pressure term.

  • Specific thrust

    F/ṁ.

  • η_p

    2/(1 + V_e/V₀).

  • Thrust power

    F·V₀.

  • Jet power

    ½ṁ(V_e² − V₀²).

  • Matched nozzle

    p_e = p₀.

Show answer

Show answer

3. Efficient engines move…

Show answer

Show answer

How this helps save the planet

NASA Glenn’s guide explains that most modern airliners use turbofans because of their high thrust and good fuel efficiency, and that high-bypass turbofans are nearly as fuel efficient as turboprops.

That efficiency comes straight from this unit’s equation: moving more air at a speed closer to flight speed raises propulsive efficiency, which means less fuel burned and less CO₂ for the same trip.

Unit 2 · Week 2

The Brayton Cycle and Thermal Efficiency

The ideal Brayton cycle, isentropic compression and expansion, thermal efficiency versus pressure ratio, compressor and turbine work, component efficiencies, and overall efficiency.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute ideal Brayton efficiency from pressure ratio.
  • Find temperatures after isentropic compression.
  • Compute compressor work.
  • Combine thermal and propulsive efficiency.

Key concepts

  • A gas turbine runs the Brayton cycle: isentropic compression, constant-pressure heat addition, isentropic expansion and heat rejection.
  • Ideal thermal efficiency depends only on the pressure ratio: η_th = 1 − π^(−(γ−1)/γ).
  • Isentropic compression raises temperature by T₂/T₁ = π^((γ−1)/γ).
  • Compressor work per kg is c_p(T₂ − T₁); the turbine must supply it before any is left for the jet.
  • Overall efficiency η₀ = η_th × η_p: heat to useful thrust power.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 Find the ideal Brayton efficiency for pressure ratios 10 and 30 (γ = 1.4).

Show the steps
  1. 1 − 10^(−0.2857); 1 − 30^(−0.2857).

Answer: 48.2% and 62.2%.

Example 2.2 Air at 288 K is compressed by a ratio of 30. Find T₂.

Show the steps
  1. T₂ = 288 × 30^0.2857 = 288 × 2.643.

Answer: T₂ = 761 K.

Example 2.3 Find the compressor work per kg.

Show the steps
  1. 1005 × (761 − 288).

Answer: w_c = 475 kJ/kg.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Efficiency at π = 20?

    Show answer

    57.5%.

  2. Efficiency at π = 40?

    Show answer

    65.1%.

  3. Overall efficiency for η_th = 0.5 and η_p = 0.8?

    Show answer

    0.40.

  4. Why not raise π forever?

    Show answer

    Compressor exit gets too hot and heavy; turbine limits.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Brayton efficiency depends on peak temperature.

    Actually: Ideal efficiency depends only on π.

  • Myth: All compressor work is lost.

    Actually: The turbine recovers it from the hot gas.

  • Myth: Thermal efficiency is the whole story.

    Actually: Multiply by propulsive efficiency.

Flashcards

  • Brayton cycle

    Compress, burn, expand.

  • Pressure ratio π

    p₂/p₁.

  • η_th

    1 − π^(−0.2857).

  • Compressor work

    c_pΔT.

  • Overall efficiency

    η_th η_p.

  • Isentropic

    Reversible and adiabatic.

Show answer

pressure ratio. Formula.

Formula.

Show answer

constant pressure. Burner.

Burner.

3. η₀ = …

Show answer

How this helps save the planet

NASA’s Hybrid Thermally Efficient Core (HyTEC) project worked with industry to develop small-core engine technologies that enable fuel-burn reductions, more electric power extraction for aircraft systems, and better compatibility with sustainable aviation fuels. NASA notes the project has concluded.

Smaller, hotter, higher-pressure cores are this unit’s efficiency formula in hardware: raising the pressure ratio raises thermal efficiency, and every percent saves fuel on every flight.

Unit 3 · Weeks 3–4

Compressible Flow: Inlets and Nozzles

Stagnation temperature and pressure, isentropic relations with Mach number, choked flow, area–Mach relations, converging and converging-diverging nozzles, and inlet pressure recovery.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use stagnation-to-static ratios.
  • Find the critical (sonic) conditions.
  • Use the area–Mach relation.
  • Compute ideal nozzle exit velocity.

Key concepts

  • Stagnation temperature T₀ = T(1 + (γ − 1)M²/2); stagnation pressure follows by the isentropic power γ/(γ − 1).
  • At the throat of a choked nozzle M = 1, with T*/T₀ = 0.833 and p*/p₀ = 0.528 for air.
  • Subsonic flow speeds up in a converging duct; supersonic flow speeds up in a diverging one. A/A* gives the area needed for each Mach number.
  • Ideal exit velocity: V_e = √(2c_pT₀(1 − (p_e/p₀)^((γ−1)/γ))).
  • Inlets slow air to subsonic speed for the compressor; losses in stagnation pressure cut thrust and efficiency.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 Find T₀/T and p₀/p at Mach 0.8.

Show the steps
  1. T₀/T = 1 + 0.2 × 0.64.
  2. p₀/p = 1.128^3.5.

Answer: T₀/T = 1.128; p₀/p = 1.524.

Example 3.2 Find A/A* for Mach 2.

Show the steps
  1. Area–Mach relation with γ = 1.4.

Answer: A/A* = 1.6875.

Example 3.3 Gas at T₀ = 1000 K expands to half its stagnation pressure (sample values). Find V_e.

Show the steps
  1. V_e = √(2 × 1005 × 1000 × (1 − 0.5^0.2857)).

Answer: V_e = 601 m/s.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Critical pressure ratio for air?

    Show answer

    p*/p₀ = 0.528.

  2. T*/T₀?

    Show answer

    0.833.

  3. A/A* at Mach 1.5?

    Show answer

    1.176.

  4. A/A* at Mach 3?

    Show answer

    4.235.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Nozzles always converge.

    Actually: Supersonic exits need a diverging section.

  • Myth: Static and stagnation values are the same.

    Actually: They differ at speed.

  • Myth: Choking stops all flow.

    Actually: It caps the mass flow for given upstream conditions.

Flashcards

  • Stagnation temperature

    T(1 + 0.2M²) for air.

  • Choked

    M = 1 at the throat.

  • A/A*

    Area for a Mach number.

  • p*/p₀

    0.528.

  • Converging–diverging

    For supersonic exits.

  • Pressure recovery

    Inlet p₀ kept.

Self-check quiz

1. At a choked throat M = …

Show answer

1. Sonic.

Sonic.

2. Supersonic flow accelerates in a…

Show answer

diverging duct. Area–Mach.

Area–Mach.

3. p*/p₀ for air…

Show answer

0.528. γ = 1.4.

4. T₀/T at M = 0.8…

Show answer

How this helps save the planet

NASA’s RS-25 page notes that during Artemis I, engine thrust and mixture-ratio control valves were within 0.5 percent of pre-flight predicted values and internal pressures and temperatures were within two percent.

Predictions that close come from the isentropic-flow relations in this unit, refined by test data: getting nozzle and chamber conditions right is what makes an engine perform as designed.

Unit 4 · Weeks 5–6

Turbojets: Components and Cycle Analysis

Inlet, compressor, burner, turbine and nozzle; work balance between compressor and turbine; turbine inlet temperature limits; afterburners; and real component efficiencies.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Trace stations through a turbojet.
  • Balance compressor and turbine work.
  • Explain turbine temperature limits.
  • Describe how afterburning trades fuel for thrust.

Key concepts

  • Stations: free stream, inlet, compressor exit, burner exit (turbine inlet), turbine exit and nozzle exit.
  • The turbine drives the compressor on a shared shaft: c_p(T₄ − T₅) = c_p(T₃ − T₂) for ideal flow with equal c_p.
  • Higher turbine inlet temperature gives more specific thrust and efficiency, limited by blade materials and cooling.
  • Real compressors and turbines have isentropic efficiencies below 1, so more work is needed and less is recovered.
  • Afterburners add fuel after the turbine for extra thrust at a large fuel cost.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 With a 475 kJ/kg compressor (Unit 2) and turbine inlet 1600 K (sample value), find the turbine exit temperature.

Show the steps
  1. ΔT = 475,440/1005 = 473 K.

Answer: T₅ = 1127 K.

Example 4.2 A real compressor with η_c = 0.85 needs how much work for the same pressure ratio?

Show the steps
  1. 475/0.85.

Answer: 559 kJ/kg.

Example 4.3 Find the thrust of a turbojet moving 100 kg/s from 250 to 450 m/s.

Show the steps
  1. 100 × 200.

Answer: 20 kN.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Temperature rise of that real compressor?

    Show answer

    559,341/1005 = 557 K.

  2. Why cool turbine blades?

    Show answer

    Gas is hotter than the metal can survive.

  3. What does an afterburner raise?

    Show answer

    V_e and thrust.

  4. What does it lower?

    Show answer

    Propulsive efficiency and range.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: The turbine makes the thrust.

    Actually: It mainly drives the compressor; the nozzle makes thrust.

  • Myth: Hotter is always possible.

    Actually: Materials limit T₄.

  • Myth: Afterburners are efficient.

    Actually: They burn fuel at low pressure.

Flashcards

  • Turbojet

    Compressor–burner–turbine–nozzle.

  • T₄

    Turbine inlet temperature.

  • Work balance

    Turbine = compressor.

  • η_c

    Ideal/actual ΔT.

  • Afterburner

    Burn after the turbine.

  • Station numbers

    0 free stream to 9 nozzle exit.

Show answer

compressor work. Shared shaft.

Show answer

materials. Blades.

Blades.

3. η_c < 1 means…

Show answer

How this helps save the planet

NASA Glenn’s guide notes that low-bypass turbofans are still more fuel efficient than basic turbojets, and that many modern fighters use low-bypass turbofans with afterburners so they can cruise efficiently but still have high thrust when needed.

The pure turbojet survives mainly as a teaching model: its simple work balance shows why later engines added a fan to cut fuel burn.

Unit 5 · Week 7

Turbofans and Turboprops

Bypass ratio, core and fan streams, the turbofan thrust equation, propulsive efficiency of the fan stream, turboprops and open rotors, and the size and weight trade.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Apply the turbofan thrust equation.
  • Compute bypass ratio and total flow.
  • Compare turbofan and turbojet thrust for the same core.
  • Explain the limits of very high bypass.

Key concepts

  • Bypass ratio bpr = ṁ_fan/ṁ_core; total flow ṁ₀ = ṁ_fan + ṁ_core.
  • NASA Glenn’s turbofan thrust: F = (ṁV)_e + bpr·ṁ_c·V_f − ṁ₀V₀.
  • The fan accelerates lots of air a little, so its stream has high propulsive efficiency.
  • Turboprops and open rotors push bypass even higher, efficient at lower speeds.
  • Bigger fans add weight, drag and noise and need slower fan speeds, often with a gearbox.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 A turbofan has core flow 100 kg/s and bpr = 10, with V_e = 450 m/s, V_f = 300 m/s and V₀ = 250 m/s (sample values). Find the total flow and thrust.

Show the steps
  1. ṁ₀ = 100 + 1000 = 1100 kg/s.
  2. F = 100 × 450 + 1000 × 300 − 1100 × 250.

Answer: F = 70 kN.

Example 5.2 Compare with a turbojet using the same core flow and exit speed.

Show the steps
  1. F = 100 × (450 − 250).

Answer: 20 kN: the fan multiplies thrust by 3.5.

Example 5.3 Find the fan stream’s propulsive efficiency.

Show the steps
  1. 2/(1 + 300/250).

Answer: η_p = 0.909.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Core-stream η_p (450 at 250 m/s)?

    Show answer

    0.714.

  2. Thrust ratio turbofan/turbojet?

    Show answer

    3.5.

  3. Why gear the fan?

    Show answer

    So the fan turns slower than the low-pressure turbine.

  4. Why are props used on slow aircraft?

    Show answer

    Efficient at low speed; tips go supersonic at high speed.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: The fan makes little thrust.

    Actually: In high-bypass engines it makes most of it.

  • Myth: Higher bypass is always better.

    Actually: Weight and drag grow with fan size.

  • Myth: Turbofans burn more fuel than turbojets.

    Actually: They burn less for the same thrust.

Flashcards

  • Bypass ratio

    Fan flow/core flow.

  • Turbofan thrust

    Core + fan − inlet momentum.

  • Fan stream

    High η_p.

  • Turboprop

    Very high bypass via propeller.

  • Geared fan

    Slower fan, faster turbine.

  • Open rotor

    Unducted fan.

Self-check quiz

1. bpr = …

Show answer

ṁ_f/ṁ_c. Definition.

Definition.

2. High-bypass fans…

Show answer

move lots of air slowly. Efficiency.

Efficiency.

Show answer

How this helps save the planet

NASA Glenn explains that because the core’s fuel flow changes only a small amount when a fan is added, a turbofan generates more thrust for nearly the same fuel, which is why it is very fuel efficient.

This unit’s example shows the effect: the same 100 kg/s core makes 20 kN as a turbojet and 70 kN with a bypass-ratio-10 fan, for nearly the same core fuel flow.

Unit 6 · Week 8

Fuel Burn, TSFC and Emissions

Fuel heating value, overall efficiency, thrust-specific fuel consumption, CO₂ from burning jet fuel, and how engine efficiency links to range and climate impact.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute overall efficiency from fuel flow.
  • Compute TSFC.
  • Estimate CO₂ emissions from fuel burn.
  • Link TSFC to range.

Key concepts

  • Fuel power is ṁ_f × LHV; jet fuel’s lower heating value is about 43 MJ/kg (textbook value).
  • Overall efficiency η₀ = F·V₀/(ṁ_f·LHV).
  • Thrust-specific fuel consumption TSFC = ṁ_f/F; lower is better.
  • Burning 1 kg of jet fuel makes about 3.16 kg of CO₂ (textbook value), since carbon picks up oxygen from the air.
  • In the Breguet range equation, range grows with η₀ × LHV, so a better engine flies farther or burns less fuel.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 6.1 The Unit 5 turbofan (70 kN at 250 m/s) burns 1.2 kg/s of fuel with LHV 43 MJ/kg (sample values). Find η₀.

Show the steps
  1. η₀ = 70,000 × 250/(1.2 × 43 × 10⁶).

Answer: η₀ = 0.339.

Example 6.2 Find its TSFC.

Show the steps
  1. 1.2/70,000 kg/(N·s).

Answer: TSFC = 17.14 mg/(N·s).

Example 6.3 Find CO₂ per hour.

Show the steps
  1. Fuel = 1.2 × 3600 = 4320 kg.

Rocket Fundamentals and Specific Impulse

Rocket thrust, effective exhaust velocity, specific impulse, total impulse, mass flow, and real numbers from NASA’s RS-25 engine.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use F = ṁV_eq.
  • Convert between Isp and exhaust velocity.
  • Compute total impulse.
  • Work with RS-25 thrust data.

Key concepts

  • A rocket carries all its propellant, so thrust is F = ṁV_e + (p_e − p₀)A_e = ṁV_eq.
  • NASA Glenn defines specific impulse Isp = F/(ṁg₀), in seconds; V_eq = Isp × g₀.
  • Total impulse I = F × Δt measures what a motor delivers overall.
  • Higher Isp means less propellant for the same impulse.
  • Thrust is often quoted as a percent of a rated level, as with the RS-25.

Key equations

Unit 8 · Week 11

Rocket Nozzles, Propellants and Throttling

Chamber conditions, nozzle expansion, exit velocity from the isentropic relations, expansion ratio and altitude, propellant choices and throttling.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute ideal rocket exhaust velocity.
  • Explain over- and under-expansion.
  • Compare propellant properties.
  • Relate throttle settings to thrust.

Key concepts

  • Ideal exit velocity: V_e = √(2γRT₀/(γ − 1) × (1 − (p_e/p₀)^((γ−1)/γ))), with R the gas constant per kg of exhaust.
  • Hot, light exhaust (high T₀/M) gives high V_e: hydrogen–oxygen engines lead chemical Isp.
  • Bigger expansion ratios lower p_e and raise V_e, but a nozzle sized for vacuum overexpands at sea level.
  • Thrust coefficient and chamber pressure link nozzle size to thrust.
  • Throttling varies propellant flow and chamber pressure; NASA quotes RS-25 power levels as percent of rated thrust.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Unit 9 · Week 12

The Rocket Equation and Electric Propulsion

The Tsiolkovsky equation, mass ratio, staging, electric thrusters, power-limited thrust, and the trade between Isp and thrust.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use the rocket equation.
  • Compare chemical and electric Δv for the same propellant.
  • Compute power-limited thrust.
  • Explain why electric thrusters are low thrust.

Key concepts

  • Tsiolkovsky: Δv = V_e ln(m₀/m_f).
  • Staging drops empty mass so later stages see better mass ratios.
  • Electric thrusters (ion, Hall) reach very high Isp but are limited by power.
  • Jet power ½ṁV_e² = ηP, so thrust F = 2ηP/V_e: higher V_e means less thrust for the same power.
  • Electric propulsion suits long, patient missions; chemical rockets suit launch and quick maneuvers.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Show the steps

Answer: V_e = 17,652 m/s.

Show the steps
  1. F = 2 × 0.5 × 4500/17,652.

Answer: F = 0.255 N.

Example 9.3 A 1000 kg craft uses 100 kg of propellant. Compare Δv with this thruster and with a 300 s chemical engine.

Show the steps
  1. 17,652 ln(1000/900); 2942 ln(1000/900).

Answer: About 1860 m/s versus 310 m/s.

    Actually: They still expel mass.

Unit 10 · Week 13

Sustainable Propulsion: SAF, Hydrogen and Electric Flight

Sustainable aviation fuels, energy density per kilogram, hydrogen and batteries compared with jet fuel, and what the numbers mean for future aircraft.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compare specific energies of fuels and batteries.
  • Describe SAF feedstocks and pathways.
  • Estimate fuel energy changes.
  • Weigh hydrogen’s mass and volume trade.

Key concepts

  • Specific energy (MJ/kg) sets how far an aircraft can fly per kilogram carried; jet fuel ≈ 43 MJ/kg, liquid hydrogen ≈ 120 MJ/kg and good batteries ≈ 0.25 MJ/kg (textbook values).
  • SAF is made from biomass, wastes or captured carbon and can drop into existing engines with blending limits.
  • Hydrogen has high energy per kilogram but very low density, so tanks are large and heavy.
  • Batteries suit short flights and small aircraft; their low specific energy limits range.
  • Life-cycle emissions depend on how the fuel is made, not just what comes out of the engine.

Glossary

Afterburner
Burner after the turbine for extra thrust.
Area ratio
A/A*, nozzle area over throat area.
Brayton cycle
Gas-turbine thermodynamic cycle.
Bypass ratio
Fan flow over core flow.
Choked flow
Sonic flow at a throat that caps mass flow.
Compressor
Raises air pressure.
Hall thruster
Electric thruster using crossed fields.
Isentropic
Reversible and adiabatic.
Mach number
Speed over sound speed.
Overall efficiency
Thrust power over fuel power.
Pressure ratio
Compressor exit over inlet pressure.
Propulsive efficiency
Thrust power over jet power.
Rocket equation
Δv = V_e ln(m₀/m_f).
SAF
Sustainable aviation fuel.
Specific impulse
Thrust per weight flow of propellant.
Specific thrust
Thrust per unit air flow.
Stagnation temperature
Temperature if flow is brought to rest adiabatically.
TSFC
Fuel flow per thrust.
Turbine
Extracts work to drive the compressor.
Turbofan
Gas turbine with a bypass fan.
