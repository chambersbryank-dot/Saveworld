About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a one-semester senior flight dynamics and controls course for aerospace engineers. It is modeled on the topics and depth of MIT 16.333 Aircraft Stability and Control, MIT 16.06 Principles of Automatic Control and MIT 16.30 Feedback Control Systems (free on MIT OpenCourseWare), using NASA Glenn’s free Beginner’s Guide to Aeronautics, which we link where it helps. None of those authors, schools or agencies endorses this page.

What we add: every unit ties to real work with a cited public source (NASA’s X-59 flight tests, Hubble’s pointing system and the Dragonfly rotorcraft), every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab has a free simulation or a spreadsheet.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Stable, well-controlled vehicles fly efficient paths, and the same control ideas point Earth-observing satellites and telescopes precisely enough to measure our planet and universe.

Control engineering also makes new, quieter and more autonomous aircraft possible. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample or textbook values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Dynamics: rigid-body rotation, moments of inertia and angular momentum.
  • Differential Equations: linear ODEs and the Laplace transform.
  • Linear Algebra: matrices and eigenvalues.
  • Aerodynamics I and Aircraft Performance: lift, drag, moments and turning flight.

How to study

  • Draw the body axes and the center of gravity before writing any moment equation.
  • Keep angles in radians inside formulas; convert to degrees only for the answer.
  • Separate static stability (the first tendency after a disturbance) from dynamic stability (what happens over time).
  • Sketch the poles in the s-plane for every system you meet.
  • Check every answer against physical sense: stable systems settle, unstable ones grow.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer, and retake the quizzes a few days later.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer or tablet for the free PhET Balancing Act and Masses and Springs simulations.
  • Spreadsheet software (any free one works), or Python.
  • NASA Glenn’s Beginner’s Guide to Aeronautics pages in a web browser.
  • A calculator.

Unit 1 · Week 1

Axes, Rotations and Moments

Body axes through the center of gravity, roll, pitch and yaw, control surfaces and the moments they make, finding the center of gravity, and Euler angles.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Define the roll, pitch and yaw axes.
  • Locate a center of gravity from component masses.
  • Compute the moment of a control force.
  • Match each control surface to its moment.

Key concepts

  • In flight an aircraft rotates about its center of gravity (cg), the average location of its mass.
  • Body axes: roll toward the nose, pitch toward the right wing tip, yaw toward the bottom of the aircraft.
  • Control surfaces make aerodynamic forces at a distance from the cg, producing moments: elevators pitch, the rudder yaws, ailerons roll.
  • The cg of several masses is x_cg = Σm_ix_i/Σm_i; moving it changes stability and control.
  • Euler angles (yaw ψ, pitch θ, roll φ, applied in that order) describe attitude relative to the Earth.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 A tail force of 2000 N acts 6 m behind the cg (sample values). Find the pitching moment.

Show the steps
  1. M = 2000 × 6.

Answer: 12,000 N·m.

Example 1.2 Masses of 1000 kg at 2 m, 500 kg at 6 m and 300 kg at 10 m from the nose (sample values). Find the cg.

Show the steps
  1. Σm = 1800 kg.
  2. Σmx = 2000 + 3000 + 3000 = 8000 kg·m.

Answer: x_cg = 4.44 m.

Example 1.3 Which surface rolls the aircraft?

Show the steps
  1. Ailerons deflect in opposite directions on each wing.

Answer: Ailerons produce a rolling moment.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Moment of 500 N at 4 m?

    Show answer

    2000 N·m.

  2. Which surface yaws the nose?

    Show answer

    The rudder.

  3. Add 200 kg at 10 m to the example. New cg?

    Show answer

    10,000/2000 = 5.0 m.

  4. About which point does a kite rotate?

    Show answer

    Its bridle point.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Aircraft rotate about their nose.

    Actually: They rotate about the cg.

  • Myth: Elevators roll the aircraft.

    Actually: They pitch it.

  • Myth: Euler angle order does not matter.

    Actually: Rotations do not commute.

    Average location of mass.

  • Roll

    Rotation about the nose axis.

  • Pitch

    Nose up or down.

  • Yaw

    Nose side to side.

  • Moment

    Force × distance.

  • Euler angles

    ψ, θ, φ.

How this helps save the planet

NASA Glenn explains that control surfaces produce forces at their centers of pressure, some distance from the cg, creating torques about the principal axes: elevators pitch, the rudder yaws and ailerons roll. The first aircraft to show active control about all three axes was the Wrights’ 1902 glider.

Every modern efficient airliner and Earth-observing aircraft still uses those three moments, now often commanded through flight-control computers.

Unit 2 · Week 2

Longitudinal Static Stability

Pitching-moment coefficient, the stability derivative C_mα, the neutral point, static margin, and how cg position and tail size set stability.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • State the condition for static pitch stability.
  • Compute static margin.
  • Relate static margin to C_mα.
  • Explain the effect of moving the cg.

Key concepts

  • Static stability is the first tendency after a disturbance: a statically stable aircraft makes a moment that pushes it back.
  • In pitch this needs C_mα < 0: more angle of attack makes a nose-down moment.
  • The neutral point is the cg location where C_mα = 0.
  • Static margin SM = (x_np − x_cg)/c̄; positive means stable. Approximately C_mα = −C_Lα × SM.
  • Moving the cg aft reduces stability and control forces; too far aft makes the aircraft unstable unless a computer stabilizes it.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 The neutral point is at 0.35c̄ and the cg at 0.25c̄ (sample values). Find the static margin.

Show the steps
  1. SM = 0.35 − 0.25.

Answer: SM = 0.10 (10%).

Example 2.2 With C_Lα = 5.5 per radian, find C_mα.

Show the steps
  1. C_mα = −5.5 × 0.10.

Answer: C_mα = −0.55 per radian: stable.

Example 2.3 Move the cg to 0.30c̄. Find the new C_mα.

Show the steps
  1. SM = 0.05; −5.5 × 0.05.

Answer: −0.275 per radian: half as stiff.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. SM with cg at 0.35c̄?

    Show answer

    0: neutrally stable.

  2. Sign of C_mα for cg at 0.40c̄?

    Show answer

    Positive: unstable.

  3. Does a bigger tail move the neutral point fore or aft?

    Show answer

    Aft, raising static margin.

  4. Why fly with small margins?

    Show answer

    Less trim drag and lighter controls.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Static stability guarantees dynamic stability.

    Actually: It is only the first tendency.

  • Myth: Aft cg is always safer.

    Actually: It reduces stability.

  • Myth: C_mα should be positive.

    Actually: Negative is stable.

    First tendency to return.

    Pitch stiffness.

  • Neutral point

    cg where C_mα = 0.

  • Static margin

    (x_np − x_cg)/c̄.

  • Mean chord c̄

    Reference length.

  • Relaxed stability

    Small or negative margin with computer help.

How this helps save the planet

NASA reports that after 25 flights the X-59’s real-world flight characteristics closely match simulations, including its flying qualities and its stability margin, which NASA describes as how close the aircraft is to becoming unstable under certain conditions.

The X-59 is shaped to turn a loud sonic boom into a quiet thump, and confirming its stability margin in flight is part of proving that quieter supersonic flight is practical.

Unit 3 · Week 3

Trim and Elevator Control

Trim as zero net pitching moment, elevator effectiveness, the elevator angle to trim, tail download and trim drag, and stick-fixed versus stick-free stability.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Write the trim condition.
  • Solve for elevator angle to trim.
  • Explain why tails often push down.
  • Describe trim drag.

Key concepts

  • Trim means zero net pitching moment: C_m = C_m0 + C_mα α + C_mδe δ_e = 0.
  • Elevator effectiveness C_mδe is negative for a tail elevator: trailing edge down gives nose down.
  • The elevator lift acts some distance from the cg, so it makes a torque and the aircraft rotates about its cg.
  • Modern tails often carry negative lift to keep the nose up, which the wing must offset with extra lift.
  • That extra lift costs induced drag, called trim drag; a smaller static margin reduces it.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 C_m0 = 0.05, C_mα = −0.55, C_mδe = −1.2 per radian (sample values). Find δ_e to trim at α = 0.05 rad.

Show the steps
  1. 0.05 − 0.0275 − 1.2δ_e = 0.
  2. δ_e = 0.0225/1.2.

Answer: δ_e = 0.01875 rad = 1.07°.

Example 3.2 What angle of attack trims with zero elevator?

Show the steps
  1. α = 0.05/0.55.

Answer: α = 0.0909 rad = 5.21°.

Example 3.3 Find C_L at that trim with C_Lα = 5.5.

Show the steps
  1. 5.5 × 0.0909.

Answer: C_L = 0.50.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. δ_e to trim at α = 0.1 rad?

    Show answer

    −0.00417 rad (−0.24°).

  2. Sign convention for δ_e?

    Show answer

    Trailing edge down positive.

  3. Why does tail download cost fuel?

    Show answer

    The wing makes more lift, adding induced drag.

  4. Where was the Wright Flyer’s elevator?

    Show answer

    At the front.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Trim means level flight.

    Actually: It means zero net moment at any steady condition.

  • Myth: Tails always lift upward.

    Actually: Many push down.

  • Myth: Elevator only changes pitch angle.

    Actually: It sets the trimmed angle of attack and speed.

Flashcards

  • Trim

    Zero net moment.

  • C_mδe

    Elevator effectiveness.

  • Elevator

    Pitch control surface.

  • Tail download

    Negative tail lift.

  • Trim drag

    Drag from trimming.

  • Canard

    Forward elevator surface.

Self-check quiz

Show answer

0. No net moment.

No net moment.

2. Example δ_e…

Show answer

1.07°. Worked.

Worked.

3. Trim drag comes from…

Show answer

Show answer

How this helps save the planet

NASA Glenn explains that the horizontal stabilizer on modern aircraft is usually set at a negative angle, making a downward force that keeps the tail down and the airplane trimmed, and that this negative force must be overcome with greater lift from the wing.

Every bit of extra lift adds drag and fuel burn, so careful trim and cg management are small but real ways to cut aviation emissions.

Unit 4 · Week 4

Lateral-Directional Stability and Turns

Weathercock (directional) stability, dihedral effect, aileron roll control, roll damping and the roll mode, adverse yaw, and the coordinated turn.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • State directional and roll stability conditions.
  • Compute roll-mode time constant and steady roll rate.
  • Find bank angle and load factor for a coordinated turn.
  • Explain adverse yaw.

Key concepts

  • Directional (weathercock) stability needs C_nβ > 0: a sideslip yaws the nose back into the wind.
  • Dihedral effect needs C_lβ < 0: a sideslip rolls the aircraft away from the slip.
  • Ailerons deflect oppositely to make a rolling moment; roll damping L_p resists roll rate, giving the roll mode with τ = 1/|L_p|.
  • The down-going aileron makes more lift and drag, yawing the nose away from the turn (adverse yaw); the rudder fixes it.
  • In a coordinated level turn tan φ = V²/(gR) and load factor n = 1/cos φ.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 A turn at 100 m/s with radius 2000 m (sample values). Find the bank angle.

Show the steps
  1. tan φ = 100²/(9.80665 × 2000) = 0.510.

Answer: φ = 27.0°.

Example 4.2 Find the load factor.

Show the steps
  1. n = 1/cos 27.0°.

Answer: n = 1.122.

Example 4.3 L_δa = 2.0 s⁻², L_p = −4.0 s⁻¹ (sample values), δ_a = 0.1 rad. Find τ and steady roll rate.

Show the steps
  1. τ = 1/4.
  2. p = 2.0 × 0.1/4.0.

Answer: τ = 0.25 s; p = 0.05 rad/s.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Load factor at 60° bank?

    Show answer

    2.0.

  2. Radius at 100 m/s and 45° bank?

    Show answer

    1020 m.

  3. What does dihedral do?

    Show answer

    Rolls the aircraft level after a sideslip.

  4. Why use the rudder in a turn?

    Show answer

    To cancel adverse yaw.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Ailerons alone make a clean turn.

    Actually: Adverse yaw needs rudder.

  • Myth: Bank angle is independent of speed.

    Actually: It grows with V² for a fixed radius.

  • Myth: C_nβ should be negative.

    Actually: Positive is stable.

Flashcards

  • Sideslip β

    Wind angle from the side.

  • C_nβ

    Directional stability.

  • C_lβ

    Dihedral effect.

  • Roll damping

    L_p.

  • Adverse yaw

    Yaw opposite the roll.

  • Load factor

    Lift/weight.

Show answer

C_nβ > 0. Restoring yaw.

2. Example bank…

Show answer

How this helps save the planet

NASA Glenn’s guide explains that ailerons usually work in opposition, one up and one down, to change lift on each wing, and that airplanes turn because of the banking created by the ailerons, not because of a rudder input: the bank tilts the wing’s lift and curves the flight path.

This unit’s turn equation is that idea in numbers. Coordinated, efficient turns keep extra drag, and so extra fuel burn, small.

Unit 5 · Weeks 5–6

Equations of Motion and Dynamic Modes

Six-degree-of-freedom equations, small-disturbance linearization, state-space form, eigenvalues, and the short-period, phugoid, roll, spiral and Dutch-roll modes.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Linearize equations about trim.
  • Write state-space form.
  • Find natural frequency and damping from a characteristic equation.
  • Estimate the phugoid period.

Key concepts

  • Newton’s laws in body axes give six coupled nonlinear equations; for small disturbances about trim they become linear.
  • State-space form ẋ = Ax + Bu; the eigenvalues of A are the modes.
  • Longitudinal modes: a fast, well-damped short period (pitch) and a slow, lightly damped phugoid (speed–altitude exchange).
  • Lateral modes: a fast roll mode, a slow spiral mode and the oscillating Dutch roll.
  • A second-order mode s² + 2ζω_n s + ω_n² has natural frequency ω_n and damping ratio ζ. The phugoid approximation is ω_n ≈ √2 g/V.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 A short-period mode has s² + 2.8s + 16 = 0 (sample values). Find ω_n and ζ.

Show the steps
  1. ω_n = √16.
  2. ζ = 2.8/(2 × 4).

Answer: ω_n = 4 rad/s; ζ = 0.35.

Example 5.2 Estimate the phugoid frequency and period at 100 m/s.

Show the steps
  1. ω = 1.4142 × 9.80665/100.
  2. T = 2π/ω.

Answer: ω = 0.1387 rad/s; T = 45.3 s.

Example 5.3 Find the damped frequency of the short period.

Show the steps
  1. ω_d = 4√(1 − 0.35²).

Answer: ω_d = 3.75 rad/s.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Phugoid period at 200 m/s?

    Show answer

  2. ζ for s² + 4s + 16?

    Show answer

    0.5.

  3. Which mode trades speed for height?

    Show answer

    Phugoid.

  4. Which lateral mode oscillates?

    Show answer

    Dutch roll.

  • Myth: Linear models work at any condition.

    Actually: Only near the trim point.

  • Myth: All modes oscillate.

    Actually: Roll and spiral are not oscillatory.

  • Myth: Phugoid is fast.

    Actually: It takes tens of seconds.

Unit 6 · Week 7

Laplace Transforms and Transfer Functions

Transfer functions, poles and zeros, block diagrams, first-order systems, time constants, DC gain and the final value theorem.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Write a transfer function from an ODE.
  • Find poles, DC gain and time constant.
  • Use the final value theorem.
  • Reduce simple block diagrams.

Key concepts

  • With zero initial conditions, the Laplace transform turns a linear ODE into algebra: Y(s) = G(s)U(s).
  • Poles (roots of the denominator) set the natural response; left-half-plane poles decay, right-half-plane poles grow.
  • A first-order system K/(τs + 1) reaches 63.2% of its final value at t = τ and about 98% at 4τ.
  • The final value theorem: y(∞) = lim s→0 of sY(s), if the system is stable. For a unit step, y(∞) = G(0).
  • Blocks in series multiply; a negative feedback loop gives G/(1 + GH).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 6.1 Find the pole, time constant and DC gain of G(s) = 5/(s + 2).

Show the steps
  1. Pole at s = −2.
  2. τ = 1/2.
  3. G(0) = 5/2.

Answer: Pole −2; τ = 0.5 s; gain 2.5.

Example 6.2 Find its unit-step response at t = 0.5 s and 1 s.

Show the steps
  1. y = 2.5(1 − e^(−2t)).

Answer: y(0.5) = 1.580; y(1) = 2.162.

Example 6.3 Close a unity feedback loop around G. Find the closed-loop DC gain.

Show the steps
  1. T = 5/(s + 7).

Answer: T(0) = 0.714.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. 2% settling time of G?

    Show answer

    About 4τ = 2 s.

  2. Fraction reached at t = τ?

    Show answer

    63.2%.

  3. Is a pole at +1 stable?

    Show answer

    No; it grows as e^t.

  4. Two blocks 2 and 3/(s+1) in series?

    Show answer

    6/(s+1).

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Final value theorem always applies.

    Actually: Only for stable systems.

Unit 7 · Week 8

Second-Order Time Response

Underdamped step response, overshoot, peak time, settling time, damping ratio and natural frequency, and handling-quality targets.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute percent overshoot from ζ.
  • Compute peak and settling times.
  • Read ζ and ω_n from pole locations.
  • Choose damping for good response.

Key concepts

  • Poles at −ζω_n ± jω_n√(1 − ζ²) for 0 < ζ < 1 give an oscillating response that decays.
  • Percent overshoot depends only on ζ: M_p = exp(−ζπ/√(1 − ζ²)).
  • Peak time t_p = π/ω_d, with ω_d = ω_n√(1 − ζ²).
  • Settling time (2%) is about t_s = 4/(ζω_n), set by the real part of the poles.
  • Damping near 0.7 gives small overshoot (about 4.6%) with a quick rise; pilots dislike both sluggish and oscillating responses.

Unit 8 · Weeks 9–10

Feedback and PID Control

Why feedback, proportional, integral and derivative action, steady-state error and system type, gain selection for damping, and a pitch-attitude autopilot example.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Close a loop and find the characteristic equation.
  • Choose a gain for a target damping.
  • Compute steady-state error.
  • Explain what D and I terms add.

Key concepts

  • Feedback compares the measured output to the command and acts on the error, reducing sensitivity to disturbances and model errors.
  • Proportional gain speeds response but can reduce damping; derivative action adds damping; integral action removes steady-state error.
  • For a plant 1/(s(s + 2)) with gain K, the closed loop is s² + 2s + K = 0, so ω_n = √K and ζ = 1/√K.
  • System type (integrators in the loop) sets steady-state error: a type-1 loop has zero step error and ramp error a/K for this plant.
  • PID: u = K_p e + K_i∫e dt + K_d de/dt.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Stability Analysis: Routh, Root Locus and Margins

The Routh–Hurwitz test, root-locus sketching, frequency response and Bode plots, gain and phase margin, and the link between phase margin and damping.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Apply the Routh test to find a gain limit.
  • Sketch how poles move with gain.
  • Compute a phase margin.
  • Relate phase margin to damping.

Key concepts

  • Routh–Hurwitz finds how many poles are in the right half plane without solving the polynomial.
  • The root locus shows closed-loop pole paths as gain rises from 0 to ∞.
  • Bode plots show loop gain and phase versus frequency; gain margin and phase margin measure distance from instability.
  • Phase margin PM = 180° + ∠G(jω_c) at the crossover frequency where |G| = 1.

Rigid-body rotation, attitude sensors (gyros, star trackers), reaction wheels and momentum, bang-bang slews, safe modes, and pointing for Earth and space science.

Key concepts

Glossary

Aileron
Roll control surface.
Center of gravity
Average location of mass.
Damping ratio
ζ, how fast oscillations decay.
Dutch roll
Coupled yaw–roll oscillation.
Elevator
Pitch control surface.
Gain margin
Gain increase to instability.
Gyroscope
Sensor that measures rotation rate.
Natural frequency
ω_n of an undamped mode.
Neutral point
cg location with neutral pitch stability.
Overshoot
Peak above the final value.
PID controller
Proportional-integral-derivative control.
Phase margin
Extra phase lag to instability.
Phugoid
Slow speed–altitude mode.
Reaction wheel
Spinning wheel for attitude control.
Root locus
Closed-loop pole paths with gain.
Rudder
Yaw control surface.
Short period
Fast pitch mode.
Static margin
Distance from cg to neutral point over chord.
Transfer function
Laplace-domain output over input.
Trim
Zero net moment.
