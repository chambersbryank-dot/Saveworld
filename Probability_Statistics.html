About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a one-semester junior probability and statistics course for engineers. It is modeled on the topics and depth of MIT 18.05 Introduction to Probability and Statistics and MIT 6.041 Probabilistic Systems Analysis (free on MIT OpenCourseWare), using the free OpenStax Introductory Statistics text and the NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods, which we link where they help. None of those authors, schools or agencies endorses this page.

What we add: every unit ties to real work with a cited public source (USGS flood and earthquake probabilities, NOAA hurricane outlooks and forecast cones, the Mauna Loa CO₂ record and NASA’s temperature analysis), every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab has a free simulation or a spreadsheet.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Protecting people and the planet is a question of odds: how likely a flood, earthquake or hurricane is, how sure we are that CO₂ is rising faster, and how reliable the systems that warn us are.

Statistics turns data into those odds and tells us how much to trust them. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample or textbook values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Calculus I and II: derivatives, integrals and series.
  • Linear Algebra: vectors and least squares (helpful for regression).
  • Intro Programming or spreadsheet skills: formulas, ranges and charts.
  • Numerical Methods (helpful, not required).

How to study

  • Write down the random variable and its units before computing anything.
  • Draw the distribution or a tree diagram first.
  • Check that probabilities lie between 0 and 1 and add up to 1.
  • State assumptions such as independence out loud; most errors start there.
  • Simulate when in doubt: a spreadsheet with RAND() can check almost any probability.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer, and retake the quizzes a few days later.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer or tablet for the free PhET Center and Variability, Plinko Probability and Least-Squares Regression simulations.
  • Spreadsheet software (any free one works) with RAND(), NORM.DIST and LINEST, or Python.
  • NOAA’s Mauna Loa CO₂ data file in a web browser.
  • A calculator; a pair of dice is optional.

Unit 1 · Week 1

Describing Data: Center, Spread and Plots

Types of data, histograms and box plots, mean and median, range, variance and standard deviation, quartiles and outliers, and why the sample standard deviation divides by n − 1.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute mean, median, range and sample standard deviation.
  • Choose between mean and median.
  • Read a histogram and box plot.
  • Flag outliers with the 1.5 IQR rule.

Key concepts

  • The mean x̄ = Σx/n is the balance point; the median is the middle value and resists outliers.
  • The sample variance s² = Σ(x − x̄)²/(n − 1) measures spread; s, its square root, has the data’s units. Dividing by n − 1 corrects for using x̄ instead of the true mean.
  • The range is max − min; the interquartile range IQR = Q3 − Q1 holds the middle half of the data.
  • Points more than 1.5 IQR beyond the quartiles are flagged as possible outliers: check them, do not just delete them.
  • Always plot first: a histogram or box plot shows skew, gaps and outliers that a single number hides.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 NOAA’s Mauna Loa annual means give yearly CO₂ increases for 2021–2025 of 2.20, 2.12, 2.55, 3.53 and 2.74 ppm. Find the mean and median.

Show the steps
  1. Sum = 13.14; divide by 5.
  2. Sort: 2.12, 2.20, 2.55, 2.74, 3.53.

Answer: Mean 2.628 ppm/yr; median 2.55 ppm/yr.

Example 1.2 Find the range and sample standard deviation.

Show the steps
  1. Range = 3.53 − 2.12.
  2. s = √(Σ(x − 2.628)²/4).

Answer: Range 1.41 ppm/yr; s = 0.564 ppm/yr.

Example 1.3 Find the z-score of the 3.53 ppm increase (2024).

Show the steps
  1. z = (3.53 − 2.628)/0.564.

Answer: z = 1.60.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Which is pulled more by the 3.53 value, mean or median?

    Show answer

    The mean (2.628 vs 2.55).

  2. Why divide by n − 1?

    Show answer

    The deviations from x̄ are slightly too small; n − 1 corrects the bias.

  3. Units of s for CO₂ increases?

    Show answer

    ppm per year.

  4. What does a long right tail do to mean vs median?

    Show answer

    Mean > median.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: The mean is always typical.

    Actually: Skew or outliers can pull it away.

  • Myth: Outliers are mistakes.

    Actually: They may be real events; investigate.

  • Myth: Standard deviation can be negative.

    Actually: It is a square root, never negative.

Flashcards

  • Mean

    Σx/n.

  • Median

    Middle value.

  • Standard deviation

    √(Σ(x − x̄)²/(n − 1)).

  • IQR

    Q3 − Q1.

  • Outlier rule

    1.5 IQR beyond quartiles.

  • z-score

    (x − x̄)/s.

Show answer

n − 1. Bias correction.

Bias correction.

Show answer

How this helps save the planet

NOAA reports that the Mauna Loa CO₂ record, begun by C. David Keeling of Scripps in March 1958, is the longest record of direct measurements of CO₂ in the atmosphere. NOAA’s annual means rose from 414.21 ppm in 2020 to 427.35 ppm in 2025.

This unit’s summary statistics turn that record into a rate: the five yearly increases average 2.628 ppm per year, with a standard deviation of 0.564 ppm per year from year-to-year variation.

Unit 2 · Week 2

Probability Rules, Counting and Independence

Sample spaces and events, the axioms, complements, addition and multiplication rules, independence, permutations and combinations, and “at least one” problems.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Apply the complement, addition and multiplication rules.
  • Test independence.
  • Count with permutations and combinations.
  • Solve “at least one” problems.

Key concepts

  • Probabilities lie between 0 and 1 and the whole sample space has probability 1. P(not A) = 1 − P(A).
  • Addition rule: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B).
  • A and B are independent if P(A and B) = P(A)P(B); then knowing one tells you nothing about the other.
  • For “at least one” in n independent trials, use the complement: 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ.
  • Combinations C(n, k) = n!/(k!(n − k)!) count unordered selections; permutations count ordered ones.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 A 100-year flood has a 1% chance each year. Find the chance of at least one in a 30-year mortgage.

Show the steps
  1. P(none) = 0.99³⁰ = 0.740.
  2. P(at least one) = 1 − 0.740.

Answer: 26.0%.

Example 2.2 Find the chance of at least one 100-year flood in 100 years.

Show the steps
  1. 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰.

Answer: 63.4%, not 100%.

Example 2.3 Find the chance of at least one 50-year flood (2% a year) in 30 years.

Show the steps
  1. 1 − 0.98³⁰.

Answer: 45.5%.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. At least one 250-year flood (0.4% a year) in 30 years?

    Show answer

    1 − 0.996³⁰ = 11.3%.

  2. How many ways to choose 6 of 9 items?

    Show answer

    C(9, 6) = 84.

  3. Can two 100-year floods happen in back-to-back years?

    Show answer

    Yes: each year starts with the same 1% chance.

  4. Are yearly floods treated as independent here?

    Show answer

    Yes; the USGS notes a rare flood does not reduce the chance of another soon after.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: A 100-year flood happens once every 100 years.

    Actually: It has a 1% chance every year.

  • Myth: After a flood, the next is less likely.

    Actually: Each year is independent.

  • Myth: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) always.

    Actually: Only if A and B cannot both happen.

Flashcards

  • Complement

    1 − P(A).

  • Addition rule

    Subtract the overlap.

  • Independence

    P(A∩B) = P(A)P(B).

  • At least one

    1 − (1 − p)ⁿ.

  • Combination

    n!/(k!(n − k)!).

  • AEP

    Annual exceedance probability.

Self-check quiz

Show answer

Show answer

How this helps save the planet

The USGS explains that a “100-year flood” is a flood with a 1 percent chance of happening in any year, and that 100-year floods can happen two years in a row. Its table pairs each recurrence interval with an annual exceedance probability: 2 years with 50%, 10 years with 10%, 100 years with 1% and 500 years with 0.2%.

The USGS notes that in the 1960s the United States chose the 1-percent annual exceedance probability flood as the basis for the National Flood Insurance Program. This unit’s complement rule shows that a 30-year home in that zone has a 26% chance of seeing at least one such flood.

Unit 3 · Week 3

Conditional Probability and Bayes’ Theorem

Conditional probability, the multiplication rule, tree diagrams, the law of total probability, Bayes’ theorem, base rates, and turning a 30-year probability into a yearly rate.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute conditional probabilities from a table or tree.
  • Apply the law of total probability.
  • Use Bayes’ theorem and explain base-rate effects.
  • Convert multi-year probabilities to annual rates.

Key concepts

  • P(A | B) = P(A and B)/P(B) is the probability of A once we know B happened.
  • The law of total probability adds the branches of a tree: P(A) = Σ P(A | Bᵢ)P(Bᵢ).
  • Bayes’ theorem reverses a condition: P(B | A) = P(A | B)P(B)/P(A).
  • Rare events give many false alarms: even a good test has a low positive predictive value when the base rate is small.
  • If events arrive at a constant rate λ, the chance of at least one in t years is 1 − e^(−λt), so λ = −ln(1 − P)/t.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 A sensor flags a fault 95% of the time when one exists and falsely 5% of the time otherwise; 2% of units have the fault (sample values). Find P(fault | flag).

Show the steps
  1. P(flag) = 0.95 × 0.02 + 0.05 × 0.98 = 0.068.
  2. Bayes: 0.019/0.068.

Answer: 27.9%: most flags are false alarms.

Example 3.2 USGS gives a 72% chance of at least one M ≥ 6.7 earthquake in the San Francisco Bay region in 30 years. Find the yearly rate, assuming a constant rate.

Show the steps
  1. λ = −ln(0.28)/30.

Answer: λ = 0.0424 per year.

Example 3.3 Find the chance of at least one such earthquake in a single year.

Show the steps
  1. 1 − e^(−0.0424).

Answer: 4.15% per year.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Chance of one in the next 10 years at that rate?

    Show answer

    1 − e^(−0.424) = 34.6%.

  2. Yearly rate for the Los Angeles region’s 60% in 30 years?

    Show answer

    −ln(0.4)/30 = 0.0305 per year.

  3. Why is P(fault | flag) so low?

    Show answer

    The base rate (2%) is small.

  4. Is P(A | B) equal to P(B | A)?

    Show answer

    Generally no.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: P(A | B) = P(B | A).

    Actually: Bayes’ theorem relates them through base rates.

  • Myth: A 95% accurate test is right 95% of the time it fires.

    Actually: Not when the condition is rare.

  • Myth: A 30-year probability divides evenly by 30.

    Actually: Use the rate formula.

Flashcards

  • Conditional

    P(A∩B)/P(B).

  • Total probability

    Sum over branches.

  • Bayes

    P(A|B)P(B)/P(A).

  • Base rate

    Prior frequency.

  • Rate

    λ = −ln(1 − P)/t.

  • Tree diagram

    Branches multiply, paths add.

Self-check quiz

1. Bayes reverses…

Show answer

a condition. P(B|A) from P(A|B).

P(B|A) from P(A|B).

2. Low base rates cause…

Show answer

false alarms. Few true positives.

Few true positives.

3. P(A|B) is…

Show answer

4. Bay Area 30-year M ≥ 6.7 chance…

Show answer

How this helps save the planet

The USGS, using the Uniform California Earthquake Rupture Forecast 3, gives 30-year probabilities of 72% for a magnitude 6.7 earthquake in the San Francisco Bay region and 60% in the Los Angeles region. It also notes that around the world, the chance an earthquake is followed by a larger one nearby within a week is about 5%.

Turning those 30-year numbers into a yearly rate of about 4% is how engineers and emergency planners compare earthquake risk with other hazards.

Unit 4 · Weeks 4–5

Discrete Random Variables: Binomial and Poisson

Random variables, probability mass functions, expected value and variance, the Bernoulli and binomial distributions, the Poisson distribution for rare events, and the geometric distribution.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute expected value and variance.
  • Apply the binomial distribution.
  • Apply the Poisson distribution to rare events.
  • Choose the right discrete model.

Key concepts

  • A random variable assigns a number to each outcome. Its expected value E[X] = Σx p(x) is the long-run average.
  • Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²]; the standard deviation is its square root.
  • Binomial: the number of successes in n independent trials with probability p. P(X = k) = C(n, k)pᵏ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ, mean np, variance np(1 − p).
  • Poisson: counts of rare events at rate λ per interval. P(X = k) = e^(−λ)λᵏ/k!, mean and variance both λ.
  • Geometric: the number of trials until the first success, mean 1/p, which is where “recurrence interval” comes from.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 NOAA sizes its hurricane forecast cone so two-thirds of historical track errors fall inside. Over 9 independent forecasts, find the expected number inside and P(all 9 inside).

Show the steps
  1. E = 9 × 2/3.
  2. P = (2/3)⁹.

Answer: E = 6; P(all 9) = 2.60%.

Example 4.2 Find P(exactly 6 of 9 inside).

Show the steps
  1. C(9, 6)(2/3)⁶(1/3)³ = 84 × 0.0878 × 0.0370.

Answer: 27.3%.

Example 4.3 Model 100-year floods over 100 years as Poisson with λ = 1. Find P(none) and P(2 or more).

Show the steps
  1. P(0) = e⁻¹.
  2. P(≥2) = 1 − 2e⁻¹.

Answer: P(0) = 36.8%; P(≥2) = 26.4%.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Standard deviation of the number inside the cone, n = 9?

    Show answer

    √(9 × 2/3 × 1/3) = 1.414.

  2. Mean wait for a 1%-per-year event (geometric)?

    Show answer

    1/0.01 = 100 years.

  3. Poisson P(at least one) with λ = 1?

    Show answer

    1 − e⁻¹ = 63.2%, close to the binomial 63.4%.

  4. When does Poisson approximate binomial well?

    Show answer

    Large n, small p.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Expected value is the most likely value.

    Actually: It is the long-run average.

  • Myth: Cone = where the storm’s impacts stay.

    Actually: It shows only the probable track of the center.

  • Myth: Poisson needs a fixed number of trials.

    Actually: It counts events in an interval.

Flashcards

    A number per outcome.

  • E[X]

    Long-run average.

  • Binomial

    Successes in n trials.

  • Poisson

    Rare-event counts.

  • Geometric

    Trials to first success.

  • Variance

    E[(X − μ)²].

How this helps save the planet

NOAA’s National Hurricane Center builds its track forecast cone from circles sized so that two-thirds of historical official forecast errors over a 5-year sample fall inside. For 2026 in the Atlantic, based on 2021–2025 errors, the circle radius grows from 25 nautical miles at 12 hours to 95 at 72 hours and 200 at 120 hours.

So the center of a storm will be outside the cone about one time in three, a binomial fact worth knowing before deciding the cone means “safe” or “not safe.”

Unit 5 · Week 6

Continuous Distributions: Normal, Exponential and Extremes

Probability density and cumulative distribution functions, the uniform, normal and exponential distributions, standardizing with z, the memoryless property, and return periods for extremes.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use a PDF and CDF.
  • Find normal probabilities with z-scores.
  • Apply the exponential distribution to waiting times.
  • Relate return period and annual exceedance probability.

Key concepts

  • For a continuous variable, probabilities are areas under the density f(x); the CDF F(x) = P(X ≤ x).
  • The normal distribution is bell-shaped with mean μ and standard deviation σ. About 68%, 95% and 99.7% of values fall within 1, 2 and 3σ.
  • Standardize with z = (x − μ)/σ and read Φ(z) from a table or NORM.S.DIST.
  • Waiting times between events at constant rate λ are exponential: P(T > t) = e^(−λt), mean 1/λ. The process has no memory: past waiting does not change future odds.
  • Return period T and annual exceedance probability are reciprocals: a 0.2 AEP event has a 5-year return period.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 Find P(Z < 1) and the share within ±1σ of a normal.

Show the steps
  1. Φ(1) = 0.8413.
  2. Φ(1) − Φ(−1).

Answer: 0.8413; 68.3% within ±1σ.

Example 5.2 With the Bay Area rate λ = 0.0424 per year from Unit 3, find the mean and median wait for an M ≥ 6.7 earthquake.

Show the steps
  1. Mean = 1/λ.
  2. Median = ln 2/λ.

Answer: Mean 23.6 years; median 16.3 years.

Example 5.3 What return period matches a 0.2 annual exceedance probability, and a 0.002 one?

Show the steps
  1. T = 1/AEP.

Answer: 5 years and 500 years, matching the USGS table.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. P(Z < −1.5)?

    Show answer

    0.0668.

  2. Share within ±2σ?

    Show answer

    95.4%.

  3. Φ(1.96)?

    Show answer

    0.975.

  4. A quake has not happened for 20 years. Does the exponential model raise the odds next year?

    Show answer

    No: it is memoryless (real faults may differ).

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: P(X = x) > 0 for continuous X.

    Actually: Single points have probability 0.

  • Myth: All data are normal.

    Actually: Check with a plot; extremes are often skewed.

  • Myth: Memoryless means events are scheduled.

    Actually: It means the opposite.

Flashcards

    Density; area = probability.

  • CDF

    P(X ≤ x).

  • Normal

    Bell curve, μ and σ.

  • z

    (x − μ)/σ.

  • Exponential

    Memoryless waiting time.

  • Return period

    1/AEP.

Unit 6 · Week 7

Sampling, Simulation and the Central Limit Theorem

Populations and samples, bias and random sampling, the sampling distribution of the mean, standard error, the central limit theorem, the law of large numbers, and Monte Carlo simulation.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Distinguish population and sample quantities.
  • Compute a standard error.
  • State and apply the central limit theorem.
  • Estimate a probability by Monte Carlo simulation.

Key concepts

  • A statistic computed from a sample varies from sample to sample; its distribution is the sampling distribution.
  • The standard error of the mean is σ/√n: four times the data halves it.
  • Central limit theorem: for large n, the sample mean is approximately normal whatever the shape of the data.
  • Law of large numbers: sample averages and frequencies settle toward their true values as n grows.
  • Monte Carlo simulation repeats a random experiment many times on a computer; its error also falls like 1/√n.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 6.1 Measurements have σ = 10 (sample value). Find the standard error of the mean of 25.

Show the steps
  1. 10/√25.

Answer: SE = 2.

Example 6.2 A fair die has mean 3.5 and σ = 1.708. Find the standard error of the average of 25 rolls.

Show the steps
  1. 1.708/5.

Answer: SE = 0.342.

Example 6.3 How many Monte Carlo runs estimate a probability near 0.5 to ±0.05 (95%)?

Show the steps
  1. n = 1.96² × 0.25/0.05².

Answer: About 384 runs.

  • Myth: n = 30 always suffices.

    Actually: Skewed data may need more.

    Actually: It has its own sampling error.

Unit 7 · Weeks 8–9

Confidence Intervals

Point and interval estimates, z and t intervals for a mean, intervals for a proportion, margin of error and sample size, and what a confidence level does and does not mean.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Build a t interval for a mean.
  • Build an interval for a proportion.
  • Choose a sample size for a target margin.
  • Interpret a confidence level correctly.

Key concepts

  • A confidence interval is estimate ± critical value × standard error.
  • With σ unknown and small n, use Student’s t with n − 1 degrees of freedom; t has fatter tails than z.
  • For a proportion, p̂ ± z√(p̂(1 − p̂)/n).
  • A 95% level means 95% of intervals built this way would contain the true value; it is not a 95% chance for one computed interval.
  • Halving the margin needs four times the sample size.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Answer: 2.628 ± 0.700: from 1.93 to 3.33 ppm/yr.

Show the steps

Answer: 0.710 ± 0.160: from 0.55 to 0.87.

Example 7.3 How large a sample gives a ±0.05 margin for a proportion near 0.5?

Show the steps

Answer: About 385 (384.2, rounded up).

Unit 8 · Week 10

Hypothesis Tests and p-Values

Null and alternative hypotheses, test statistics, p-values, one- and two-sided tests, t tests and proportion tests, Type I and Type II errors, power, and statistical versus practical significance.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Set up null and alternative hypotheses.
  • Run a one-sample t test and a proportion test.
  • Interpret a p-value.
  • Explain Type I and Type II errors and power.

Key concepts

  • A test asks whether data are surprising if the null hypothesis H₀ were true.
  • The p-value is the probability, under H₀, of a result at least as extreme as the one seen. Small p is evidence against H₀; it is not the probability H₀ is true.
  • A Type I error rejects a true H₀ (rate α); a Type II error misses a false H₀ (rate β). Power = 1 − β.
  • More data raise power; a tiny, unimportant effect can be “significant” with enough data.
  • Decide α and the test before looking at the data.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 8.1 Test H₀: μ = 2 ppm/yr against μ > 2 for the five CO₂ increases (x̄ = 2.628, s = 0.564).

Show the steps
  1. t = (2.628 − 2)/(0.564/√5).

Answer: t = 2.49 with 4 df, above the one-sided 5% cutoff of 2.132: reject H₀.

Example 8.2 If each Atlantic season had a 1/3 chance of being above normal, how many of 31 would we expect?

Show the steps
  1. 31/3.

Answer: About 10.3; NOAA reports 22.

Example 8.3 Find the exact binomial P(22 or more of 31) under p = 1/3.

Show the steps
  1. Sum C(31, k)(1/3)ᵏ(2/3)³¹⁻ᵏ for k ≥ 22.

Answer: p ≈ 2.06 × 10⁻⁵: strong evidence the high-activity era is real.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

    Show answer

  2. Is p = 0.03 the chance H₀ is true?

    Show answer

  3. Type I error in a flood-warning test?

    Show answer

    A false alarm.

  4. How do you raise power?

    Show answer

    More data, larger effect, or larger α.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: p > 0.05 proves H₀.

    Actually: It only fails to reject.

  • Myth: Significant means important.

    Actually: Check the effect size.

  • Myth: Test many things, report the winner.

    Actually: That inflates false positives.

    Null hypothesis.

  • p-value

    Surprise under H₀.

  • α

    Type I rate.

  • β

    Type II rate.

  • Power

    1 − β.

  • Effect size

    How big, not just whether.

Correlation and Linear Regression

Scatter plots, correlation r, least-squares regression, slope interpretation, R², residuals, prediction versus extrapolation, and correlation versus causation.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute and interpret r and R².
  • Fit and interpret a least-squares line.
  • Use residual plots to check a model.
  • Separate correlation from causation.

Key concepts

  • Correlation r measures linear association from −1 to 1; it has no units.
  • The least-squares line ŷ = b₀ + b₁x has slope b₁ = Sxy/Sxx and passes through (x̄, ȳ).
  • R² = r² is the share of variation in y explained by the line.
  • Residual plots reveal curvature or changing spread that r hides.
  • Correlation is not causation; physical mechanisms and designed studies are needed for that. Extrapolation beyond the data is risky.

Unit 10 · Weeks 13–15

Reliability and Risk

Reliability functions, failure rates and MTBF, series and parallel systems, redundancy, the exponential and Weibull life models, and combining probability with consequences into risk.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute reliability of series and parallel systems.
  • Use the exponential life model with MTBF.
  • Explain the bathtub curve and Weibull shapes.
  • Combine likelihood and consequence into risk.

Key concepts

  • Reliability R(t) is the probability a part survives to time t. With constant failure rate λ, R(t) = e^(−λt) and MTBF = 1/λ.
  • Series systems need every part: R = R₁R₂…Rₙ, always worse than the weakest part.
  • Parallel (redundant) systems need only one: R = 1 − (1 − R₁)(1 − R₂)….
  • The Weibull model covers early failures (shape < 1), random failures (shape = 1, exponential) and wear-out (shape > 1), the three parts of the bathtub curve.
  • Risk combines probability and consequence; redundancy and warnings reduce it, but only if failures are independent.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 10.1 Three parts in series each have R = 0.99 (sample values). Find the system reliability.

Show the steps
  1. 0.99³.

Answer: R = 0.9703.

Example 10.2 Two redundant pumps each have R = 0.9. Find the system reliability.

Show the steps
  1. 1 − 0.1².

Answer: R = 0.99.

Example 10.3 A sensor has MTBF 50,000 h. Find its reliability over a 1000-hour mission.

Show the steps
  1. e^(−1000/50,000).

Answer: R = 0.980.

    Show answer

    e^(−0.0876) = 0.916.

  4. Two 0.95 units in parallel?

    Show answer

    0.9975.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Redundancy always helps.

    Actually: Not against common-cause failures.

  • Myth: MTBF is how long every unit lasts.

    Actually: It is an average; many fail sooner.

  • Myth: A series system is as reliable as its best part.

    Actually: It is worse than its weakest.

    1/λ.

    Bathtub shapes.

    Probability × consequence.

    1. 0.009/(0.009 + 0.099).

    Answer: 8.33%.

  4. Find P(X = 0) for a binomial with n = 5, p = 0.2.

    Show full solution
    1. 0.8⁵.

    Answer: 0.328.

  5. Find P(X = 0) for a Poisson with λ = 2.

    Show full solution
    1. e⁻².

    Answer: 0.135.

Final practice set (all units)

  1. Find P(Z > 1.96).

    Show full solution
    1. 1 − 0.975.

    Answer: 0.025.

  2. Find the standard error for σ = 6, n = 36.

    Show full solution
    1. 6/6.

    Answer: 1.

  3. Build a 95% interval for x̄ = 50, s = 4, n = 16 (t = 2.131).

    Show full solution
    1. 2.131 × 4/4.

    Answer: 50 ± 2.13.

  4. Test H₀: μ = 50 with x̄ = 52, s = 4, n = 16.

    Show full solution
    1. t = 2/(4/4).

    Answer: t = 2.0, just below 2.131: do not reject at 5% (two-sided).

  5. Find the reliability of four 0.95 parts in series.

    Show full solution
    1. 0.95⁴.

    Answer: 0.815.

  6. A part has MTBF 2000 h. Find P(it survives 500 h).

    Show full solution
    1. e^(−0.25).

    Answer: 0.779.

Glossary

Annual exceedance probability
Chance an event is equalled or exceeded in a year.
Bayes’ theorem
Reverses a conditional probability.
Binomial distribution
Successes in n independent trials.
Central limit theorem
Sample means become normal.
Conditional probability
Probability given that another event occurred.
Confidence interval
Range from a procedure that captures the truth at a set rate.
Correlation
Strength of linear association.
Expected value
Long-run average.
Exponential distribution
Memoryless waiting time.
Independence
One event does not change another’s probability.
MTBF
Mean time between failures.
Median
Middle value.
Monte Carlo
Estimating by repeated random simulation.
Normal distribution
Bell-shaped distribution.
Poisson distribution
Counts of rare events.
Recurrence interval
Average years between events of a given size.
Reliability
Probability of surviving to a given time.
Standard error
Standard deviation of a statistic.
Type I error
Rejecting a true null hypothesis.
p-value
Chance of data this extreme if H₀ holds.
