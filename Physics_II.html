About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows the second semester of calculus-based college physics (electricity and magnetism, with a first look at optics). It is modeled on the topics and depth of MIT OpenCourseWare 8.02, Caltech’s Ph1b and Ph1c, Georgia Tech PHYS 2212 and the free OpenStax University Physics Volume 2, which we link throughout. None of those authors or schools endorses this page.

What we add: every unit ties to real clean-energy or Earth-observation work with a cited public source (generators, solar panels, electric cars, the power grid, Earth’s magnetic field, space weather, satellite sensors), every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab has a free PhET version plus a safe low-voltage home version.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Almost all clean electricity is electromagnetism at work. Wind and hydro turbines spin generators that run on Faraday’s law, solar panels push charge with built-in electric fields, electric cars turn current into motion with magnetic forces, and the grid uses transformers and high voltage to move power with small losses.

Electromagnetic waves are also how we watch the planet: satellites such as Landsat 9 and GOES-19 read visible, infrared and microwave light to map crops, forests, water and storms. And Earth’s own magnetic field shields us, while space weather from the Sun can push currents into power lines. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Physics I: forces, energy, momentum, circular motion and oscillations.
  • Calculus I and II: derivatives, integrals, and exponential growth and decay.
  • Vectors: components, dot and cross products (taught briefly where needed).
  • Calculus III helps with Gauss’s and Ampère’s laws in integral form but is not required.

How to study

  • Draw the field or the circuit first. Most E&M mistakes are sign or direction mistakes.
  • Solve with symbols first, then put in numbers in SI units, and check the units of the answer.
  • Use symmetry: before applying Gauss’s or Ampère’s law, ask which way the field must point and on what it can depend.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer.
  • Predict, then test it in the PhET simulation. A wrong prediction you fix sticks better than a right guess.
  • Retake the quizzes a few days later. Spaced practice beats one long session.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer or tablet for the free PhET simulations (they run in a browser).
  • A digital multimeter and a few AA batteries with a battery holder (low voltage only).
  • A small set of resistors (100 Ω to 10 kΩ), a 100 µF or larger electrolytic capacitor rated above the battery voltage, jumper wires or a breadboard.
  • A smartphone with a magnetometer app (most phones have the sensor), a small compass, a few fridge magnets.
  • A clear glass or plastic box of water, a laser pointer of class 2 or lower or a narrow flashlight beam, a protractor.
  • Spreadsheet software for graphs and fits.

Unit 1 · Weeks 1–2

Charge, Coulomb’s Law and Electric Fields

Electric charge, conductors and insulators, Coulomb’s law, the electric field of point charges and continuous charge, field lines, and charges moving in fields.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use Coulomb’s law with vectors and superposition.
  • Find the electric field of point charges and simple charge distributions.
  • Sketch field lines and read them.
  • Find the force and acceleration of a charge in a field.

Key concepts

  • Charge comes in two signs, is conserved, and is quantized in units of e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.
  • Coulomb’s law: F = k|q₁q₂|/r², along the line between the charges; like charges repel.
  • The electric field E = F/q is the force per unit charge. A point charge makes E = kq/r², pointing away from positive charge.
  • Fields add as vectors (superposition). For continuous charge, add up dE = k dq/r² with an integral.
  • A charge in a field feels F = qE, so an electron accelerates opposite to E.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 Charges of +3.0 µC and −5.0 µC sit 0.20 m apart. Find the force between them.

Show the steps
  1. F = k|q₁q₂|/r² = 8.988 × 10⁹ × (3.0 × 10⁻⁶)(5.0 × 10⁻⁶)/0.20².
  2. Opposite signs attract.

Answer: 3.37 N, attractive.

Example 1.2 Find the field 0.30 m from a 2.0 nC point charge.

Show the steps
  1. E = kq/r² = 8.988 × 10⁹ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁹/0.09.

Answer: ≈ 200 N/C, pointing away from the charge.

Example 1.3 Charges of +4.0 nC and −4.0 nC are 0.10 m apart. Find the field at the midpoint.

Show the steps
  1. Each charge is 0.05 m away and gives E = kq/r² = 14,380 N/C.
  2. Both fields point toward the negative charge, so they add.

Answer: 28,760 N/C (2.88 × 10⁴ N/C) toward the negative charge.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Find the electric force between the proton and electron in hydrogen (r = 5.29 × 10⁻¹¹ m).

    Show answer

    F = ke²/r² ≈ 8.24 × 10⁻⁸ N.

  2. How many times stronger is the electric repulsion of two protons than their gravitational attraction?

    Show answer

    ke²/(Gmₚ²) ≈ 1.23 × 10³⁶, independent of distance.

  3. An electron sits in a 200 N/C field. Find the force and acceleration.

    Show answer

    F = eE = 3.20 × 10⁻¹⁷ N; a = F/mₑ ≈ 3.52 × 10¹³ m/s², opposite to E.

  4. Where is the field zero between +q and +4q a distance d apart?

    Show answer

    kq/x² = 4kq/(d − x)² gives x = d/3 from +q.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: A bigger charge feels a bigger force from a smaller one.

    Actually: Newton’s third law: the forces are equal and opposite.

  • Myth: Field lines are paths charges follow.

    Actually: They show force direction; a moving charge can leave a curved line.

  • Myth: The field is zero wherever there is no charge.

    Actually: Empty space can hold a strong field; only the charge density is zero there.

Flashcards

  • Elementary charge

    e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

  • Coulomb’s law

    F = k|q₁q₂|/r².

  • Electric field

    E = F/q.

  • Point-charge field

    E = kq/r².

  • Superposition

    Fields from several charges add as vectors.

  • Field lines

    Start on + charge, end on − charge; denser means stronger.

Show answer

×¼. Inverse square.

Inverse square.

2. The field of a positive point charge points…

Show answer

away from it. Direction of force on a positive test charge.

Direction of force on a positive test charge.

3. An electron in a field pointing east accelerates…

Show answer

F = qE with q < 0.

4. The SI unit of electric field is…

Show answer

How this helps save the planet

A solar cell works because sunlight frees charges that a built-in electric field then pushes apart. The U.S. Department of Energy explains that sunlight absorbed in a PV cell creates electrical charges that move in response to an internal electric field, so electricity flows. Every solar panel is a sheet of carefully engineered electric fields.

Unit 2 · Week 3

Gauss’s Law

Electric flux, Gauss’s law, and using symmetry to find fields of spheres, lines and sheets; conductors in electrostatic equilibrium.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute electric flux through a surface.
  • State Gauss’s law and choose a Gaussian surface that matches the symmetry.
  • Find fields of spherical, cylindrical and planar charge.
  • Explain why the field inside a conductor is zero and charge sits on its surface.

Key concepts

  • Flux Φ = ∮E·dA counts field lines through a surface.
  • Gauss’s law: the net flux out of any closed surface equals the enclosed charge divided by ε₀.
  • Gauss’s law is always true but only useful when symmetry makes E constant on the surface: spheres, long lines, large sheets.
  • Outside a uniform sphere of charge, the field is the same as a point charge at the center.
  • In a conductor in equilibrium E = 0 inside, excess charge sits on the surface, and E just outside is perpendicular to the surface.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 A closed surface surrounds a 1.0 nC charge. Find the net flux.

Show the steps
  1. Φ = Q/ε₀ = 1.0 × 10⁻⁹/8.854 × 10⁻¹²; the shape does not matter.

Answer: ≈ 113 N·m²/C.

Example 2.2 A long wire carries 2.0 nC per meter. Find E at 0.10 m.

Show the steps
  1. Use a cylinder: E(2πrL) = λL/ε₀, so E = λ/(2πε₀r) = 2kλ/r.
  2. E = 2 × 8.988 × 10⁹ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁹/0.10.

Answer: ≈ 360 N/C, pointing radially outward.

Example 2.3 A large sheet holds σ = 5.0 µC/m². Find E near it.

Show the steps
  1. A pillbox through the sheet: 2EA = σA/ε₀.

Answer: E = σ/(2ε₀) ≈ 2.82 × 10⁵ N/C, the same at every distance near the sheet.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. A 1.0 µC charge sits on a 0.10 m metal sphere. Find E at 0.50 m from the center and at 0.05 m.

    Show answer

    Outside: kQ/r² = 3.60 × 10⁴ N/C. Inside the conductor: 0.

  2. Why does a car protect you from lightning?

    Show answer

    Charge stays on the outer metal surface, so the field inside is small (a Faraday cage).

  3. A cube surrounds a dipole. What is the net flux?

    Show answer

    Zero: the enclosed charge is zero.

  4. How does E from a line of charge fall with distance?

    Show answer

    As 1/r, slower than a point charge’s 1/r².

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Zero flux means zero field everywhere on the surface.

    Actually: Only the total is zero; field can go in on one side and out on another.

  • Myth: Gauss’s law only works for symmetric charges.

    Actually: It always holds; symmetry is what makes it easy to use.

  • Myth: Charges outside the surface change the flux.

    Actually: They change E on the surface but add zero net flux.

Flashcards

  • Electric flux

    Φ = ∮E·dA.

  • Gauss’s law

    Φ = Q_enc/ε₀.

  • Line of charge

    E = λ/(2πε₀r).

  • Sheet of charge

    E = σ/(2ε₀).

  • Conductor

    E = 0 inside; charge on the surface.

  • Faraday cage

    A closed conductor shields its inside.

Self-check quiz

1. Net flux through a closed surface depends on…

Show answer

the enclosed charge. Q_enc/ε₀.

Q_enc/ε₀.

2. Inside a conductor in equilibrium, E is…

Show answer

zero. Free charges rearrange until E = 0.

Free charges rearrange until E = 0.

3. E near a large charged sheet varies with distance as…

Show answer

constant. σ/(2ε₀).

σ/(2ε₀).

4. The best Gaussian surface for a long wire is a…

Show answer

coaxial cylinder. Matches the symmetry.

Matches the symmetry.

How this helps save the planet

Gauss’s law has a magnetic twin: the net magnetic flux through any closed surface is zero, because there are no magnetic monopoles. Earth’s field is a good example. NOAA’s National Centers for Environmental Information say more than 90% of the field measured at the surface is generated in Earth’s outer core. Every field line that leaves the planet comes back to it.

Unit 3 · Weeks 4–5

Electric Potential and Capacitance

Potential energy and potential, equipotentials, finding E from V, capacitors in series and parallel, stored energy, and dielectrics.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute potential and potential energy of point charges.
  • Relate E and V: E = −dV/dx.
  • Find capacitance and combine capacitors.
  • Compute stored energy and the effect of a dielectric.

Key concepts

  • Potential V is potential energy per charge (volts = joules per coulomb). Only differences matter.
  • A point charge gives V = kq/r; potentials add as numbers, not vectors.
  • The field points downhill in potential: E = −dV/dx. Equipotentials are perpendicular to field lines.
  • A capacitor stores charge Q = CV. A parallel-plate capacitor has C = ε₀A/d.
  • Stored energy U = ½CV². A dielectric with constant κ multiplies C by κ.
  • Parallel capacitors add; in series, reciprocals add.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 Find the potential 0.30 m from a 2.0 nC charge.

Show the steps
  1. V = kq/r = 8.988 × 10⁹ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁹/0.30.

Answer: ≈ 59.9 V.

Example 3.2 An electron starts from rest and moves through a 100 V potential difference. Find its speed.

Show the steps
  1. eΔV = ½mv².
  2. v = √(2 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 100/9.109 × 10⁻³¹).

Answer: ≈ 5.93 × 10⁶ m/s.

Example 3.3 Plates of 0.010 m² sit 1.0 mm apart and connect to 12 V. Find C, Q and U.

Show the steps
  1. C = ε₀A/d = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² × 0.010/0.001 = 88.5 pF.
  2. Q = CV = 1.06 nC.
  3. U = ½CV² = 6.37 nJ.

Answer: 88.5 pF, 1.06 nC, 6.37 nJ.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Combine 2 µF and 3 µF in series and in parallel.

    Show answer

    Series 1.2 µF; parallel 5 µF.

  2. Fill the 88.5 pF capacitor with a dielectric of κ = 3.4.

    Show answer

    C = 301 pF.

  3. A large supercapacitor of 3,000 F is charged to 2.7 V (sample values). How much energy does it hold?

    Show answer

    U = ½CV² ≈ 10.9 kJ, a tiny fraction of an EV battery but delivered in seconds.

  4. V = 4x² volts. Find E at x = 2 m.

    Show answer

    E = −dV/dx = −16 V/m (pointing toward −x).

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Potential and potential energy are the same thing.

    Actually: U = qV; V is per unit charge.

  • Myth: High voltage alone is what makes a shock dangerous.

    Actually: Current through the body matters; voltage drives it through the body’s resistance.

  • Myth: Where V = 0, E = 0.

    Actually: Midway between +q and −q, V = 0 but E is large.

Flashcards

  • Electric potential

    V = U/q, in volts.

  • Point-charge potential

    V = kq/r.

  • Field from potential

    E = −dV/dx.

  • Capacitance

    C = Q/V; farads.

  • Parallel plates

    C = κε₀A/d.

  • Capacitor energy

    U = ½CV².

  • Electron volt

    1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

Self-check quiz

1. 1 volt equals…

Show answer

1 J/C. Energy per charge.

Energy per charge.

2. Halving the plate gap of a capacitor makes C…

Show answer

double. C = ε₀A/d.

C = ε₀A/d.

3. Doubling the voltage on a capacitor multiplies stored energy by…

Show answer

4. U ∝ V².

U ∝ V².

4. Equipotential lines meet field lines at…

Show answer

No work along an equipotential.

How this helps save the planet

Electric cars store energy in batteries, not capacitors, because a capacitor holds far less energy per kilogram. The supercapacitor in the practice set holds about 10.9 kJ, about 3.04 watt-hours. Capacitors earn their place where power must flow in or out in seconds, such as smoothing the output of power electronics in EVs, solar inverters and wind turbines.

Unit 4 · Weeks 6–7

Current, Resistance and DC Circuits

Current and drift speed, resistivity and Ohm’s law, power, EMF and internal resistance, series and parallel resistors, Kirchhoff’s rules, and RC circuits.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Relate current to drift speed and resistance to resistivity.
  • Use Ohm’s law and P = IV.
  • Reduce series-parallel networks and apply Kirchhoff’s rules.
  • Analyze charging and discharging RC circuits.

Key concepts

  • Current I = dQ/dt; in a wire I = nqv_dA, and the drift speed is tiny even though signals travel near light speed.
  • Resistance R = ρL/A. Ohm’s law V = IR holds for ohmic materials.
  • Power P = IV = I²R = V²/R.
  • Series resistors add; in parallel, reciprocals add.
  • Kirchhoff: currents into a junction sum to zero; voltage changes around any loop sum to zero.
  • An RC circuit charges as V = V₀(1 − e^(−t/RC)); the time constant is τ = RC.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 Find the resistance of 100 m of copper wire 1.0 mm in diameter (ρ = 1.68 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m).

Show the steps
  1. A = π(0.5 × 10⁻³)² = 7.85 × 10⁻⁷ m².
  2. R = ρL/A = 1.68 × 10⁻⁸ × 100/7.85 × 10⁻⁷.

Answer: ≈ 2.14 Ω.

Example 4.2 Two batteries (12 V and 6 V) and three resistors: loop 1 has 12 V, R₁ = 2 Ω and the shared R₃ = 6 Ω; loop 2 has 6 V, R₂ = 3 Ω and R₃. Find the currents.

Show the steps
  1. Junction: I₃ = I₁ + I₂.
  2. Loop 1: 12 = 2I₁ + 6I₃. Loop 2: 6 = 3I₂ + 6I₃.
  3. Solve: I₁ = 2 A, I₂ = −0.667 A, I₃ = 1.33 A.

Answer: I₁ = 2 A; I₂ = −0.667 A (the minus sign means the 12 V battery is pushing current backward through the 6 V one, charging it); I₃ = 1.33 A.

Example 4.3 A 100 µF capacitor charges from a 9 V battery through 10 kΩ. Find τ and the voltage after 1 s and 3 s.

Show the steps
  1. τ = RC = 10⁴ × 10⁻⁴ = 1.0 s.
  2. V(1) = 9(1 − e^(−1)); V(3) = 9(1 − e^(−3)).

Answer: 1.0 s; 5.69 V; 8.55 V.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. A 60 W bulb runs on 120 V. Find its current and resistance.

    Show answer

    I = 0.5 A; R = 240 Ω.

  2. Find the equivalent of 6 Ω and 3 Ω in parallel, then the current from 12 V through that pair in series with 4 Ω.

    Show answer

    2 Ω; I = 12/6 = 2 A.

  3. Find the drift speed for 1 A in the 1.0 mm copper wire (n = 8.5 × 10²⁸ m⁻³).

    Show answer

    v_d = I/(neA) ≈ 9.4 × 10⁻⁵ m/s, about a third of a meter per hour.

  4. A solar panel has 60 cells in series, each giving 0.6 V (sample values). What is the panel voltage? How do you double the current instead?

    Show answer

    36 V; put two panels in parallel.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Current is used up in a resistor.

    Actually: Charge is conserved; energy is converted, not current.

  • Myth: Electrons race through wires at near light speed.

    Actually: The signal does; the electrons drift at fractions of a millimeter per second.

  • Myth: A battery supplies constant current.

    Actually: It supplies roughly constant EMF; the current depends on the circuit.

Flashcards

  • Current

    I = dQ/dt; amperes.

  • Ohm’s law

    V = IR.

  • Resistivity

    R = ρL/A.

  • Power

    P = IV = I²R = V²/R.

  • Junction rule

    ΣI = 0.

  • Loop rule

    ΣΔV = 0.

  • RC time constant

    τ = RC.

How this helps save the planet

A solar panel is a DC circuit. Cells are wired in series to raise the voltage and panels in parallel to raise the current, exactly as in the practice set. A cell pushes charge one way only, so a panel’s output is direct current; it must be converted to alternating current before it can join the grid (Unit 9). The U.S. Department of Energy explains how the cells themselves work.

Unit 5 · Week 8

Magnetic Fields and Forces

The magnetic force on moving charges and currents, circular motion of charged particles, torque on a current loop, and how electric motors work.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use F = qv × B with the right-hand rule.
  • Find the radius and frequency of a charged particle’s circular orbit.
  • Find the force on a current-carrying wire and the torque on a loop.
  • Explain how a DC motor turns current into rotation.

Key concepts

  • A magnetic field pushes on moving charge: F = qv × B, perpendicular to both v and B. It does no work, so it changes direction, not speed.
  • A charge moving across a uniform field circles with radius r = mv/(|q|B) and cyclotron frequency f = |q|B/(2πm), independent of speed.
  • A wire carrying current I in field B feels F = IL × B.
  • A current loop has magnetic moment μ = NIA and feels torque τ = μB sin θ. That torque is what spins an electric motor.
  • Crossed E and B fields pass only particles with v = E/B (a velocity selector).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 A solar-wind proton moves at 400 km/s perpendicular to a 50 µT field (sample values). Find the radius of its circle.

Show the steps

Answer: ≈ 83.5 m. Charged particles spiral tightly along field lines instead of crossing them.

Example 5.2 A 0.20 m wire carrying 200 A sits perpendicular to a 0.50 T field. Find the force.

Show the steps
  1. F = ILB = 200 × 0.20 × 0.50.

Answer: 20 N.

Example 5.3 A motor coil has 100 turns of area 0.01 m² carrying 2 A in a 0.5 T field. Find the maximum torque.

Show the steps
  1. τ = NIAB = 100 × 2 × 0.01 × 0.5.

Answer: 1.0 N·m, when the coil’s plane is parallel to the field.

    Show answer

  4. Can a static magnetic field speed up a charge?

    Show answer

  • Myth: Magnetic force points along the field.

    Actually: It is perpendicular to B and to v.

  • Myth: A charge at rest feels a magnetic force.

    Actually: Only moving charges do.

  • Myth: Bigger speed means a higher cyclotron frequency.

    Actually: The radius grows with speed, but the frequency does not.

Sources of Magnetic Fields: Biot–Savart and Ampère

How currents make magnetic fields: the Biot–Savart law, fields of wires, loops and solenoids, forces between parallel currents, and Ampère’s law.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use the Biot–Savart law for a wire and a loop.
  • Apply Ampère’s law to wires and solenoids.
  • Find the force between parallel currents.
  • Compare man-made fields with Earth’s field.

Key concepts

  • Biot–Savart: each bit of current I dl makes dB = (μ₀/4π) I dl × r̂/r². Field lines circle the current (right-hand rule: thumb along I, fingers curl with B).
  • A long straight wire: B = μ₀I/(2πr). At the center of a loop of radius R: B = μ₀I/(2R).
  • Ampère’s law: ∮B·dl = μ₀I_enc. Like Gauss’s law, it is easiest with symmetry.
  • Inside a long solenoid B = μ₀nI, uniform, where n is turns per meter.
  • Parallel currents attract; opposite currents repel, with force per length μ₀I₁I₂/(2πd).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Answer: 10 µT, smaller than Earth’s 25–65 µT surface field.

Example 6.2 A solenoid has 1,000 turns per meter and carries 2 A. Find B inside.

Show the steps
  1. B = μ₀nI = 4π × 10⁻⁷ × 1,000 × 2.

Answer: ≈ 2.51 mT, about 50 times Earth’s field.

Example 6.3 Two parallel wires 1 cm apart each carry 10 A in the same direction. Find the force per meter.

Show the steps
  1. F/L = μ₀I₁I₂/(2πd) = 4π × 10⁻⁷ × 100/(2π × 0.01).

Answer: 2.0 mN/m, attractive.

    Actually: It always holds, but it gives B directly only with symmetry.

Electromagnetic Induction: Faraday and Lenz

Magnetic flux, Faraday’s law, Lenz’s law, motional EMF, induced electric fields, eddy currents, and the generators behind wind and hydro power.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute magnetic flux and the EMF from a changing flux.
  • Use Lenz’s law to find the direction of induced current.
  • Find motional EMF for a moving rod.
  • Compute the output of an AC generator and the power of a hydro plant.

Key concepts

  • Magnetic flux Φ_B = ∫B·dA, in webers.
  • Faraday’s law: a changing flux through a coil of N turns induces EMF ε = −N dΦ_B/dt.
  • Lenz’s law (the minus sign): the induced current opposes the change in flux that caused it, which is energy conservation.
  • A rod of length L moving at v across B has motional EMF ε = BLv.
  • A coil spinning at angular speed ω in B gives ε = NBAω sin ωt: alternating current. Turning it takes mechanical work, which is how generators convert motion into electrical energy.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 7.1 A generator coil has 100 turns of area 0.04 m² spinning at 60 rev/s in a 0.5 T field. Find the peak EMF.

Show the steps
  1. ω = 2π × 60 = 377 rad/s.
  2. ε_max = NBAω = 100 × 0.5 × 0.04 × 377.

Answer: ≈ 754 V.

Example 7.2 A 200-turn coil of area 0.01 m² sees B rise from 0 to 0.3 T in 0.5 s. Find the average EMF.

Show the steps
  1. ΔΦ = BA = 0.003 Wb per turn.
  2. ε = NΔΦ/Δt = 200 × 0.003/0.5.

Answer: 1.2 V.

Example 7.3 A hydro plant passes 100 m³/s through a 50 m head at 90% efficiency (sample values). Find its power.

Show the steps
  1. P = ηρgQh = 0.9 × 1,000 × 9.81 × 100 × 50.

Answer: ≈ 44.1 MW.

    Actually: Only a changing flux induces EMF.

  • Myth: Lenz’s law opposes the field.

    Actually: It opposes the change in flux.

  • Myth: Generators make energy.

    Actually: They convert mechanical work into electrical energy.

Unit 9 · Week 12

AC Circuits, Transformers and the Power Grid

Alternating current, RMS values, reactance and impedance, phasors, RLC resonance, power factor, transformers, and why the grid transmits at high voltage.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Convert between peak and RMS values.
  • Find reactance, impedance, phase and current in a series RLC circuit.
  • Compute average power and power factor.
  • Use transformer ratios and explain high-voltage transmission.

Key concepts

  • Household AC is sinusoidal; meters read RMS values, with V_rms = V₀/√2.
  • Inductors have reactance X_L = ωL and capacitors X_C = 1/(ωC). In a series RLC circuit Z = √(R² + (X_L − X_C)²).
  • The current leads or lags the voltage by φ with tan φ = (X_L − X_C)/R. Average power is P = I_rms V_rms cos φ; cos φ is the power factor.
  • At resonance ω₀ = 1/√(LC), X_L = X_C and the current is largest.
  • An ideal transformer keeps power: V₂/V₁ = N₂/N₁ and I₂/I₁ = N₁/N₂. It works only with AC, by mutual induction.
  • Line loss is I²R. Sending the same power at 10 times the voltage cuts current 10 times and losses 100 times.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 9.1 A series circuit has R = 20 Ω, L = 0.1 H and C = 100 µF on 120 V RMS at 60 Hz. Find the impedance, current, phase and power.

Show the steps
  1. X_L = 2π × 60 × 0.1 = 37.7 Ω; X_C = 1/(2π × 60 × 10⁻⁴) = 26.5 Ω.
  2. Z = √(20² + 11.2²) = 22.9 Ω; I = 120/22.9 = 5.24 A.
  3. tan φ = 11.2/20, φ = 29.2°; P = I²R = 549 W.

Answer: 22.9 Ω, 5.24 A, current lags by 29.2° (power factor 0.873), 549 W.

Example 9.2 Send 100 MW over a line with 10 Ω resistance at 345 kV and at 34.5 kV. Compare the losses.

Show the steps
  1. 345 kV: I = 290 A, loss = I²R = 0.84 MW (0.84%).
  2. 34.5 kV: I = 2,899 A, loss = 84 MW.

Answer: 0.84 MW versus 84 MW. Ten times the voltage gives one hundredth of the loss, which is why long lines run at hundreds of kilovolts.

Example 9.3 A pole transformer steps 7,200 V down to 240 V (sample values). Find the turns ratio, and the primary current when the secondary supplies 30 A.

Show the steps
  1. N₁/N₂ = 7,200/240 = 30.
  2. I₁ = I₂/30.

Answer: 30:1; 1.0 A.

Maxwell’s Equations, Electromagnetic Waves and Optics

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Derive c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) and relate f, λ and photon energy.
  • Compute intensity, field amplitudes and radiation pressure.
  • Use Snell’s law, the critical angle, Malus’s law and the thin-lens equation.

Key concepts

  • Maxwell added the displacement current ε₀ dΦ_E/dt to Ampère’s law. With it, the four equations (Gauss for E and B, Faraday, Ampère–Maxwell) predict waves.
  • The waves travel at c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s, with E ⊥ B ⊥ direction of travel and E = cB. Light is one of these waves.

  • At a boundary, light reflects at equal angles and refracts by Snell’s law n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂. Going from a denser medium, beyond the critical angle all light reflects.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 10.2 Landsat 9’s near-infrared band runs 0.85–0.88 µm and its red band 0.64–0.67 µm (NASA). Find the frequency at each band’s center (0.865 and 0.655 µm) and the photon energy in the red.

Show the steps
  1. f = c/λ: 3.47 × 10¹⁴ Hz and 4.58 × 10¹⁴ Hz.
  2. E = hc/λ = 3.03 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 1.89 eV.

Answer: NIR 3.47 × 10¹⁴ Hz; red 4.58 × 10¹⁴ Hz; 1.89 eV.

Example 10.3 Bright sunlight of 1,000 W/m² (sample value) hits a panel. Find E₀, B₀ and the pressure on an absorbing surface.

Show the steps
  1. E₀ = √(2I/(cε₀)) = 868 V/m.
  2. B₀ = E₀/c = 2.90 µT.
  3. p = I/c = 3.34 µPa.

Answer: 868 V/m, 2.90 µT, 3.34 µPa.

Example 10.4 Light enters water (n = 1.33) at 30° from the normal. Find the refraction angle and the critical angle for light leaving water.

Show the steps
  1. sin θ₂ = sin 30°/1.33, θ₂ = 22.1°.
  2. sin θ_c = 1/1.33, θ_c = 48.8°.

Answer: 22.1°; 48.8°.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Light needs a medium.

    Actually: EM waves travel through vacuum.

  • Myth: Radio and light are different things.

    Actually: Same waves, different wavelengths.

  • Myth: Light bends away from the normal entering water.

    Actually: Toward the normal: it slows down.

How this helps save the planet

Satellites watch the planet by measuring electromagnetic waves. NASA lists Landsat 9’s bands, from the coastal/aerosol band at 0.43 µm through visible, near-infrared and shortwave infrared to thermal infrared at 10.60–12.51 µm. NASA says these observations support tropical deforestation alerts, water-quality monitoring and crop condition reports, and the thermal sensor measures land surface temperature to help manage water in drought-prone regions. Each band is a slice of the spectrum chosen because land, water and plants reflect or emit it differently.

NASA’s tour of the electromagnetic spectrum shows how each band, from radio to gamma rays, is used to study Earth and space.

Midterm practice set (Units 1–5)

  1. Charges of +2.0 µC and +8.0 µC are 0.30 m apart. Find the force between them and the point between them where E = 0.

    Show full solution

  2. A closed surface surrounds a 3.0 nC charge. Find the net flux.

    Show full solution

    Answer: ≈ 339 N·m²/C.

  3. A 10 µF capacitor is charged to 12 V. Find Q and U.

    Show full solution
    1. Q = CV; U = ½CV².

    Answer: 120 µC; 0.72 mJ.

  4. A 9 V battery drives 3 Ω in series with a parallel pair of 6 Ω and 3 Ω. Find the current and total power.

    Show full solution
    1. Parallel pair = 2 Ω; total 5 Ω.
    2. I = 9/5; P = IV.

    Answer: 1.8 A; 16.2 W.

  5. A proton moves at 1.0 × 10⁶ m/s across a 0.20 T field. Find the orbit radius.

    Answer: ≈ 5.22 cm.

Final practice set (all units)

  1. Find B 0.10 m from a long wire carrying 50 A.

    Show full solution
