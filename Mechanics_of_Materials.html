About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a one-semester sophomore mechanics of materials (strength of materials) course. It is modeled on the topics and depth of MIT 2.001 Mechanics and Materials I (on MIT OpenCourseWare), Purdue ME 323 Mechanics of Materials and Georgia Tech COE 3001 Mechanics of Deformable Bodies, and on the free OpenStax University Physics, which we link where it helps. None of those authors or schools endorses this page.

What we add: every unit ties to real clean-energy, infrastructure or space work with a cited public source (bridges, lighter cars, wind turbine towers, shafts and blades, mass timber, hydrogen tanks, NASA rocket structures), every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab has a free simulation or a cheap home version.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Mechanics of materials decides how much material a structure needs. Using just enough steel, aluminum, composite or wood, and no more, makes cars lighter and more efficient, wind turbines taller, buildings lower in carbon, and rockets able to carry more to orbit.

It is also how we keep what we have built safe: bridges, towers and tanks must carry their loads for decades without yielding, cracking from fatigue or buckling. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample values, and material properties are typical textbook values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Statics: free-body diagrams, equilibrium, centroids and area moments of inertia.
  • Calculus I and II: derivatives and integrals; Unit 9 integrates the beam equation twice.
  • Physics I helps with springs, energy and units.
  • Dynamics is not required.

How to study

  • Start every problem with statics: a free-body diagram and the internal force, torque or moment at the cut.
  • Then ask how the part deforms: stretch, twist or bend, and which formula fits that deformation.
  • Check that the formula applies: linear elastic, small deformations, thin walls or long slender members where required.
  • Solve with symbols first, then put in SI numbers (N, m, Pa), and check units: 1 MPa = 1 N/mm².
  • Compare the stress with the material’s allowable stress and state a factor of safety.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer, and retake the quizzes a few days later.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer or tablet for the free PhET simulations (they run in a browser).
  • Rubber bands, a bag for coins or washers, and a kitchen scale (to know each mass).
  • A 30 cm plastic ruler, a second ruler or tape measure, and tape or a clamp to hold it to a table edge.
  • A foam rod or pool-noodle offcut and a marker for torsion lines.
  • Dry spaghetti and small weights for the buckling lab.
  • Safety glasses, a smartphone camera and spreadsheet software for graphs and fits.

Unit 1 · Weeks 1–2

Stress: Normal, Shear and Bearing; Allowable Stress and Factor of Safety

Internal forces found by cutting a member, average normal stress in rods, average shear stress in pins and bolts (single and double shear), bearing stress, allowable stress design and the factor of safety.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Find internal forces with a free-body diagram of a cut section.
  • Compute average normal, shear and bearing stresses.
  • Tell single shear from double shear in connections.
  • Size a member from an allowable stress and a factor of safety.

Key concepts

  • Stress is internal force per unit area. Its SI unit is the pascal (1 Pa = 1 N/m²); engineers use MPa, and 1 MPa = 1 N/mm².
  • Normal stress σ = P/A acts perpendicular to a cut; tension is positive and compression negative.
  • Average shear stress τ = V/A acts in the plane of the cut. A pin in double shear has two cut planes, so each carries V = P/2.
  • Bearing stress σ_b = P/(td) is the contact pressure between a bolt and the hole in a plate of thickness t.
  • Factor of safety FS = failure load/allowable load (or failure stress/allowable stress). Design keeps working stress at or below σ_allow = σ_fail/FS.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 A steel rod of 20 mm diameter carries a tensile load of 50 kN. Find the normal stress.

Show the steps
  1. A = πd²/4 = π × 20²/4 = 314.2 mm².
  2. σ = P/A = 50,000 N/314.2 mm².

Answer: 159.2 MPa (tension).

Example 1.2 A 12 mm pin in double shear carries a 20 kN load. Find the average shear stress in the pin.

Show the steps
  1. Each of the two shear planes carries V = 10 kN.
  2. A = π × 12²/4 = 113.1 mm²; τ = 10,000/113.1.

Answer: 88.4 MPa.

Example 1.3 A round steel hanger must carry 100 kN. The steel yields at 250 MPa and the factor of safety is 2. Find the smallest diameter.

Show the steps
  1. σ_allow = 250/2 = 125 MPa.
  2. A = P/σ_allow = 100,000/125 = 800 mm².
  3. d = √(4A/π).

Answer: d = 31.9 mm (use 32 mm).

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. A 100 mm × 100 mm square post carries 200 kN in compression. Find the stress.

    Show answer

    σ = 200,000/10,000 = 20 MPa (compression).

  2. A 10 mm bolt in single shear carries 8 kN. Find the average shear stress.

    Show answer

    A = 78.54 mm²; τ = 101.9 MPa.

  3. The same 10 mm bolt passes through a 6 mm thick plate. Find the bearing stress.

    Show answer

    σ_b = 8000/(6 × 10) = 133 MPa.

  4. A link fails at 90 kN and works at 30 kN. Find the factor of safety.

    Show answer

    FS = 90/30 = 3.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Stress is the same as force.

    Actually: Stress is force per area; a thin wire and a thick bar with the same force have very different stresses.

  • Myth: A pin in double shear carries the full load on each plane.

    Actually: Each of the two planes carries half.

  • Myth: A higher factor of safety is always better.

    Actually: It also means heavier, costlier parts; designers choose FS from codes and the uncertainty in loads and materials.

How this helps save the planet

Stress limits decide which bridges trucks may cross. The ASCE Report Card for America’s Infrastructure counts 623,218 bridges in the United States, and 63,085 of them were posted for load in 2024, meaning they restrict the weight of vehicles that can safely cross, which is about 10.1% of all bridges. ASCE warns that trucks heavier than bridges were designed for threaten to overstress bridge elements and cause metal fatigue and cracking. Keeping working stress below the allowable stress, as in this unit, is how engineers rate and repair them, and preserving a bridge costs less than replacing it.

Unit 2 · Week 3

Strain and Material Behavior: Hooke’s Law, Poisson’s Ratio and the Stress–Strain Curve

Normal and shear strain, the tensile test and the stress–strain diagram, Young’s modulus, yield and ultimate strength, ductility, Poisson’s ratio, the shear modulus, strain energy and specific stiffness.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute normal and shear strain from deformations.
  • Read E, yield strength, ultimate strength and ductility from a stress–strain curve.
  • Use Hooke’s law, Poisson’s ratio and G = E/(2(1 + ν)).
  • Compare materials by stiffness and strength per unit mass.

Key concepts

  • Normal strain ε = δ/L is a stretch per unit length (dimensionless, often in μm/m). Shear strain γ is the change in a right angle, in radians.
  • In the linear-elastic range, Hooke’s law holds: σ = Eε and τ = Gγ. E is Young’s modulus, the slope of the stress–strain curve.
  • Beyond the yield strength, the material deforms plastically and does not spring back; the ultimate strength is the peak engineering stress, and ductile metals neck before they break.
  • Stretching a bar makes it thinner: lateral strain = −ν × axial strain, with Poisson’s ratio ν about 0.3 for metals. For isotropic materials G = E/(2(1 + ν)).
  • Specific stiffness E/ρ and specific strength σ/ρ compare materials per kilogram; that is why aluminum, magnesium and carbon fiber can replace steel in light structures.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 A 2 m steel bar stretches 1.2 mm when the stress is 120 MPa. Find the strain and Young’s modulus.

Show the steps
  1. ε = 1.2/2000 = 6 × 10⁻⁴.
  2. E = σ/ε = 120 MPa/0.0006.

Answer: ε = 600 μm/m; E = 200 GPa.

Example 2.2 An aluminum alloy has E = 70 GPa and ν = 0.33. Find its shear modulus.

Show the steps
  1. G = E/(2(1 + ν)) = 70/2.66.

Answer: G = 26.3 GPa.

Example 2.3 A 20 mm steel rod (ν = 0.3) has an axial strain of 0.001. How much does its diameter change?

Show the steps
  1. Lateral strain = −0.3 × 0.001 = −0.0003.
  2. Δd = −0.0003 × 20 mm.

Answer: It shrinks by 0.006 mm.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Typical values: steel has E = 200 GPa and ρ = 7850 kg/m³; aluminum has E = 70 GPa and ρ = 2700 kg/m³. Compare E/ρ.

    Show answer

    Steel 25.5 × 10⁶ m²/s², aluminum 25.9 × 10⁶ m²/s²: almost the same stiffness per kilogram.

  2. A tensile specimen with a 50 mm gauge length measures 62 mm at fracture. Find the elongation at break.

    Show answer

    (62 − 50)/50 = 24%: a ductile metal.

  3. Find the modulus of resilience of steel with σ_Y = 250 MPa and E = 200 GPa.

    Show answer

    u_r = 250²/(2 × 200,000) = 0.156 MPa = 156 kJ/m³.

  4. Sample values: a car that gets 30 mpg is made 10% lighter. Using DOE’s 6% to 8% figure, estimate its new fuel economy.

    Show answer

    30 × 1.06 = 31.8 to 30 × 1.08 = 32.4 mpg (sample car).

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Stronger means stiffer.

    Actually: Strength (yield) and stiffness (E) are different; most steels share E = 200 GPa whatever their strength.

  • Myth: Strain has units of length.

    Actually: It is length per length, dimensionless.

  • Myth: A material breaks at its yield strength.

    Actually: Yield starts permanent deformation; ductile metals carry more load up to the ultimate strength.

  • Poisson’s ratio

  • Shear modulus

    G = E/(2(1 + ν)).

  • Yield vs ultimate

    Start of plastic flow vs peak stress.

  • Specific stiffness

How this helps save the planet

Choosing materials by stiffness and strength per kilogram saves fuel. The U.S. Department of Energy says a 10% reduction in vehicle weight can result in a 6% to 8% fuel economy improvement, and that replacing cast iron and traditional steel with high-strength steel, magnesium and aluminum alloys, carbon fiber and polymer composites can cut the weight of a vehicle’s body and chassis by up to 50 percent. DOE adds that lightweight materials are especially important for electric vehicles, where they offset the weight of batteries and motors and can increase all-electric range.

Unit 3 · Week 4

Axially Loaded Members: Deformation, Indeterminate Bars and Thermal Stress

Elongation of bars δ = PL/(AE), bars with several segments, statically indeterminate bars solved with compatibility, thermal strain and thermal stress, and stress concentrations.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute the elongation of uniform and stepped bars.
  • Solve statically indeterminate axial problems with equilibrium plus compatibility.
  • Compute thermal expansion and the stress in a restrained bar.
  • Explain stress concentrations at holes and fillets.

Key concepts

  • A uniform bar under axial load stretches δ = PL/(AE). For several segments, add the δ of each: δ = Σ PᵢLᵢ/(AᵢEᵢ).
  • If equilibrium alone cannot give the forces (statically indeterminate), add a compatibility equation: how the deformations must fit together.
  • A temperature change makes a free bar grow δ_T = αΔT L with no stress. If the bar is held, the growth is pushed back and σ = −EαΔT appears.
  • Rails, pipelines, bridges and pavements need expansion gaps or joints for this reason.
  • Holes, notches and sharp corners raise local stress: σ_max = K σ_avg, where K is the stress concentration factor (often 2 to 3 for a small hole).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 A 3 m steel rod (E = 200 GPa) with A = 500 mm² carries 60 kN. Find its elongation.

Show the steps
  1. δ = PL/(AE) = 60,000 × 3000/(500 × 200,000) in N and mm.

Answer: 1.8 mm.

Example 3.2 A steel bar (E = 200 GPa, α = 12 × 10⁻⁶/°C) is fixed between two rigid walls and heated by 40 °C. Find the stress.

Show the steps
  1. Free growth would be αΔT L; the walls push it back.
  2. σ = −EαΔT = −200,000 × 12 × 10⁻⁶ × 40 MPa.

Answer: 96 MPa compression.

Example 3.3 A uniform bar fixed at both ends carries an axial load P = 30 kN at a point 1 m from end A and 2 m from end B. Find both wall reactions.

Show the steps
  1. Equilibrium: R_A + R_B = 30 kN.
  2. Compatibility: the stretch of segment A equals the shortening of segment B, so R_A × 1 = R_B × 2.

Answer: R_A = 20 kN, R_B = 10 kN.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. A 25 m steel rail warms by 30 °C with nothing holding it. How much does it grow?

    Show answer

    δ = 12 × 10⁻⁶ × 30 × 25,000 = 9 mm.

  2. A bar has an aluminum segment (E = 70 GPa, A = 400 mm², L = 1 m) and a steel segment (A = 200 mm², L = 0.5 m) in series, carrying 20 kN. Find the total stretch.

    Show answer

    0.714 + 0.250 = 0.964 mm.

  3. Find the strain in aluminum at 100 MPa (E = 70 GPa).

    Show answer

    ε = 100/70,000 = 1.43 × 10⁻³.

  4. DOE says land-based hub heights reached about 103.4 m in 2023 after growing 83% since 1998–1999. What was the earlier average?

    Show answer

    103.4/1.83 = 56.5 m.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Thermal expansion always causes stress.

    Actually: Only when the expansion is restrained.

  • Myth: An indeterminate bar can be solved with equilibrium alone.

    Actually: You need compatibility (deformation) equations too.

  • Myth: A small hole barely matters.

    Actually: It can triple the local stress, which is where fatigue cracks start.

How this helps save the planet

Taller wind turbines catch stronger wind but need taller towers. The U.S. Department of Energy reports that the hub height of utility-scale land-based turbines grew 83% since 1998–1999 to about 103.4 meters in 2023, taller than the Statue of Liberty, and that offshore hub heights are projected to reach about 150 meters by 2035. Each tower is an axially loaded column that must carry the weight above it within its allowable stress.

Unit 4 · Weeks 5–6

Torsion of Circular Shafts and Power Transmission

Shear strain and stress in twisted circular shafts, the polar moment of inertia for solid and hollow shafts, angle of twist, power and torque, shaft design, and statically indeterminate shafts.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute shear stress with τ = Tρ/J.
  • Compute the angle of twist φ = TL/(GJ).
  • Convert power and speed to torque with P = Tω.
  • Size solid and hollow shafts and explain why hollow shafts save material.

Key concepts

  • In a twisted circular shaft, plane sections stay plane and shear strain grows linearly from the center: γ = ρφ/L.
  • Shear stress is τ = Tρ/J, largest at the outer surface: τ_max = Tc/J.
  • Polar moment of inertia: J = πd⁴/32 for a solid shaft and J = π(d_o⁴ − d_i⁴)/32 for a hollow one.
  • Angle of twist φ = TL/(GJ) in radians; for several segments, add them.
  • Power P = Tω. Slow shafts carry huge torques for the same power, which is why a gearbox speeds up a wind turbine’s rotation before the generator.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 A solid 50 mm steel shaft carries a torque of 1.5 kN·m. Find the maximum shear stress.

Show the steps
  1. J = π × 0.05⁴/32 = 6.136 × 10⁻⁷ m⁴.
  2. τ_max = Tc/J = 1500 × 0.025/6.136 × 10⁻⁷.

Answer: 61.1 MPa.

Example 4.2 The same shaft is 2 m long with G = 80 GPa. Find the angle of twist.

Show the steps
  1. φ = TL/(GJ) = 1500 × 2/(80 × 10⁹ × 6.136 × 10⁻⁷).

Answer: 0.0611 rad = 3.50°.

Example 4.3 A motor delivers 50 kW at 1,500 rpm. Size a solid shaft for an allowable shear stress of 40 MPa.

Show the steps
  1. ω = 1500 × 2π/60 = 157.1 rad/s; T = P/ω = 318.3 N·m.
  2. τ = 16T/(πd³) gives d = (16T/(πτ))^(1/3).

Answer: d = 34.3 mm (use 35 mm).

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. A hollow shaft (d_o = 60 mm, d_i = 40 mm) carries 2 kN·m. Find τ_max.

    Show answer

    J = 1.021 × 10⁻⁶ m⁴; τ = 2000 × 0.03/J = 58.8 MPa.

  2. Compare that hollow shaft with a solid 60 mm shaft: what fraction of the material and of the stiffness J does it keep?

    Show answer

    Area 1 − (40/60)² = 55.6%; J 1 − (40/60)⁴ = 80.2%.

  3. A solid 30 mm shaft carries 500 N·m. Find τ_max.

    Show answer

    τ = 16T/(πd³) = 94.3 MPa.

  4. Sample values: a 3.4 MW wind turbine rotor turns at a sample 12 rpm. Find the torque on its main shaft.

    Show answer

    ω = 1.257 rad/s; T = P/ω = 2.71 MN·m (sample speed).

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Shear stress is uniform across a shaft.

    Actually: It is zero at the center and largest at the surface.

  • Myth: Torque and power are the same.

    Actually: P = Tω: the same power at low speed needs much more torque.

  • Myth: A hollow shaft is much weaker.

    Actually: Material near the center carries little stress; removing it saves weight for a small loss in J.

Shear and Moment Diagrams and Bending Stress

Internal shear and bending moment in beams, the relations dV/dx = −w and dM/dx = V, shear and moment diagrams, the flexure formula σ = My/I, the section modulus and efficient beam shapes.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Draw shear and moment diagrams for common loads.
  • Use dV/dx = −w and dM/dx = V to check diagrams.
  • Compute bending stress with σ = My/I.
  • Choose efficient cross-sections using the section modulus S = I/c.

Key concepts

  • Cut a beam and the internal shear V and moment M hold the piece in equilibrium. Slopes follow dV/dx = −w and dM/dx = V, so M is largest where V crosses zero.
  • Pure bending keeps plane sections plane; strain and stress grow linearly from the neutral axis, which passes through the centroid.
  • The flexure formula is σ = −My/I; the largest stress is σ_max = Mc/I = M/S, where S = I/c is the section modulus.
  • For a rectangle, I = bh³/12, so depth matters much more than width: a plank on edge is far stiffer and stronger than one lying flat.
  • I-beams and hollow tubes put material far from the neutral axis, giving a large I for little mass.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Show the steps
  1. By symmetry each reaction is 5 kN; V = ±5 kN.
  2. M_max = PL/4 at midspan.

Answer: 10 kN·m.

Example 5.2 The beam is a 100 mm wide × 200 mm deep rectangle. Find the maximum bending stress.

Show the steps
  1. I = 0.1 × 0.2³/12 = 6.667 × 10⁻⁵ m⁴; c = 0.1 m.
  2. σ = Mc/I = 10,000 × 0.1/6.667 × 10⁻⁵.

Show the steps

  2. M_max = wL²/8 at midspan, where V = 0.

Transverse Shear in Beams, Shear Flow and Composite Sections

Shear stress from transverse loads, the shear formula τ = VQ/(It), shear stress in rectangular and circular sections, shear flow in built-up and glued beams, fastener spacing, and beams of two materials.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute Q and the shear stress τ = VQ/(It).
  • Use τ_max = 1.5V/A for rectangles and 4V/(3A) for circles.
  • Find shear flow q = VQ/I and the spacing of nails, bolts or glue strength in built-up beams.
  • Explain how layered beams act together.

Key concepts

  • A beam with changing moment also carries shear stress on horizontal planes; that is why a stack of loose boards slides and bends much more than one glued block.
  • The shear formula τ = VQ/(It) uses Q = A′ȳ′, the first moment about the neutral axis of the area beyond the cut.
  • In a rectangle the shear stress is parabolic, zero at the top and bottom and 1.5V/A at the neutral axis.
  • Shear flow q = VQ/I is the force per unit length that a glue line or a row of fasteners must carry; fastener spacing s = F_fastener/q.
  • In most long beams bending stress governs; in short, deep beams and in wood, shear can govern.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 6.1 A 100 mm × 200 mm rectangular beam carries V = 15 kN. Find the maximum shear stress.

Show the steps
  1. A = 0.02 m².
  2. τ_max = 1.5V/A = 1.5 × 15,000/0.02.

Answer: 1.125 MPa at the neutral axis.

Example 6.2 Two 150 mm × 50 mm boards are glued flat on top of each other to make a 150 mm wide × 100 mm deep beam carrying V = 10 kN. Find the shear stress the glue must carry.

Show the steps
  1. I = 0.15 × 0.1³/12 = 1.25 × 10⁻⁵ m⁴.
  2. Q = 0.15 × 0.05 × 0.025 = 1.875 × 10⁻⁴ m³.
  3. q = VQ/I = 150 N/mm; τ = q/b = 150/150.

Answer: τ = 1.0 MPa (the same as 1.5V/A).

Example 6.3 The same two boards are nailed instead, under V = 2 kN, and each nail can carry 900 N. Find the nail spacing.

Show the steps
  1. q = VQ/I = 2000 × 1.875 × 10⁻⁴/1.25 × 10⁻⁵ = 30 N/mm.
  2. s = F/q = 900/30.

Answer: 30 mm.

Unit 7 · Week 10

Stress Transformation, Mohr’s Circle, Failure Theories and Fatigue

Plane stress on rotated planes, principal stresses and maximum shear, Mohr’s circle, the Tresca and von Mises yield criteria, and an introduction to fatigue under repeated loading.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Transform plane stress to any angle and find principal stresses and planes.
  • Draw and use Mohr’s circle.
  • Apply the Tresca and von Mises criteria to find a factor of safety.
  • Explain fatigue and why parts can fail far below their static strength.

Key concepts

  • The same stress state looks different on different planes. Rotating by θ changes σ and τ, but some planes have zero shear: the principal planes.
  • Principal stresses are σ₁,₂ = (σx + σy)/2 ± R, with R = √(((σx − σy)/2)² + τxy²). The maximum in-plane shear is R, on planes 45° from the principal planes.
  • Mohr’s circle plots (σ, τ) for every plane: center at (σx + σy)/2, radius R. An angle θ on the element is 2θ on the circle.
  • Ductile metals yield by shear. Tresca: the largest shear stress reaches σ_Y/2. Von Mises: σ_vm = √(σ₁² − σ₁σ₂ + σ₂²) reaches σ_Y (it fits test data a little better).
  • Repeated loading grows cracks from small flaws, so parts can break after millions of cycles at stresses well below yield. Designers use S–N curves and test full parts for their lifetime of cycles.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 7.1 A point has σx = 80 MPa, σy = −40 MPa and τxy = 30 MPa. Find the principal stresses, the principal angle and the maximum in-plane shear.

Show the steps
  1. Center = 20 MPa; R = √(60² + 30²) = 67.1 MPa.
  2. σ₁ = 20 + 67.1; σ₂ = 20 − 67.1.
  3. tan 2θ_p = 60/120 = 0.5.

Answer: σ₁ = 87.1 MPa, σ₂ = −47.1 MPa, θ_p = 13.3°, τ_max = 67.1 MPa.

Example 7.2 For that stress state, find the von Mises stress and the factor of safety for steel with σ_Y = 250 MPa.

Show the steps
  1. σ_vm = √(87.1² − (87.1)(−47.1) + 47.1²).
  2. FS = 250/σ_vm.

Answer: σ_vm = 117.9 MPa; FS = 2.12.

Example 7.3 Use Tresca for the same point.

Show the steps
  1. σ₁ and σ₂ have opposite signs, so τ_abs max = (σ₁ − σ₂)/2 = 67.1 MPa.
  2. FS = (σ_Y/2)/τ_max = 125/67.1.

Answer: FS = 1.86 (Tresca is a little more conservative).

Thin-Walled Pressure Vessels and Combined Loading

Hoop and axial stress in thin cylinders, stress in thin spheres, required wall thickness, and combining axial, bending, torsion and pressure stresses at a point.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute hoop and axial stress in thin-walled cylinders and spheres.
  • Find the required wall thickness for a pressure and allowable stress.
  • Superpose axial, bending, torsion and pressure stresses at a point.
  • Find principal and von Mises stresses for combined loads.

Key concepts

  • A vessel is thin-walled when r/t is about 10 or more; then the wall stress is nearly uniform through the thickness.
  • Cylinder: hoop stress σ_h = pr/t, axial stress σ_a = pr/(2t). The hoop stress is twice the axial, so overpressured pipes split lengthwise.

  • Under combined loads, add the stresses from each load at a point (superposition), then use Mohr’s circle or a failure criterion.
  • Units matter: 1 bar = 0.1 MPa and 1 psi = 6894.757 Pa (NIST).

The elastic curve and EI v″ = M, integration with boundary conditions, standard deflection formulas, superposition, serviceability limits and statically indeterminate beams.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Integrate EI v″ = M with boundary conditions to find slope and deflection.
  • Use standard deflection formulas and superposition.
  • Check deflection against a serviceability limit such as L/360.
  • Solve propped cantilevers and other indeterminate beams.

Key concepts

  • For small slopes, the elastic curve obeys EI d²v/dx² = M(x). Integrate twice and use the supports to fix the constants (v = 0 at pins; v = 0 and v′ = 0 at a fixed end).

Key concepts

  • A slender column can fail suddenly by bending sideways at a load far below the crushing load. That load is the critical load P_cr.
  • Euler: P_cr = π²EI/(KL)². K = 1 for pinned–pinned, 2 for fixed–free, 0.5 for fixed–fixed and about 0.7 for fixed–pinned.
  • Buckling uses the smallest I of the section: a column buckles about its weak axis.
  • Critical stress σ_cr = π²E/(KL/r)², with radius of gyration r = √(I/A). If σ_cr exceeds the yield strength, the short column yields first; between the two, inelastic buckling applies.
  • Thin cylindrical shells, like rocket tanks, are very sensitive to tiny imperfections, so designers multiply the ideal buckling load by a knockdown factor taken from tests.

Show the steps
  1. K = 2, so KL = 6 m and P_cr drops by 4.

Example 10.3 A solid 50 mm steel rod, 2 m long and pinned at both ends, has σ_Y = 250 MPa. Does it buckle or yield first?

  1. r = d/4 = 12.5 mm; KL/r = 2000/12.5 = 160.
  2. σ_cr = π² × 200,000/160² MPa.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Columns fail when the stress reaches yield.

    Actually: Slender columns buckle at much lower stress.

  • Myth: A column buckles about its strong axis.

    Actually: It buckles about the axis with the smallest I.

  • Myth: The Euler load is what a real column will carry.

    Actually: Imperfections and eccentric loads lower it, especially for thin shells.

How this helps save the planet

Lighter rockets launch the satellites that watch Earth’s climate, and buckling sets how light their tanks can be. NASA’s Engineering and Safety Center reports that its Shell Buckling Knockdown Factor team re-examined knockdown factors that had not been updated since the Apollo era of the 1960s. Eight subscale and two full-scale metallic cylinder tests led to new knockdown factors that are used on the Space Launch System core stage, giving both mass and cost savings. In one test, an 8-foot-diameter cylinder loaded up to 750,000 pounds buckled within 2.5% of the engineer’s prediction, and early results showed that removing weld lands can raise load capacity by up to 28%.

Failure load divided by allowable load.

Failure from repeated loading, often below yield.

Line of zero bending stress through the centroid.

Ratio of lateral to axial strain, with a minus sign.
