About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a one-semester junior aerospace structures course. It is modeled on the topics and depth of MIT 16.20 Structural Mechanics and the structures parts of MIT 16.01–16.04 Unified Engineering (free on MIT OpenCourseWare), and on courses such as Purdue AAE 352 and Georgia Tech AE 3140, using the FAA Pilot’s Handbook chapter on aircraft construction and PhET simulations, which we link where they help. None of those authors, schools or agencies endorses this page.

What we add: every unit ties to real work with a cited public source (composite truss-braced wings, high-rate composite manufacturing and lightweight materials), every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab has a free simulation, a spreadsheet or a safe home version.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

An aircraft carries its own structure on every flight, so every kilogram of structure costs fuel for the aircraft’s whole life. Lighter wings and fuselages, made possible by better analysis and better materials, are one of the main levers for cleaner flight.

NASA lists efficient aircraft technology as one of the three main ways to make aviation sustainable. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample or textbook values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Mechanics of Materials: stress, strain, bending, torsion and buckling basics.
  • Statics: free-body diagrams, shear and moment diagrams.
  • Calculus II and Linear Algebra: integrals and matrices.
  • Aircraft Performance helps with load factors but is not required.

How to study

  • Start every problem with a free-body diagram and the load path: where does the load enter, and how does it reach the supports?
  • Keep limit and ultimate loads separate, and write the safety factor explicitly.
  • For thin walls, think in shear flow q (N/m) first, then divide by thickness.
  • Check stiffness and buckling, not just strength: light structures usually fail by buckling.
  • Compare materials per kilogram (specific stiffness and strength), not per cubic metre.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer, and retake the quizzes a few days later.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer or tablet for the free PhET Hooke’s Law simulation.
  • A wooden or plastic ruler, a table edge, coins or small weights, tape and a second ruler for measuring.
  • Dry spaghetti, a kitchen scale, paper clips and safety glasses.
  • Spreadsheet software for the materials, fuselage and composites labs.

Unit 1 · Week 1

Flight Loads, Safety Factors and Materials

The structural parts of an aircraft and their load paths, load factors, limit and ultimate loads, the 1.5 safety factor, margin of safety, and choosing materials by specific stiffness and specific strength.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Trace the load path through wing, fuselage and tail.
  • Compute limit and ultimate loads from a load factor.
  • Compute a margin of safety.
  • Compare materials by specific stiffness and strength.

Key concepts

  • Lift on the wing skins goes into stringers and ribs, then into spars, then through the wing box into the fuselage. Every part of an aircraft is sized by following that load path.
  • Loads scale with load factor n = L/W. The limit load is the largest load expected in service (for example n = 2.5 for many transports); the structure must carry it without permanent deformation.
  • The ultimate load is the limit load times a safety factor, traditionally 1.5 in aviation; the structure must carry it without failing, if only for a few seconds.
  • Margin of safety MS = allowable/actual − 1. A positive margin passes; aircraft designers aim for small positive margins because extra margin means extra weight.
  • Because weight matters, materials are compared per kilogram: specific stiffness E/ρ and specific strength σ/ρ. Aluminum, titanium and steel have almost the same E/ρ; carbon composites beat all three.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 The sample airliner weighs 588.6 kN and has a limit load factor of 2.5. Find the limit and ultimate wing loads.

Show the steps
  1. Limit = 2.5 × 588.6 = 1471.5 kN.
  2. Ultimate = 1.5 × 1471.5.

Answer: Limit 1471.5 kN; ultimate 2207 kN.

Example 1.2 A 7075 part (σ_y = 503 MPa) sees 400 MPa at limit load. Find the margin of safety on yield.

Show the steps
  1. MS = 503/400 − 1.

Answer: MS = 0.258 (positive, so it passes).

Example 1.3 Compare the specific stiffness of 7075 aluminum and the sample carbon laminate.

Show the steps
  1. Al: 71.7 × 10⁹/2810 = 25.5 MJ/kg.
  2. CFRP: 70 × 10⁹/1600 = 43.75 MJ/kg.

Answer: The laminate is 1.71 times stiffer per kilogram.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Find the specific strength of 7075 aluminum.

    Show answer

    503 × 10⁶/2810 = 179 kN·m/kg.

  2. Find the specific strength of Ti-6Al-4V.

    Show answer

    880 × 10⁶/4430 = 199 kN·m/kg.

  3. Find E/ρ for 4130 steel.

    Show answer

  4. What does MS = −0.1 mean?

    Show answer

    The part fails the requirement; redesign.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: A bigger safety factor is always better.

    Actually: It adds weight and fuel burn for life.

  • Myth: Steel is stiffer, so it makes stiffer aircraft.

    Actually: Per kilogram it is no stiffer than aluminum.

  • Myth: Ultimate load is the expected load.

    Actually: Limit load is the expected maximum; ultimate is 1.5 times it.

How this helps save the planet

DOE’s lightweight-materials program reports that for cars and trucks a 10% cut in vehicle weight can improve fuel economy by 6% to 8%, because it takes less energy to accelerate a lighter object. DOE lists typical mass reductions of 50–70% for carbon fiber composites, 30–60% for aluminum and 40–55% for titanium when they replace conventional steel parts.

Aircraft gain even more than cars from weight savings, because they must also hold their weight up for the whole flight. Choosing materials by specific stiffness and strength is where that saving starts.

Unit 2 · Week 2

Wing Shear and Bending Loads

The wing as a cantilever beam, spanwise lift distributions, shear force and bending moment diagrams, root loads, relief from wing weight and fuel, and how load factor scales everything.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Model a wing as a cantilever.
  • Draw shear and bending moment diagrams for a distributed lift.
  • Compute root shear and root bending moment.
  • Explain bending relief from fuel and engines on the wing.

Key concepts

  • Each wing half carries half the lift and acts as a cantilever clamped at the fuselage. Shear and moment are largest at the root.
  • For a uniform lift w (N/m) on a semispan s, the root shear is V = ws and the root moment is M = ws²/2.
  • An elliptical lift distribution puts the half-lift’s centroid at 4s/(3π) from the root, a little inboard of the uniform case’s s/2, so the root moment is lower.
  • Wing structure and fuel weigh down on the wing, opposite to lift. This bending relief is why airliners carry fuel in the wings and why wing-mounted engines help.
  • All loads scale with load factor: at n = 2.5 the root moment is 2.5 times its 1-g value.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 The sample airliner (588.6 kN) has a 17 m semispan. Assuming uniform lift at 1 g, find w, root shear and root moment for one wing.

Show the steps
  1. Half-lift = 294.3 kN; w = 294,300/17 = 17.31 kN/m.
  2. V = 294.3 kN.
  3. M = 17,312 × 17²/2.

Answer: w = 17.31 kN/m; V = 294.3 kN; M = 2.50 MN·m.

Example 2.2 Repeat the moment for the elliptical distribution.

Show the steps
  1. M = 294,300 × 4 × 17/(3π).

Answer: M = 2.12 MN·m, 15.1% lower.

Example 2.3 Find the uniform-lift root moment at limit (n = 2.5) and ultimate (×1.5).

Show the steps
  1. Limit: 2.5 × 2.50 = 6.25 MN·m.
  2. Ultimate: 1.5 × 6.25.

Answer: Limit 6.25 MN·m; ultimate 9.38 MN·m.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Halve the semispan at the same lift. Root moment?

    Show answer

    Halves (M = Ls/4 for uniform lift).

  2. Find the moment ratio, elliptical to uniform.

    Show answer

    8/(3π) = 0.849.

  3. Why do airliners use wing fuel first or last?

    Show answer

    Fuel in the wing relieves bending; keeping it longer helps.

  4. Root shear at n = 2.5?

    Show answer

    2.5 × 294.3 = 735.8 kN.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: The wing tip carries the biggest loads.

    Actually: The root does.

  • Myth: Wing weight adds to the bending moment.

    Actually: It relieves it.

  • Myth: Moment scales with the semispan.

    Actually: At fixed w it scales with s².

How this helps save the planet

NASA’s SWEET-15 test article is a 15-foot composite wing supported by an aerodynamic strut, based on NASA’s Transonic Truss-Braced Wing concept. A strut takes part of the bending load near mid-span, so the wing can be long and thin without a huge root moment.

NASA engineers bent the test wing for several months in the Flight Loads Laboratory at Armstrong, using numerous strain and load sensors including fiber-optic strain sensors. The data confirmed the computer models’ predictions and showed the wing withstood the anticipated in-flight forces.

Unit 3 · Weeks 3–4

Bending of Thin-Walled Beams

Bending stress σ = My/I, idealizing a wing box as flanges and skins, second moment of area, flange forces, unsymmetric bending, and sizing spar caps for ultimate load.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute bending stress from M, y and I.
  • Idealize a box beam as concentrated flange areas.
  • Size spar caps for an ultimate moment.
  • Recognize unsymmetric bending.

Key concepts

  • In bending, σ = My/I: stress grows linearly from the neutral axis. Material far from the neutral axis does most of the work.
  • Wing boxes are idealized as concentrated flange (boom) areas carrying direct stress, joined by thin skins carrying shear.
  • For two flanges of area A a depth h apart, I = 2A(h/2)² = Ah²/2 and the flange force is F = M/h.
  • Deeper wing boxes need smaller flanges for the same moment. That is why thick wing roots save weight, and why thin, high-aspect-ratio wings need help such as struts.
  • If the section is not symmetric, or the moment is not about a principal axis, bending about one axis causes deflection about the other (unsymmetric bending).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 The ultimate root moment is 9.38 MN·m and the wing box is 0.6 m deep. Find the flange force.

Show the steps
  1. F = 9.38 × 10⁶/0.6.

Answer: F = 15.6 MN.

Example 3.2 Size each flange for 400 MPa allowable stress.

Show the steps
  1. A = 9.38 × 10⁶/(400 × 10⁶ × 0.6).

Answer: A = 0.0391 m² (391 cm²) per flange.

Example 3.3 Find I for two 0.0391 m² flanges 0.6 m apart, and check σ.

Show the steps
  1. I = 0.0391 × 0.6²/2 = 0.00704 m⁴.
  2. σ = 9.38 × 10⁶ × 0.3/0.00704.

Answer: σ = 400 MPa, as designed.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Double the box depth. Required flange area?

    Show answer

    Halves.

  2. Where is bending stress zero?

    Show answer

    At the neutral axis.

  3. Find I for flanges of 0.01 m² at ±0.25 m.

    Show answer

    2 × 0.01 × 0.25² = 0.00125 m⁴.

  4. Why are spar caps placed at the top and bottom?

    Show answer

    Largest y gives the most I per kilogram.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Thin skins carry most of the bending.

    Actually: In the idealization, flanges and stringers carry direct stress; skins carry shear.

  • Myth: Stress is uniform across a beam.

    Actually: It varies linearly with y.

  • Myth: A deeper beam is heavier.

    Actually: It usually needs less flange material.

How this helps save the planet

NASA describes the truss-braced wing as a long, thin wing with aerodynamically shaped structural supports that could cut fuel and operating costs for future airliners. NASA and Boeing tested a semispan model of the concept in a large pressurized wind tunnel in December 2025.

A thin wing has a shallow wing box, so by F = M/h its flanges would need to be heavy. The strut lowers M, which lets the wing stay thin without growing heavy.

Unit 4 · Week 5

Torsion and Shear Flow in Closed Sections

Shear flow q, Bredt–Batho torsion of thin-walled closed sections, skin shear stress, the rate of twist, multi-cell boxes, and why wings are built as closed boxes.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use shear flow q = τt.
  • Apply Bredt–Batho: T = 2Aq.
  • Compute the rate of twist of a closed section.
  • Explain why closed sections resist torsion so well.

Key concepts

  • In a thin wall, shear stress times thickness is the shear flow q = τt (N/m). Around a closed section carrying pure torque, q is constant.
  • Bredt–Batho: T = 2A_e q, where A_e is the area enclosed by the wall’s midline.
  • The rate of twist is dθ/dz = (T/(4A_e²G)) ∮ds/t.
  • Closed boxes are far stiffer in torsion than open sections of the same material, which is why wings are built as torsion boxes from spars and skins.
  • Wing torsion comes from lift acting ahead of or behind the shear center, and from ailerons and engines. Too little torsional stiffness can cause aileron reversal or flutter.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 A 2.0 m × 0.6 m wing box carries a 500 kN·m torque. Find the shear flow.

Show the steps
  1. A_e = 1.2 m².
  2. q = 500,000/(2 × 1.2).

Answer: q = 208.3 kN/m.

Example 4.2 Find the skin shear stress for 4 mm skins.

Show the steps
  1. τ = 208,333/0.004.

Answer: τ = 52.1 MPa.

Example 4.3 Find the rate of twist (G = 27 GPa, uniform 4 mm wall).

Show the steps
  1. ∮ds/t = 5.2/0.004 = 1300.
  2. dθ/dz = 500,000 × 1300/(4 × 1.2² × 27 × 10⁹).

Answer: dθ/dz = 4.18 × 10⁻³ rad/m.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. A thin tube of radius 0.1 m and wall 2 mm carries 10 kN·m. Find τ.

    Show answer

    T/(2πr²t) = 79.6 MPa.

  2. Find J for that tube.

    Show answer

    2πr³t = 1.257 × 10⁻⁵ m⁴.

  3. Double the enclosed area at the same torque. Shear flow?

    Show answer

    Halves.

  4. Why does cutting a slit in a tube ruin its torsional stiffness?

    Show answer

    It becomes an open section.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Shear flow varies around a closed section in pure torsion.

    Actually: It is constant.

  • Myth: Thicker skins always help torsion most.

    Actually: Enclosed area matters more.

  • Myth: Open and closed sections are similar in torsion.

    Actually: Closed ones are many times stiffer.

Unit 5 · Week 6

Shear in Open Sections and the Shear Center

Shear stress in beams τ = VQ/(It), shear flow in open thin-walled sections, the shear center, why channel sections twist under load, and shear lag.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use τ = VQ/(It).
  • Find shear flow in an open thin-walled section.
  • Locate the shear center qualitatively.
  • Explain why loads off the shear center cause twist.

Key concepts

  • Transverse shear produces shear stress τ = VQ/(It), where Q is the first moment of area beyond the cut.
  • In open thin-walled sections, shear flow starts at zero at a free edge and builds up along the wall: q = VQ/I.
  • The shear center is the point where a transverse load causes bending without twist. For symmetric I-beams and closed boxes it is on the symmetry axis; for a channel it lies outside the web.
  • Loads applied away from the shear center twist the section. Designers place spars and stringers so the wing’s shear center sits where aerodynamic loads act, or provide enough torsional stiffness.
  • Shear lag: in wide flanges, direct stress is highest near the webs and lower in between, so not all of the flange is fully effective.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 A solid rectangular beam 20 mm wide and 60 mm deep carries 6 kN of shear. Find the maximum shear stress.

Show the steps
  1. A = 1200 mm².
  2. τ_max = 1.5 × 6000/1200.

Answer: τ_max = 7.5 MPa at the neutral axis.

Example 5.2 An I-beam web is 4 mm thick; at the neutral axis Q = 50 × 10³ mm³ and I = 20 × 10⁶ mm⁴. Find τ for V = 40 kN.

Show the steps
  1. τ = 40,000 × 50,000/(20 × 10⁶ × 4).

Answer: τ = 25 MPa.

Example 5.3 Find the shear flow there.

Show the steps
  1. q = τt = 25 × 4.

Answer: q = 100 N/mm (100 kN/m).

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Where is shear flow zero in an open section?

    Show answer

    At free edges.

  2. Shear center of a closed symmetric box under symmetric load?

    Show answer

    On the symmetry axis.

  3. Why does a channel twist when loaded through its web?

    Show answer

    The shear center lies outside the web.

  4. Double V on the rectangle. τ_max?

    Show answer

    15 MPa.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Shear stress is largest at the outer fibres.

    Actually: It is largest at the neutral axis.

  • Myth: The shear center is the centroid.

Unit 6 · Weeks 7–8

Buckling of Columns, Stringers and Plates

Euler column buckling and end conditions, slenderness, stringers as columns, plate buckling of skin panels, post-buckling strength of stiffened panels, and why light structures are usually sized by stability.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute Euler buckling loads with end-fixity factors.
  • Compute the buckling stress of a skin panel.
  • Explain why stiffened panels carry load after skin buckling.
  • Recognize when buckling, not yield, governs.

Key concepts

  • A slender column buckles at P_cr = π²EI/(KL)², where K = 1 for pinned ends, 0.5 for fixed ends and 2 for a cantilever.
  • Buckling depends on stiffness E and geometry, not strength, so high-strength materials do not help a slender column.
  • A thin flat plate in compression buckles at σ_cr = kπ²E/(12(1 − ν²)) (t/b)², with k = 4 for a long simply supported panel.
  • Aircraft skins are allowed to buckle in shear or compression at loads well below ultimate; the stringers and spars then carry the load (tension-field and post-buckling behavior).
  • Because light structures are thin, buckling usually sets the size of compression skins, stringers and spar webs.

Unit 7 · Week 9

Pressurized Fuselages and Thin Shells

Cabin pressurization, hoop and axial stresses in thin cylinders, spherical bulkheads, combined pressure and bending, cutouts and stress concentrations around windows and doors, and fatigue from pressure cycles.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute hoop and axial stress in a pressurized cylinder.
  • Find the skin thickness needed for a given stress.
  • Explain why windows are rounded.
  • Relate pressure cycles to fatigue.

Key concepts

  • A thin cylinder of radius r and wall t under pressure difference Δp has hoop stress σ_h = Δp r/t and axial stress σ_a = Δp r/(2t): the hoop stress is twice the axial.
  • A spherical shell has σ = Δp r/(2t), so domed pressure bulkheads can be thinner than the cylinder.
  • The fuselage also bends under its own weight and tail loads, adding to the axial stress on top or bottom.
  • Cutouts for windows and doors concentrate stress; rounded corners and doublers keep the concentration low. Sharp corners can concentrate stress by a factor of three or more.
  • Each flight is one pressure cycle. Over tens of thousands of flights, these cycles drive fatigue cracking, the subject of Unit 9.

Unit 8 · Weeks 10–11

Composite Materials and Laminates

Fibers and matrices, fiber volume fraction, the rule of mixtures for stiffness and density, transverse stiffness, ply orientation and laminates, quasi-isotropic layups, and composite manufacturing.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use the rule of mixtures for longitudinal stiffness and density.
  • Estimate transverse stiffness.
  • Explain why laminates use several ply angles.
  • Compare composite and metal panels at equal stiffness.

Key concepts

  • A composite ply is stiff fibers (carbon, glass) in a softer matrix (epoxy or a thermoplastic). Fibers carry the load; the matrix holds them together and transfers load.
  • Along the fibers the rule of mixtures holds: E₁ = E_fV_f + E_mV_m, and density ρ = ρ_fV_f + ρ_mV_m.
  • Across the fibers the inverse rule applies: 1/E₂ = V_f/E_f + V_m/E_m, so a ply is many times less stiff transversely.
  • Laminates stack plies at 0°, ±45° and 90° so the part is stiff in every direction it is loaded. A quasi-isotropic layup behaves almost like a metal sheet in-plane, but lighter.
  • For a panel in bending at equal stiffness, mass scales as ρ/E^(1/3), which is why low-density materials win for skins.

Unit 9 · Weeks 12–13

Fatigue, Fracture and Damage Tolerance

S-N curves and endurance, Miner’s rule for cumulative damage, stress concentrations, the stress intensity factor, fracture toughness and critical crack length, crack growth and the Paris law, and damage-tolerant design and inspection.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use Miner’s rule.
  • Compute the stress intensity factor for a crack.
  • Find a critical crack length.
  • Explain safe-life, fail-safe and damage-tolerant design.

Key concepts

  • Repeated loads well below yield can grow cracks until a part fails: fatigue. S-N curves give cycles to failure at each stress amplitude.
  • Miner’s rule adds damage from different stress levels: D = Σ nᵢ/Nᵢ, with failure predicted at D = 1.
  • A crack of length a under stress σ has stress intensity K = σ√(πa) (times a geometry factor). Fracture happens when K reaches the material’s fracture toughness K_IC.
  • So the critical crack length is a_c = (K_IC/σ)²/π. Cracks grow per cycle as da/dN = C(ΔK)^m (Paris law), so inspection intervals are set to find cracks long before a_c.
  • Modern aircraft are damage tolerant: they are designed to carry limit load with a crack or damage present until the next inspection finds it.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 9.1 A part sees 10⁴ cycles at a level with N = 10⁵, and 2 × 10⁵ cycles at a level with N = 10⁶. Find the Miner damage.

Show the steps
  1. D = 10⁴/10⁵ + 2 × 10⁵/10⁶.

Answer: D = 0.30: 30% of life used.

Example 9.2 Find K for a 10 mm crack under 100 MPa.

Show the steps
  1. K = 100 × √(π × 0.01).

Answer: K = 17.7 MPa√m.

Example 9.3 Find the critical crack length at 100 MPa for an alloy with K_IC = 37 MPa√m (sample value).

Show the steps
  1. a_c = (37/100)²/π.

Answer: a_c = 43.6 mm.

Lightweight Design and Sustainable Structures

Learning objectives

  • Estimate fuel saved by removing structural weight.
  • Explain the weight snowball.
  • Use material indices to choose materials.
  • Discuss recycling and life-cycle trade-offs.

Key concepts

  • Structure is typically about a quarter to a third of an airliner’s takeoff weight (a textbook range), so structural weight is a major lever on fuel.
  • Weight snowballs: extra structure needs more lift, more fuel and bigger engines, which add more structure. Savings snowball the same way in reverse.
  • From the Breguet equation at fixed range, fuel burned = W_landing (e^(Rc/(V·L/D)) − 1), so fuel scales with landing weight; every kilogram saved cuts fuel in proportion.
  • Material indices pick the lightest material for each job: E/ρ for ties, E^(1/2)/ρ for beams and E^(1/3)/ρ for panels in bending.
  • Lifecycle matters too: manufacturing energy, repairability and recycling of composites and alloys all count alongside in-service fuel.

Lab F: Paper clip fatigue and Miner’s rule

Week 13

Goal: Bend paper clips back and forth to failure at two bend angles and test Miner’s rule.

Procedure

  1. Straighten several identical paper clips.
  2. Bend one through ±90° until it breaks; count cycles. Repeat for ±45°.
  3. On a fresh clip, do half the ±90° life, then bend at ±45° to failure.
  4. Compare the second-stage cycles with Miner’s prediction.

Glossary

Buckling
Sudden sideways instability of a compressed member.
Damage tolerance
Ability to carry load safely with cracks until inspection finds them.
Fatigue
Failure under repeated loading below yield.
Fracture toughness
Stress intensity at which a crack runs.
Hoop stress
Circumferential stress in a pressurized cylinder.
Laminate
Stack of composite plies at chosen angles.
Limit load
Largest load expected in service.
Load factor
Lift divided by weight.
Margin of safety
Allowable over actual, minus one.
Miner’s rule
Cumulative fatigue damage sum.
Rib
Chordwise member that shapes the wing and supports stringers.
Shear center
Point where a transverse load causes no twist.
Shear flow
Shear force per unit length along a thin wall.
Spar
Main spanwise beam of a wing.
Specific stiffness
Elastic modulus divided by density.
Stress intensity factor
Measure of stress near a crack tip.
Stringer
Spanwise stiffener attached to the skin.
Torsion box
Closed wing box resisting twist.
Ultimate load
Limit load times the safety factor.
Wing box
Spars and skins forming the main wing structure.
