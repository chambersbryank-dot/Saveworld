Space Disposal

Impact Statement

Space disposal has moved from slides to hardware. In 2024 Astroscale's ADRAS-J flew to within about 15 meters of a large piece of debris and photographed it. The next step is no longer looking. It is removing.

Featured Mission: ADRAS-J2

ADRAS-J2 is Astroscale's follow-on mission and Phase II of JAXA's Commercial Removal of Debris Demonstration (CRD2) program, a contract worth about 13.2 billion yen. It targets the same Japanese H-IIA rocket upper stage that ADRAS-J photographed in 2024 — an 11-meter, 3-ton object never designed to be captured. ADRAS-J2 will use a robotic arm to capture that non-cooperative debris and lower its orbit so it burns up in the atmosphere. Launch is targeted for Japan's fiscal 2027 (April 2027 to March 2028) on Isar Aerospace's Spectrum rocket from Andøya, Norway. It turns an inspection demo into the world's first removal of legacy debris.

Europe is close behind with ClearSpace: PRELUDE in June 2027, then ClearSpace-1 for ESA's PROBA-1 (targeted 2028; ESA lists 2029). Full stories: Astroscale and ClearSpace.

Potential solution on the horizon

PRELUDE (with ESA). This is a potential solution ClearSpace is advancing. We expect to know more within the next nine months and will keep you updated here.

The Five W's of Space Disposal

Who

The organizations doing hands-on debris removal: Astroscale and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA) in Japan, and ClearSpace working with the European Space Agency (ESA) in Europe.

What

Robotic capture and deorbit of legacy debris — servicer spacecraft that rendezvous with dead rocket bodies and satellites, grab them with robotic arms, and lower their orbits so they burn up in the atmosphere.

When

Ongoing. Inspection missions have already flown, and the first capture-and-removal missions are targeted for 2027–2028.

Where

Low Earth orbit — the crowded band a few hundred kilometers up where most debris, and most of the satellites we depend on, travel.

Why

Debris threatens the satellites that monitor our climate and guide ships safely across the ocean. One collision can make thousands of new fragments. Protecting orbits protects Earth.

Natural Debris and Saving Moons

Jupiter

The original debris remover. Jupiter's gravity swallows or flings away comets and asteroids, taking comet impacts roughly 2,000 times more often than Earth, with honest trade-offs for asteroids.

Saving Moons: Titan

Saturn's hazy moon is a prebiotic time capsule. NASA's Dragonfly rotorcraft (launch period July 2028, arrival late 2034) will explore it, building the radar, long-life power and autonomy toolkit that also helps protect Earth.

Natural Debris Solution: Atmospheric Drag

Earth's upper atmosphere pulls low-orbit junk down to burn up, harder when the Sun is active. Below 600 km it works within years; above 1,000 km, not for a millennium. Jupiter's partner in natural cleanup.

Missions and Solutions

ClearSpace

MMX

U.S. Deorbit Vehicle (NASA / SpaceX)

Agency solution · active (contracted)

Impact Statement

The International Space Station is the largest object humans have put in orbit, and it cannot simply be left to fall. NASA selected SpaceX to develop the U.S. Deorbit Vehicle, which will steer the station down in a controlled way after its operational life ends in 2030, avoiding risk to populated areas. NASA will own and operate the vehicle once it is built.

Source: NASA, U.S. Deorbit Vehicle selection

For Further Information: NASA, U.S. Deorbit Vehicle selection

The Five W's of U.S. Deorbit Vehicle

Who

NASA, with SpaceX developing the vehicle; the station's safe deorbit is the responsibility of all five partner space agencies.

Source: NASA, U.S. Deorbit Vehicle selection

What

A dedicated spacecraft that will provide the capability to deorbit the station in a controlled manner.

Source: NASA, U.S. Deorbit Vehicle selection

When

Station operations are planned through 2030; the deorbit follows the end of its operational life.

Source: NASA, U.S. Deorbit Vehicle selection

Where

Low Earth orbit, ending in a controlled reentry that avoids risk to populated areas.

Source: NASA, U.S. Deorbit Vehicle selection

Why

A controlled end keeps the largest human-made object in orbit from becoming the largest piece of uncontrolled debris.

Source: NASA, U.S. Deorbit Vehicle selection

Aeolus Assisted Reentry (ESA)

Agency solution · active (Zero Debris approach)

Impact Statement

When ESA's Aeolus wind satellite ran out of mission life, it was not designed for a controlled ending. In July 2023 ESA's mission control used its last fuel to lower it to 120 km and line it up so any surviving pieces would fall along a planned Atlantic track, the first assisted reentry of its kind. The already low risk from falling debris dropped by a factor of 150, and the satellite spent weeks less adrift in orbit.

Source: ESA, Aeolus: a historic end

Source: ESA, Final images before Aeolus demise

It is part of ESA's Zero Debris approach: by 2030 all ESA missions are to be debris neutral.

Source: ESA, Zero Debris Charter

For Further Information: ESA, Aeolus: a historic end

ESA's first-of-its-kind assisted reentry of the Aeolus satellite cut the already low risk from falling debris by a factor of 150.

The Five W's of Aeolus Assisted Reentry

Who

The European Space Agency, operating from its ESOC mission control in Darmstadt, Germany.

Source: ESA, Aeolus: a historic end

What

A series of engine burns that steered a satellite never designed for controlled reentry into a safer, planned reentry.

Source: ESA, Aeolus: a historic end

When

Burns from July 24, 2023; Aeolus reentered above Antarctica on July 28, 2023.

Source: ESA, Aeolus: a historic end

Where

Low Earth orbit, ending in Earth's atmosphere with debris targeted at the Atlantic ground track.

Source: ESA, Aeolus: a historic end

Why

To show that old satellites can be brought home more safely, and to push ESA toward debris-neutral missions by 2030.

Source: ESA, Aeolus: a historic end

Source: ESA, Zero Debris Charter

LignoSat Wooden Satellite (Kyoto University)

University and company solution · demonstrated; follow-on on the horizon

Impact Statement

Metal satellites leave alumina particles in the upper atmosphere when they burn up; wood burns away more cleanly. LignoSat, built by Kyoto University with Sumitomo Forestry from honoki magnolia panels joined without glue or nails, was deployed from the International Space Station by JAXA on December 9, 2024. It stayed in one piece until reentering on April 4, 2025, though no radio signal was received.

Source: NASA, JAXA’s first wooden satellite deploys

Source: Kyoto University, LignoSat report (UNISEC, Aug 2026)

Potential solution on the horizon

LignoSat follow-on. This is a potential solution Kyoto University is advancing. We expect to know more within the coming months and will keep you updated here.

Source: Kyoto University, LignoSat report (UNISEC, Aug 2026)

For Further Information: NASA, JAXA’s first wooden satellite deploys

Kyoto University with Sumitomo Forestry; JAXA deployed it from the space station.

Source: NASA, JAXA’s first wooden satellite deploys

A 10 cm CubeSat with a wooden structure, testing how wood handles strain, temperature and radiation in space.

When

Deployed December 9, 2024; reentered April 4, 2025.

Europa Clipper (NASA)

Agency solution · active

Impact Statement

Europa Clipper launched on October 14, 2024 and is the first mission designed to study in detail an ocean world beyond Earth. It will travel 1.8 billion miles to reach Jupiter in April 2030, orbit the planet and fly past Europa 49 times to find out whether the moon's hidden ocean has conditions suitable for life.

Source: NASA, Europa Clipper

Learning how a frozen ocean world works sharpens our understanding of water, chemistry and habitability, the same things we protect on Earth.

For Further Information: NASA, Europa Clipper

NASA's Europa Clipper will travel 1.8 billion miles to Jupiter and fly past the ocean moon Europa 49 times.

Image: NASA/JPL-Caltech.

The Five W's of Europa Clipper

Who

NASA.

Source: NASA, Europa Clipper

What

An orbiter that will make 49 close flybys of Europa to study its ice shell, ocean and chemistry.

Source: NASA, Europa Clipper

When

Launched October 14, 2024; Earth gravity-assist flyby December 3, 2026; arrives at Jupiter in April 2030.

Source: NASA, Europa Clipper

Where

Jupiter's icy moon Europa, about 1.8 billion miles of travel from Earth.

Source: NASA, Europa Clipper

Why

To determine whether Europa has conditions suitable to support life.

Source: NASA, Europa Clipper

Juice (ESA)

Agency solution · active

Impact Statement

ESA's Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer, Juice, launched on April 14, 2023 to study Jupiter and its three large ocean-bearing moons, Ganymede, Callisto and Europa, as both worlds and possible habitats. Its September 2026 Earth flyby is done; the next is in January 2029, and it reaches Jupiter in July 2031 for 35 moon flybys. NASA's JPL built parts of its RIME radar, which probes beneath the ice.

Source: ESA, Juice

Comparing ocean moons with our own ocean planet helps explain what makes a world habitable, and how rare Earth is.

For Further Information: ESA, Juice

Image: NASA/JPL-Caltech.

The Five W's of Juice

Who

ESA, flying ten instruments.

Source: ESA, Juice

What

A spacecraft with 85 square metres of solar wings and ten instruments, studying Jupiter and three ocean-bearing moons.

Source: ESA, Juice

When

Launched April 14, 2023 on Ariane 5; Earth flybys September 2026 (done) and January 2029; arrival July 2031.

Source: ESA, Juice

Where

Jupiter and its moons Ganymede, Callisto and Europa.

Source: ESA, Juice

Why

To characterise the icy moons as possible habitats and learn how gas giants and their moons form.

Source: ESA, Juice

Juice: Mission Status

Cruising. September 2026 Earth flyby complete; next Earth flyby January 2029. Dates per ESA; missions shift.

Northrop Grumman SpaceLogistics

Corporate solution · active (company figures labeled)

Impact Statement

Northrop Grumman says its SpaceLogistics Mission Extension Vehicles are the only spacecraft to extend the life of commercial satellites running low on fuel: two MEVs have docked with three satellites in geosynchronous orbit and provided more than ten years of combined life extension (company figures). Its Mission Robotic Vehicle and three Mission Extension Pods launched in July 2026; the robot will install the pods as "jetpacks" on client satellites. Keeping satellites working longer means fewer replacements and less junk.

Source: Northrop Grumman, MRV launch (company release)

Source: Northrop Grumman, SpaceLogistics (company page)

Starfish Space Otter

Corporate solution · active (company figures labeled)

Impact Statement

Starfish Space's first Otter launched on October 1, 2026 from Vandenberg on SpaceX's Transporter-18 rideshare to fly NASA's SSPICY mission. In early 2027 it will autonomously approach up to four inoperable U.S. objects in low Earth orbit, such as satellites and rocket bodies, to inspect them without touching. NASA says these technologies could one day enable repair, or pulling dead objects into the atmosphere for disposal. Starfish designs Otter for life extension and end-of-life disposal (company statement).

Source: NASA, SSPICY mission
