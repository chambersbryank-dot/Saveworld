Space Weather & Shields

Impact Statement

Shields We Live Inside

Earth is protected by layers: the Sun's own bubble, our planet's magnetic field, and the air itself. Space weather is what happens when the Sun pushes on those shields. The missions on this page watch the Sun so power grids, satellites and astronauts get warning in time.

The Five W's of Space Weather & Shields

Who

NOAA, ESA and ISRO, plus Earth's natural shields.

What

Watching the Sun so forecasters can warn about solar storms, and understanding the shields that protect us.

When

SOLAR-1 entered operations June 10, 2026; Aditya-L1 has observed from L1 since January 2024; Vigil launches in 2031.

Source: NOAA, SOLAR-1 enters operations

Source: ISRO, Halo orbit insertion

Source: ESA, Vigil mission overview

Where

The Sun-Earth L1 and L5 points, and Earth's magnetosphere and atmosphere.

Why

Vigil can give up to four to five days' notice of some space weather effects, protecting power grids, satellites and astronauts.

Source: ESA, Vigil mission overview

Three Invisible Shields

Natural solution

1. The Heliosphere

The Sun blows a bubble of solar wind around the whole solar system. Voyager 1 crossed its edge, the heliopause, on August 25, 2012, at about 122 AU (about 18 billion km) from the Sun, the first human-made object to reach interstellar space. Voyager 2 followed in 2018.

Source: NASA, Voyager Interstellar Mission

NASA's IMAP launched on September 24, 2025 to the L1 point to map that boundary from afar.

Source: NASA, IMAP launch release

2. The Magnetosphere

Earth's magnetic field deflects most of the charged solar wind and helps keep it from stripping away our atmosphere. It is not a perfect wall: strong solar storms still get through and drive geomagnetic storms and auroras.

Source: NASA, Earth's magnetosphere

3. The Atmosphere and Ozone Layer

High in the stratosphere, the ozone layer absorbs most of the Sun's biologically harmful UV-B radiation before it reaches the ground.

Source: NOAA, Ozone basics

These shields are free, natural and have run for billions of years. Our job is to understand them and avoid damaging the one we can: the ozone layer.

SOLAR-1 (NOAA)

Agency solution · active

NOAA's SOLAR-1 (formerly SWFO-L1) launched on September 24, 2025 on a Falcon 9, reached the Sun-Earth L1 point, about one million miles from Earth, on January 23, 2026, and entered operational service on June 10, 2026.

Source: NOAA NESDIS, SOLAR-1 reaches L1

Source: NOAA, SOLAR-1 enters operations

It is the first U.S. satellite designed exclusively for continuous, operational space weather observations, streaming solar wind data and coronal mass ejection images to NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center in real time, free to the public.

Source: NOAA, SOLAR-1 enters operations

Read more about SOLAR-1

Vigil (ESA)

Agency solution · active (in development, contracted)

Vigil will watch the Sun from the fifth Lagrange point (L5), seeing the "side" of the Sun and solar activity days before it rotates into view from Earth. ESA says it can give up to four to five days' notice of certain space weather effects, and measure the speed and direction of solar eruptions more clearly.

Source: ESA, Vigil mission overview

ESA signed a €340 million contract with Airbus Defence and Space UK to build it. Launch is planned for 2031 on an Ariane 6, with an expected five years of operations at L5.

Source: ESA, Airbus to build Vigil

Source: ESA, Vigil mission overview

Read more about Vigil

Aditya-L1 (ISRO)

Agency solution · active

India's first space-based solar observatory launched on PSLV-C57 on September 2, 2023 and entered a halo orbit around L1, about 1.5 million km from Earth, on January 6, 2024. From there it watches the Sun without eclipses.

Source: ISRO, Halo orbit insertion

It carries seven payloads: four look directly at the Sun (VELC, SUIT, SoLEXS, HEL1OS) and three measure particles and fields at L1 (ASPEX, PAPA, magnetometer), helping explain coronal mass ejections, flares and space weather.

Source: ISRO, Aditya-L1

Read more about Aditya-L1

IMAP (NASA)

Agency solution · active

Impact Statement

NASA's IMAP (Interstellar Mapping and Acceleration Probe) launched on September 24, 2025 and works from the Sun–Earth L1 point, about one million miles from Earth toward the Sun. From there it can give astronauts and spacecraft near Earth about a half hour's warning of harmful radiation, and its I-ALiRT real-time link shares space weather data with forecasters.

Source: NASA, IMAP mission

Source: NASA, IMAP launch release

For Further Information: NASA, IMAP mission

From about a million miles toward the Sun, NASA's IMAP can give astronauts and spacecraft near Earth about a half hour's warning of incoming radiation.

The Five W's of IMAP

Who

NASA, which runs IMAP as an active heliophysics mission.

Source: NASA, IMAP mission

What

A 900 kg spacecraft that measures particles from the Sun and maps the boundary of the heliosphere, the bubble that helps shield the solar system from cosmic rays.

Source: NASA, IMAP mission

When

Launched September 24, 2025, together with NOAA's space weather satellite; now operating at L1.

Source: NASA, IMAP launch release

Where

The Sun–Earth L1 Lagrange point, about one million miles from Earth toward the Sun.

Source: NASA, IMAP mission

Why

Earlier warnings of solar radiation protect astronauts, satellites and the technology we rely on, and IMAP's maps show how the heliosphere shields life in the solar system.

Source: NASA, IMAP mission

GOES-19 and CCOR-1 (NOAA)

Agency solution · active

Impact Statement

NOAA's GOES-19 became the operational GOES-East satellite on April 7, 2025. On board is CCOR-1, the world's first operational, space-based coronagraph dedicated to space weather: it blocks the Sun's bright disk and images its faint outer corona every 15 minutes to spot coronal mass ejections heading our way.

Source: NOAA OSPO, GOES status

Source: NOAA SWPC, CCOR-1 coronagraph

For Further Information: NOAA SWPC, CCOR-1 coronagraph

GOES-19's CCOR-1 is the world's first operational space-based coronagraph, imaging the Sun's faint corona every 15 minutes.

The Five W's of GOES-19 and CCOR-1

Who

NOAA, which operates the GOES series of weather satellites and its Space Weather Prediction Center uses CCOR-1 imagery.

Source: NOAA NESDIS, first operational space-based coronagraph

What

A weather satellite whose CCOR-1 coronagraph watches the corona from 3.7 to 17 solar radii, replacing the aging coronagraphs on SOHO.

Source: NOAA SWPC, CCOR-1 coronagraph

When

Launched June 25, 2024; CCOR-1 first light September 19, 2024; operational as GOES-East April 7, 2025.

Source: NOAA OSPO, GOES status

Source: NOAA NESDIS, first operational space-based coronagraph

Where

Geostationary orbit at 75.2° West, making CCOR-1 the first coronagraph to fly in geostationary orbit.

Source: NOAA OSPO, GOES status

Source: NOAA SWPC, CCOR-1 coronagraph

Why

Coronal mass ejections can disrupt satellites, GPS and power grids; seeing them early gives forecasters time to warn operators.

Source: NOAA NESDIS, first operational space-based coronagraph

PUNCH (NASA)

Agency solution · active

Impact Statement

NASA's PUNCH (Polarimeter to Unify the Corona and Heliosphere) is a constellation of four small satellites in low Earth orbit, launched March 11, 2025. Together they make global, 3D observations of the Sun's corona to learn how its mass and energy become the solar wind that fills the solar system, treating the Sun, solar wind and Earth as one connected system.

Source: NASA, PUNCH

Better understanding of the solar wind improves the space weather forecasts that protect satellites and power grids.

For Further Information: NASA, PUNCH

Image: NASA.

NASA.

Source: NASA, PUNCH

What

Four suitcase-sized satellites making 3D images of the corona and solar wind.

Source: NASA, PUNCH

When

Launched March 11, 2025; operating.

Source: NASA, PUNCH

Where

Low Earth orbit, watching the inner heliosphere.

Source: NASA, PUNCH

Why

To learn how the Sun's corona becomes the solar wind that reaches Earth.

Source: NASA, PUNCH

TRACERS (NASA)

Agency solution · active

Impact Statement

NASA's TRACERS (Tandem Reconnection and Cusp Electrodynamics Reconnaissance Satellites) launched on July 23, 2025. The twin satellites fly in low Earth orbit through the polar cusps, funnel-shaped openings in Earth's magnetic field, to study magnetic reconnection: what happens when activity from the Sun meets the magnetosphere that protects us. Understanding it helps scientists prepare for the effects of solar activity on Earth.

Source: NASA, TRACERS

See how the magnetic field shields us on Three Shields.

For Further Information: NASA, TRACERS

Image: NASA.

Carruthers Geocorona Observatory (NASA)

Agency solution · active

Impact Statement

NASA's Carruthers Geocorona Observatory launched on September 24, 2025 and studies the geocorona, the ultraviolet glow of Earth's exosphere, our planet's outermost atmospheric layer. It is the first mission dedicated to charting changes in this region, which is hard to observe from the ground. It is named after Dr. George R. Carruthers, whose telescope took the first images of the geocorona from the Moon on Apollo 16 in 1972.

Source: NASA, Carruthers Geocorona Observatory

The exosphere is where Earth's environment meets space; learning how it responds to space weather completes the picture of our shields.

For Further Information: NASA, Carruthers Geocorona Observatory

Earth wears a faint ultraviolet halo, the geocorona, first photographed from the Moon by Apollo 16 in 1972; NASA's Carruthers mission now charts it from a million miles away.

Image: NASA/KSC.

The Five W's of Carruthers

Who

NASA, honoring scientist George R. Carruthers.

Source: NASA, Carruthers

What

A space telescope surveying Earth's exosphere in ultraviolet light.

Source: NASA, Carruthers

When

Launched September 24, 2025; operating.

Source: NASA, Carruthers

Where

About one million miles from Earth, toward the Sun.

Source: NASA, Carruthers

Why

To chart changes in the exosphere, the transition from Earth's environment to space.

Source: NASA, Carruthers

Solar Orbiter (ESA / NASA)

Agency solution · active

Impact Statement

Solar Orbiter, an international mission between ESA and NASA, launched in February 2020. It has taken the closest-ever images of the Sun and the first close-up images of the Sun's polar regions, coming within 42 million km of the Sun, and links the solar wind to where it comes from on the Sun's surface.

Source: ESA, Solar Orbiter

Source: NASA, Solar Orbiter

Tracing solar wind to its source helps forecast the space weather that reaches Earth.

For Further Information: ESA, Solar Orbiter

Image: NASA/KSC.

The Five W's of Solar Orbiter

Who

ESA and NASA.

Source: NASA, Solar Orbiter

What

A Sun-observing spacecraft with ten instruments.

Source: ESA, Solar Orbiter

When

Launched February 2020; routine science since November 2021.

Source: ESA, Solar Orbiter

Where

Orbiting the Sun, as close as 42 million km.

Source: ESA, Solar Orbiter

Why

To understand how the Sun creates and controls the solar wind and how that affects our planet.

Source: ESA, Solar Orbiter
