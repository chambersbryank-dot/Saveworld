About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a one-semester junior aircraft performance course. It is modeled on the topics and depth of the performance parts of MIT 16.01–16.04 Unified Engineering and MIT 16.885J Aircraft Systems Engineering (free on MIT OpenCourseWare), on courses such as Purdue AAE 251 and Georgia Tech AE 3310, and on the FAA Pilot’s Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge, Chapter 11, which we link where they help. None of those authors, schools or agencies endorses this page.

What we add: every unit ties to real work with a cited public source (laminar-flow wings, truss-braced wings, hybrid engines, sustainable aviation fuel and transportation energy), every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab has a free simulation, a spreadsheet or a safe home version.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Performance is where aircraft design meets fuel burn. Every percent of drag removed, every kilogram saved and every better climb or cruise profile shows up directly in the Breguet range equation, and so in fuel use and emissions.

NASA lists three main ways to make aviation sustainable: efficient aircraft technology, sustainable aviation fuel, and operations and infrastructure. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Aerodynamics I: lift and drag coefficients, the drag polar and induced drag.
  • Dynamics: Newton’s laws, work and energy.
  • Calculus II: integrals and natural logarithms (for range and endurance).
  • Thermodynamics helps with engines and the atmosphere but is not required.

How to study

  • Start every problem with a free-body diagram: lift, weight, thrust and drag, and the flight-path angle.
  • Write down the atmosphere first: altitude, density, temperature and speed of sound.
  • Keep weight in newtons and mass in kilograms; most errors in performance come from mixing them.
  • Know the key speeds by heart: stall, minimum drag, minimum power, best climb and approach.
  • Check limits: zero excess thrust means zero climb; heavier aircraft need more runway, roughly as weight squared.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer, and retake the quizzes a few days later.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer or tablet for the free NASA FoilSim and PhET Projectile Motion simulations.
  • Spreadsheet software for the atmosphere, drag-polar, range and takeoff labs.
  • Printer paper, a ruler or tape measure, paper clips and a stopwatch or phone timer.
  • A safe open indoor space or a calm outdoor space for glider flights.

Unit 1 · Week 1

The Standard Atmosphere, Airspeeds and Density Altitude

Why performance depends on the air, the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA), temperature, pressure and density with altitude, the speed of sound and Mach number, indicated, calibrated, equivalent and true airspeed, and pressure and density altitude.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute ISA temperature, pressure and density in the troposphere.
  • Find the speed of sound and Mach number.
  • Convert between equivalent and true airspeed.
  • Explain why hot, high airports hurt performance.

Key concepts

  • Lift, drag and engine thrust all depend on air density, so performance is always quoted for a stated atmosphere. The ISA is a reference: 288.15 K, 101,325 Pa and 1.225 kg/m³ at sea level.
  • In the troposphere (up to 11 km) temperature falls 6.5 K per km: T = T₀ − 0.0065h. Hydrostatics plus the ideal-gas law give p/p₀ = (T/T₀)^5.26 and ρ/ρ₀ = (T/T₀)^4.26. Above 11 km temperature is constant at 216.65 K and density falls exponentially.
  • The speed of sound is a = √(γRT); Mach number M = V/a. Because a depends only on temperature, it is lower at cruise altitude.
  • An airspeed indicator senses dynamic pressure ½ρV². Equivalent airspeed EAS = V√σ, with density ratio σ = ρ/ρ₀; true airspeed is higher than EAS at altitude.
  • Pressure altitude is the ISA altitude with the measured pressure; density altitude is the ISA altitude with the actual density. High density altitude (a high, hot or humid airfield) means thinner air, so less lift at a given speed and less engine thrust: takeoff gets longer and climb gets weaker.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 Find the ISA temperature, pressure and density at 3000 m.

Show the steps
  1. T = 288.15 − 0.0065 × 3000 = 268.65 K.
  2. p = 101,325 × (268.65/288.15)^5.26 = 70.1 kPa.
  3. ρ = 1.225 × (268.65/288.15)^4.26 = 0.909 kg/m³.

Answer: T = 268.65 K, p = 70.1 kPa, ρ = 0.909 kg/m³ (σ = 0.742).

Example 1.2 Find the speed of sound at sea level and at 11 km (216.65 K).

Show the steps
  1. a₀ = √(1.4 × 287 × 288.15).
  2. a = √(1.4 × 287 × 216.65).

Answer: a₀ = 340.3 m/s; a = 295.0 m/s.

Example 1.3 Find the ISA density and pressure at 11 km.

Show the steps
  1. T/T₀ = 216.65/288.15.
  2. ρ = 1.225 × (0.7519)^4.26; p = 101,325 × (0.7519)^5.26.

Answer: ρ = 0.364 kg/m³ and p = 22.6 kPa: less than a third of sea-level density.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: True airspeed equals indicated airspeed.

    Actually: Only at sea-level standard conditions.

  • Myth: The speed of sound depends on pressure.

    Actually: For an ideal gas it depends only on temperature.

  • Myth: Density altitude is the airport elevation.

    Actually: It also depends on temperature, pressure and humidity.

Unit 2 · Week 2

Forces in Flight and the Drag Polar

The four forces, steady level flight, lift and drag coefficients, parasite and induced drag, the parabolic drag polar C_D = C_D0 + KC_L², Oswald efficiency and aspect ratio, maximum lift-to-drag ratio, and drag in cruise.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Write the equations of steady level flight.
  • Use the parabolic drag polar.
  • Find (L/D)max and the lift coefficient where it occurs.
  • Compute cruise drag for an airliner.

Key concepts

  • In steady level flight lift equals weight and thrust equals drag: L = W = ½ρV²SC_L and T = D = ½ρV²SC_D.
  • Drag has two parts. Parasite (zero-lift) drag C_D0 comes from skin friction and pressure drag; induced drag KC_L² is the price of making lift, with K = 1/(πeAR) for aspect ratio AR and Oswald efficiency e.
  • The drag polar C_D = C_D0 + KC_L² sets almost every performance number in this course.
  • Lift-to-drag ratio L/D = C_L/C_D is the aircraft’s aerodynamic efficiency. It is largest when induced drag equals parasite drag: C_L* = √(C_D0/K) and (L/D)max = 1/(2√(KC_D0)).
  • At (L/D)max the drag is smallest for a given weight: D_min = W/(L/D)max. Long thin wings (high AR) lower K, which is why gliders and the most efficient airliners have them.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 The sample airliner has m = 60,000 kg, S = 120 m², C_D0 = 0.020 and K = 0.045. Find W, (L/D)max, C_L* and the minimum drag.

Show the steps
  1. W = 60,000 × 9.81 = 588.6 kN.
  2. (L/D)max = 1/(2√(0.045 × 0.020)) = 16.67.
  3. C_L* = √(0.020/0.045) = 0.667.
  4. D_min = 588,600/16.67.

Answer: (L/D)max = 16.67 at C_L = 0.667; D_min = 35.3 kN.

Example 2.2 The airliner cruises at 11 km (ρ = 0.364 kg/m³) at 230 m/s. Find q, C_L, C_D and drag.

Show the steps
  1. q = ½ × 0.3636 × 230² = 9618 Pa.
  2. C_L = 588,600/(9618 × 120) = 0.510.
  3. C_D = 0.020 + 0.045 × 0.510² = 0.0317.
  4. D = 9618 × 120 × 0.0317.

Answer: D = 36.6 kN; L/D = 16.1, close to the maximum.

Example 2.3 What is the cruise Mach number?

Show the steps
  1. M = 230/295.0.

Answer: M = 0.780.

  4. Find the cruise L/D above.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Drag always grows with speed.

    Actually: Induced drag falls with speed; total drag has a minimum.

  • Myth: Bigger wings always mean less drag.

    Actually: They add parasite drag.

  • Myth: L/D is fixed for an aircraft.

    Actually: It depends on C_L, so on speed, weight and altitude.

How this helps save the planet

NASA describes the truss-braced wing as a long, thin wing with aerodynamically shaped structural supports that could reduce fuel and operating costs for future airliners. In December 2025, NASA and Boeing tested a semispan model of Boeing’s Subsonic Ultra Green Aircraft Research (SUGAR) concept in a large pressurized wind tunnel in Farnborough, England.

A long, thin wing is a high-aspect-ratio wing: it lowers K in the drag polar and raises (L/D)max. The struts carry the loads that would make such a wing too heavy on its own.

Unit 3 · Week 3

Thrust and Power Required

Thrust required versus speed, the minimum-drag speed, power required and the minimum-power speed, thrust and power available from jets and propellers, maximum and minimum level speeds, and the effect of altitude and weight.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Plot thrust and power required against speed.
  • Find the minimum-drag and minimum-power speeds.
  • Explain how altitude changes these speeds.
  • Find maximum level speed from thrust available.

Key concepts

  • Thrust required equals drag: T_R = ½ρV²SC_D0 + 2KW²/(ρV²S). The first term grows with V², the second falls as 1/V², so T_R has a minimum.
  • The minimum-drag speed is V_md = √(2W/(ρS)) (K/C_D0)^¼. It is the speed for (L/D)max.
  • Power required is P_R = T_R V. It is least at V_mp = V_md/3^¼ = 0.760 V_md, where induced drag is three times parasite drag and L/D = 0.866(L/D)max.
  • At altitude, all speeds scale as 1/√σ at the same weight: the true speed for best L/D rises, but the drag is unchanged.
  • Jet thrust is roughly constant with speed and falls with density; propeller power is roughly constant, so propeller thrust falls with speed. Maximum level speed is where available thrust (or power) meets required.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 Find V_md for the sample airliner at sea level.

Show the steps
  1. √(2 × 588,600/(1.225 × 120)) = 89.5 m/s.
  2. (0.045/0.020)^¼ = 1.225.
  3. V_md = 89.5 × 1.225.

Answer: V_md = 109.6 m/s.

Example 3.2 Find V_md at 11 km and the speed ratio.

Show the steps
  1. Ratio = √(1.225/0.3636) = 1.835.
  2. V_md = 109.6 × 1.835.

Answer: V_md = 201.2 m/s at the same 35.3 kN drag.

Example 3.3 Find V_mp at sea level and the power required at V_md.

Show the steps
  1. V_mp = 109.6/1.316 = 83.3 m/s.
  2. P_R(V_md) = 35,316 × 109.6.

Answer: V_mp = 83.3 m/s; P_R at V_md = 3.87 MW.

  • Myth: Minimum drag and minimum power happen at the same speed.

    Actually: V_mp is 24% slower.

  • Myth: Flying higher lowers drag at best L/D.

    Actually: Drag is the same; only the speed is higher.

  • Myth: Thrust required is the same as power required.

    Actually: Power = thrust × speed.

How this helps save the planet

NASA explains that during flight a thin layer of air, the boundary layer, forms very near an aircraft’s surface, and that on most aircraft it becomes turbulent, increasing friction drag and fuel use. NASA’s Crossflow Attenuated Natural Laminar Flow (CATNLF) design aims to keep that flow laminar on large swept wings.

A NASA computational study from 2014 to 2017 estimated that a CATNLF wing on a large, long-range aircraft like the Boeing 777 could save up to 10% of fuel a year. In this unit’s terms, laminar flow lowers C_D0, which lowers thrust and power required at every speed.

Unit 4 · Weeks 4–5

Climb Performance

Forces in a steady climb, climb angle and rate of climb, excess thrust and excess power, best-angle and best-rate speeds, specific excess power, time to climb, and absolute and service ceilings.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Write the force balance in a steady climb.
  • Compute rate and angle of climb.
  • Distinguish best-angle and best-rate speeds.
  • Estimate time to climb and ceilings.

Key concepts

  • In a steady climb at flight-path angle γ: T − D − W sin γ = 0 and L = W cos γ. So sin γ = (T − D)/W: excess thrust sets the climb angle.
  • Rate of climb RC = V sin γ = (T − D)V/W = (P_avail − P_req)/W: excess power sets the climb rate.
  • The best-angle speed (V_x) maximizes excess thrust and is used to clear obstacles; the best-rate speed (V_y) maximizes excess power and gets to altitude soonest.
  • Specific excess power P_s = (T − D)V/W combines climb and acceleration: it is the rate at which energy height h + V²/(2g) can grow.
  • Excess power falls with altitude. The absolute ceiling is where maximum RC is zero; the service ceiling is where it is 0.5 m/s (100 ft/min).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 The sample airliner climbs at 130 m/s at sea level with 120 kN of thrust. Find C_L and drag.

Show the steps
  1. q = ½ × 1.225 × 130² = 10,351 Pa.
  2. C_L = 588,600/(10,351 × 120) = 0.474.
  3. D = 10,351 × 120 × (0.020 + 0.045 × 0.474²).

Answer: C_L = 0.474; D = 37.4 kN (taking L ≈ W).

Example 4.2 Find the rate and angle of climb.

Show the steps
  1. RC = (120,000 − 37,394) × 130/588,600.
  2. sin γ = 82,606/588,600.

Answer: RC = 18.2 m/s (3590 ft/min); γ = 8.07°.

Example 4.3 At an average 10 m/s, how long does a climb to 10,000 m take?

Show the steps
  1. t = 10,000/10 = 1000 s.

Answer: t = 16.7 min.

  4. Why does climb fall with altitude?

    Actually: It comes from excess thrust or power; in a climb L < W.

  • Myth: V_x and V_y are the same.

    Actually: V_x is slower.

  • Myth: Climb rate is independent of weight.

    Actually: RC ∝ 1/W.

How this helps save the planet

NASA’s sustainable aviation page notes that needing less energy to fly means using less fuel or, for electric aircraft, less power that must be generated elsewhere, which lowers lifecycle emissions for the whole time an aircraft is in use.

Climb is the most energy-hungry part of a short flight. Specific excess power shows why: every kilogram of weight saved and every newton of drag removed raises the climb rate and shortens the high-thrust phase.

Unit 5 · Week 6

Gliding Flight

Forces in an unpowered glide, glide angle and glide ratio, distance from a given height, sink rate and minimum sink, the effect of weight and wind, and engine-out planning.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Relate glide angle to L/D.
  • Find glide distance from a height.
  • Compute sink rate.
  • Explain the effects of weight and wind.

Key concepts

  • In a steady glide, thrust is zero: D = W sin γ and L = W cos γ, so tan γ = D/L = 1/(L/D).
  • Glide distance from height h is d = h(L/D). The flattest glide is at (L/D)max, so at V_md.
  • Sink rate V_s = V sin γ ≈ V/(L/D). Minimum sink occurs at the minimum-power speed V_mp, best for staying up longest.
  • Weight does not change the best glide ratio, only the speed at which it happens (heavier gliders fly faster at the same angle). Headwind shortens the distance over the ground; tailwind lengthens it.
  • NASA notes that the Wright brothers measured their gliders’ performance by flying parallel to a dune slope, so the glide angle equalled the slope of the hill.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 The sample airliner loses all engines at 10 km. How far can it glide at (L/D)max = 16.67 in still air?

Show the steps
  1. d = 10,000 × 16.67.

Answer: d = 166.7 km.

Example 5.2 A sailplane with L/D = 40 is 1000 m up. Find its glide distance and angle.

Show the steps
  1. d = 1000 × 40 = 40 km.
  2. γ = arctan(1/40).

Answer: 40 km at 1.43°.

Example 5.3 Find the sailplane’s sink rate at 30 m/s.

Show the steps
  1. V_s = 30/40.

Answer: 0.75 m/s.

    Actually: Same distance in still air, just faster.

  • Myth: Pull the nose up to stretch a glide.

    Actually: Below best-glide speed the glide gets steeper.

  • Myth: Minimum sink and best glide are the same speed.

    Actually: Minimum sink is slower.

How this helps save the planet

NASA’s Beginner’s Guide explains that the smaller the glide angle, the farther an aircraft can fly for a given change in altitude, so the glide angle is a measure of flying efficiency. The Wright brothers used gliders between 1900 and 1902 to learn aerodynamics and piloting before powered flight.

The glide ratio is just L/D, the same number that sets fuel burn in cruise. An aircraft that glides well also cruises on less fuel.

Unit 6 · Weeks 7–8

Range and Endurance: the Breguet Equations

Specific fuel consumption for jets and propellers, fuel weight fractions, the Breguet range and endurance equations, best-range and best-endurance speeds, cruise-climb, payload–range trade-offs and reserves.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use thrust- and power-specific fuel consumption.
  • Apply the Breguet range equation for jets and propeller aircraft.
  • Compute endurance.
  • Identify which design changes extend range.

Key concepts

  • A jet’s thrust-specific fuel consumption c is fuel weight burned per unit thrust per unit time (1/s). A piston or turboprop’s power-specific consumption is fuel per unit power per unit time.
  • Integrating dW/dt = −cT with T = W/(L/D) at constant V and L/D gives the Breguet range for jets: R = (V/c)(L/D) ln(W_i/W_f).
  • For propeller aircraft, with propeller efficiency η and power-specific consumption c_p (1/m): R = (η/c_p)(L/D) ln(W_i/W_f). Endurance for jets is E = (1/c)(L/D) ln(W_i/W_f).
  • Range grows with three groups: engine efficiency (V/c or η/c_p), aerodynamics (L/D) and structures (the weight ratio). That is why the equation guides almost every aircraft improvement.
  • Jets fly for best range a little faster than V_md (maximizing V·L/D) and for best endurance at V_md. Propeller aircraft fly for range at V_md and for endurance at V_mp.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 6.1 A jet cruises at 230 m/s with c = 0.6 per hour and L/D = 16, burning fuel equal to 25% of its starting weight (W_f/W_i = 0.75). Find the range.

Show the steps
  1. c = 0.6/3600 = 1.667 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹.
  2. ln(1/0.75) = 0.2877.
  3. R = (230/1.667 × 10⁻⁴) × 16 × 0.2877.

Answer: R = 6352 km.

Example 6.2 Find the endurance for the same aircraft.

Show the steps
  1. E = (1/1.667 × 10⁻⁴) × 16 × 0.2877 = 27,618 s.

Answer: E = 7.67 h.

Example 6.3 A turboprop has η = 0.8, SFC = 0.3 kg/kWh, L/D = 15 and burns 15% of its weight (W_f/W_i = 0.85). Find the range.

Show the steps
  1. c_p = 0.3 × 9.81/3.6 × 10⁶ = 8.175 × 10⁻⁷ m⁻¹.
  2. ln(1/0.85) = 0.1625.
  3. R = (0.8/8.175 × 10⁻⁷) × 15 × 0.1625.

Answer: R = 2386 km.

  • Myth: Range is proportional to fuel carried.

    Actually: It grows with the log of the weight ratio.

  • Myth: Best range and best endurance are the same speed.

    Actually: Endurance is flown slower.

  • Myth: Breguet needs constant altitude.

    Actually: It assumes constant V and L/D, which means a slow cruise-climb.

How this helps save the planet

Unit 7 · Weeks 9–10

Takeoff Performance

Stall speed and C_Lmax, high-lift devices, the takeoff ground roll, liftoff speed, the effect of weight, density altitude, wind and runway slope, the airborne segment over an obstacle, and balanced field length.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Find stall and liftoff speeds.
  • Estimate takeoff ground roll.
  • Show how weight and density altitude lengthen takeoff.
  • Explain balanced field length.

Key concepts

  • Stall speed is the slowest steady flight speed: V_s = √(2W/(ρSC_Lmax)). Flaps and slats raise C_Lmax for takeoff and landing.
  • Liftoff speed is typically V_LOF ≈ 1.2V_s, giving a margin above stall.
  • With thrust much larger than drag and friction, the ground roll is about s_g ≈ 1.44W²/(gρSC_LmaxT).
  • Takeoff distance therefore grows roughly with W², and with 1/ρ twice over for jets (through V_s and through lower thrust). A 10 kt headwind shortens the roll; a tailwind lengthens it a lot.
  • Balanced field length is where, if an engine fails at the decision speed V₁, stopping and continuing need the same distance.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 7.1 The sample airliner has C_Lmax = 2.4 with takeoff flaps at sea level. Find V_s and V_LOF.

Show the steps
  1. V_s = √(2 × 588,600/(1.225 × 120 × 2.4)).
  2. V_LOF = 1.2 × 57.8.

Answer: V_s = 57.8 m/s; V_LOF = 69.3 m/s.

Example 7.2 Estimate the ground roll with 180 kN thrust.

Show the steps
  1. s_g = 1.44 × 588,600²/(9.81 × 1.225 × 120 × 2.4 × 180,000).

Answer: s_g = 801 m.

Example 7.3 Repeat at a 1500 m airport (ISA ρ = 1.058 kg/m³), same thrust.

Show the steps
  1. s_g = 801 × 1.225/1.058.

Answer: s_g = 927 m, 1.158 times longer (more with the real thrust loss).

  • Myth: Liftoff happens at stall speed.

Unit 8 · Week 11

Landing Performance

The approach, flare and ground roll, approach speed, braking, reverse thrust and spoilers, runway condition, landing distance estimates and the energy that must be removed.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Find approach speed from stall speed.
  • Estimate landing ground roll from deceleration.
  • Explain how runway condition and spoilers matter.
  • Compute the kinetic energy at touchdown.

Key concepts

  • Landing has three segments: the approach over a 15 m (50 ft) obstacle, the flare and the ground roll.
  • Approach speed is typically V_app ≈ 1.3V_s in the landing configuration.
  • With an average deceleration a, the ground roll is s = V²/(2a). Brakes, spoilers (which dump lift so the wheels can brake) and reverse thrust all raise a.
  • Wet or icy runways lower friction and can greatly lengthen the roll; performance charts give corrections.
  • The kinetic energy at touchdown ½mV² must be absorbed, mostly as heat in the brakes.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 8.1 With landing flaps, C_Lmax = 2.8. Find V_s and V_app for the sample airliner at sea level.

Show the steps
  1. V_s = √(2 × 588,600/(1.225 × 120 × 2.8)) = 53.5 m/s.
  2. V_app = 1.3 × 53.5.

Answer: V_s = 53.5 m/s; V_app = 69.5 m/s.

Example 8.2 Estimate the ground roll from 69.5 m/s at an average 0.4g.

Show the steps
  1. s = 69.5²/(2 × 0.4 × 9.81).

Answer: s = 616 m.

Example 8.3 Find the kinetic energy at 69.5 m/s.

Show the steps
  1. E = ½ × 60,000 × 69.5².

Answer: E = 145 MJ.

Unit 9 · Weeks 12–13

Turning Flight and the V-n Diagram

Level coordinated turns, bank angle and load factor, turn radius and rate, stall speed in a turn, maneuvering speed, the V-n diagram with limit load factors and gust loads, and pull-ups.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Relate bank angle to load factor.
  • Compute turn radius and rate.
  • Find stall speed in a turn and maneuvering speed.
  • Read a V-n diagram.

Key concepts

  • In a level coordinated turn, lift tilts with the bank angle φ: L cos φ = W and L sin φ = mV²/R. The load factor n = L/W = 1/cos φ.
  • Turn radius R = V²/(g√(n² − 1)) = V²/(g tan φ); turn rate ω = V/R.
  • Stall speed rises with load factor: V_s,turn = V_s√n. At 60° bank, n = 2 and stall speed is 41% higher.
  • Maneuvering speed V_A = V_s√n_limit is the speed at which full control deflection stalls the wing before the limit load is reached.
  • The V-n diagram plots allowed load factor against speed: stall curves bound the left, limit load factors the top and bottom, and dive speed the right. Gust lines add the loads from turbulence.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 9.1 Find n, turn radius and turn rate at 100 m/s for 30°, 45° and 60° bank.

Show the steps
  1. n = 1/cos φ = 1.155, 1.414, 2.00.
  2. R = 100²/(9.81 tan φ) = 1766 m, 1019 m, 589 m.
  3. ω = V/R.

Answer: Turn rates 3.25°/s, 5.62°/s and 9.74°/s.

Example 9.2 How much does stall speed rise at 30°, 45° and 60° bank?

Show the steps
  1. √n = 1.075, 1.189, 1.414.

Answer: 7.5%, 18.9% and 41.4%.

Example 9.3 A light aircraft has V_s = 50 m/s and n_limit = 2.5. Find V_A.

Show the steps
  1. V_A = 50 × √2.5.

Answer: V_A = 79.1 m/s.

Unit 10 · Weeks 14–15

Performance of Electric, Hybrid and Sustainable-Fuel Aircraft

Range of battery-electric aircraft, why weight does not fall as batteries discharge, specific energy of batteries versus jet fuel, hybrid-electric engines, sustainable aviation fuel, CO₂ from fuel burn, and putting all the performance levers together.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Derive the range equation for a battery-electric aircraft.
  • Compare the specific energy of batteries and jet fuel.
  • Compute CO₂ from fuel burned.
  • Rank technologies by which Breguet term they improve.

Key concepts

  • A battery-electric aircraft does not get lighter as it flies, so there is no logarithm: R = (e_b η/g)(L/D)(m_b/m), with battery specific energy e_b (J/kg), total efficiency η and battery mass fraction m_b/m.
  • Jet fuel holds about 43 MJ/kg (about 11,900 Wh/kg, a textbook value). Batteries of about 250 Wh/kg (a sample value) hold roughly 48 times less per kilogram, which is why electric aircraft today are small and short-range.
  • Hybrid-electric engines use electric motors to help a jet engine run more efficiently, lowering the specific fuel consumption c.
  • Burning a kilogram of jet fuel makes about 3.16 kg of CO₂ (a textbook value). Fuel saved by better L/D, lighter structures or better engines cuts CO₂ in direct proportion.
  • Sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) has properties similar to conventional jet fuel, so it does not change the performance equations; it changes where the carbon comes from.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 10.1 An electric aircraft has 250 Wh/kg batteries (sample value), η = 0.8, L/D = 15 and a 30% battery fraction. Find its range.

Show the steps
  1. e_b = 250 × 3600 = 900,000 J/kg.
  2. R = (900,000 × 0.8/9.81) × 15 × 0.3.

Answer: R = 330 km.

Example 10.2 How much more energy per kilogram does jet fuel hold?

Show the steps
  1. 43 × 10⁶/3600 = 11,944 Wh/kg.
  2. 11,944/250.

Answer: About 47.8 times.

Example 10.3 How much CO₂ comes from burning 1000 kg of jet fuel?

Show the steps
  1. 3.16 × 1000.

Answer: 3160 kg.

How this helps save the planet

DOE says sustainable aviation fuel made from biomass and waste resources can deliver performance similar to petroleum jet fuel. DOE’s 2023 Billion-Ton Report concluded that the United States could triple its biomass production to more than 1 billion tons a year, producing an estimated 60 billion gallons of liquid biofuels, enough feedstock to meet the projected fuel demand of U.S. aviation.

NASA notes that SAF does not release new CO₂ into the environment and has been shown to reduce contrails that can trap heat in Earth’s atmosphere. NASA also estimates that with today’s fleet and efficiency, worldwide air traffic in 2050 would need more than 620 megatons of fuel and produce close to 2,000 megatons of CO₂; the United States has committed to net-zero greenhouse gas emissions from U.S. aviation by 2050.

Lab A: Build a standard-atmosphere calculator

Week 1

Goal: Build a spreadsheet of ISA temperature, density and speed of sound from sea level to 11 km.

Procedure

  1. Make a column of altitudes every 500 m from 0 to 11,000 m.

Lab B: Paper glider glide ratio

Week 6

Goal: Measure the glide ratio of a paper glider from its launch height and flight distance, and find its glide angle and sink rate.

Procedure

  1. Fold a simple glider from printer paper; add a paper clip at the nose to trim it.
  2. In a clear space, release it gently (no throw) from a measured height, about 1.5 m.
  3. Measure where it lands and time the flight; repeat five times and average.
  4. Move the paper clip and see how the glide ratio changes.

Lab C: Measuring a drag polar in FoilSim

Week 2

Goal: Collect lift and drag coefficients at several angles of attack, fit a parabolic drag polar and find (L/D)max.

Procedure

  1. Open NASA FoilSim and choose a wing shape and speed.
  2. Record C_L and C_D at five angles of attack.
  3. Plot C_D against C_L² and fit a straight line: the intercept is C_D0 and the slope is K.
  4. Compute (L/D)max = 1/(2√(KC_D0)).

Glossary

Absolute ceiling
Altitude where the maximum rate of climb is zero.
Balanced field length
Runway length where stopping and continuing after an engine failure need the same distance.
Breguet equation
Range or endurance from engine efficiency, L/D and weight ratio.
Density altitude
ISA altitude with the same air density as the actual air.
Drag polar
Relation between drag and lift coefficients.
Equivalent airspeed
True airspeed times √σ.
Glide ratio
Horizontal distance per unit of height lost; equal to L/D.
Induced drag
Drag caused by producing lift.
Load factor
Lift divided by weight.
Maneuvering speed
Speed at which full control input stalls the wing before limit load.
Parasite drag
Drag not due to lift.
Rate of climb
Vertical speed in a climb.
Service ceiling
Altitude where maximum climb rate is 0.5 m/s.
Specific excess power
Excess power per unit weight.
Specific fuel consumption
Fuel used per unit thrust or power per unit time.
Stall speed
Slowest steady flight speed, at C_Lmax.
Standard atmosphere
Reference model of temperature, pressure and density with altitude.
Sustainable aviation fuel
Jet fuel made from biomass and wastes.
V-n diagram
Allowed load factor against airspeed.
Wing loading
Weight divided by wing area.
