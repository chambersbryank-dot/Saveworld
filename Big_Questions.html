People ask hard questions about Earth. We answer with NASA, NOAA, IPCC and ESA sources — hopeful where the evidence is, honest where the work is not done.

Is our planet dying?

Earth is not “dying” as a planet — it will keep orbiting the Sun — but it is warming fast because of human greenhouse-gas emissions, and living systems are already under stress. NASA says the evidence that Earth is warming at an unprecedented rate, with human activity as the principal cause, is unequivocal. The IPCC reports substantial damages and increasingly irreversible losses in ecosystems, while also stressing that deep, rapid emissions cuts can still limit further harm.

Sources

  • NASA, Evidence
  • IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report SPM

Will Earth really be gone in the next ten years?

No. Earth will not be “gone” in ten years. Talk of a ten-year clock usually means a window to cut emissions enough to keep warming closer to 1.5°C — not that the planet vanishes. NASA is clear that warming and its effects are already here, and that future risk depends strongly on how much we emit next. The IPCC says some changes are unavoidable or irreversible over long timescales, but deep, rapid, sustained cuts can still limit further sea-level rise and the chance of abrupt, high-impact changes.

Sources

  • NASA, Is it too late?
  • IPCC AR6 SYR SPM B.3

Is it too late to stop climate change?

It is not too late to limit how bad things get. NASA explains that if greenhouse-gas emissions stopped today, the rise in global temperatures would begin to flatten within a few years, though temperatures would stay elevated for many centuries. Responding means both mitigation (cutting emissions) and adaptation (living with change already locked in). Every bit of avoided future warming reduces damage that would otherwise last essentially forever.

Sources

  • NASA FAQ: Is it too late?

Are humans causing climate change?

Yes. NASA states that human activity is the principal cause of the current warming, which is happening at a rate not seen in the past 10,000 years. The IPCC likewise treats human influence on the climate system as established fact. Natural factors such as the Sun and volcanoes matter for climate history, but they do not explain the warming trend of recent decades.

Sources

  • NASA, Evidence
  • NASA FAQ: Is the Sun causing global warming?

How can I help save the planet?

Start with actions that compound: cut energy waste at home, choose cleaner travel when you can, eat more plants and waste less food, buy less and repair more, and support groups that already remove plastic, restore forests or protect wildlife. The United Nations ActNow campaign lists practical steps anyone can take. On Saving Planets, Ways to Help links you straight to donate and join pages for the organizations doing the work — we do not take a cut.

Is sea level rising, and how fast?

Yes. Sea level is rising as oceans warm and land ice melts. The IPCC says sea-level rise is unavoidable for centuries to millennia, but deep, rapid emissions cuts would limit further acceleration. Relative to 1995–2014, likely global mean sea-level rise by 2100 ranges from about 0.28–0.55 m in a very low emissions pathway to about 0.63–1.01 m in a very high emissions pathway (medium confidence).

Sources

  • IPCC AR6 SYR SPM B.3.1

Is the ozone hole fixed?

It is healing, not finished. NOAA and NASA ranked the 2025 Antarctic ozone hole the fifth smallest since 1992, when the Montreal Protocol began to take effect, and the ozone layer remains on track to recover fully later this century. Recovery is slow because banned chemicals still leak from old products; scientists expect the Antarctic hole to recover around the late 2060s.

Can we stop an asteroid from hitting Earth?

We have already moved one. NASA’s DART spacecraft hit the asteroid moonlet Dimorphos in 2022 and shortened its orbit by 33 minutes and 15 seconds. DART was a test on a small asteroid that posed no threat. Finding asteroids early gives time to act, which is why NASA is building NEO Surveyor.

Where does most of Earth’s oxygen come from?

About half of Earth’s oxygen comes from the ocean, mostly from tiny drifting plankton, according to NOAA. Ocean life also uses about as much oxygen as the ocean produces, so healthy forests and a healthy ocean both matter for the air we breathe.

Is space junk a real problem?

Yes, and agencies are acting. ESA guided its Aeolus satellite down in 2023 in a first-of-its-kind assisted reentry, and ESA’s Zero Debris approach aims to sharply limit new debris from its missions by 2030. ESA’s 2025 report still says better rule-following is not yet enough to stop debris from increasing, so removal missions are the next step.

Sources

  • ESA Zero Debris

How do satellites help protect Earth?

Earth-observing satellites track forests, crops, ice, oceans, air quality and disasters so people can act earlier. Missions such as Landsat (NASA/USGS) and Copernicus Sentinels (ESA) deliver free public data used for deforestation alerts, flood maps, crop health and climate services. Saving Planets’ Earth Observation hub explains these missions in plain language and links each one to the problems it helps solve.

Sources

  • NASA Landsat 9
  • ESA Copernicus

What is the difference between climate change and global warming?

NASA puts it simply: “global warming” is the long-term heating of the planet; “climate change” includes that warming plus broader shifts such as rising seas, shrinking glaciers, earlier ice melt and changes in when plants bloom.
