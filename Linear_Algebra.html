About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a one-semester sophomore linear algebra course. It is modeled on the topics and depth of MIT 18.06 Linear Algebra taught by Gilbert Strang (free on MIT OpenCourseWare), and on the Georgia Tech and Purdue sophomore linear algebra courses, using the free Georgia Tech text Interactive Linear Algebra by Dan Margalit and Joseph Rabinoff and Jim Hefferon’s free Linear Algebra, which we link where it helps. None of those authors or schools endorses this page.

What we add: every unit ties to real planet work with a cited public source (GPS, Landsat 9, NASA sea-level satellites, NOAA weather supercomputers, EPA input–output and recycling data, USGS building studies, NASA’s EMIT mineral spectrometer), every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab runs in a free spreadsheet or on paper.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Linear algebra is the language of data about the planet. A satellite pixel is a vector, a GPS fix is the solution of a linear system, a weather forecast is millions of equations solved again and again, and an economy’s pollution can be traced with one matrix.

The same few ideas, elimination, projection, eigenvalues and the SVD, turn raw measurements into maps of forests, sea-level trends and mineral dust. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • High-school algebra: solving two equations in two unknowns, and working with functions.
  • Calculus I helps with limits and derivatives in a few examples, but no calculus is needed for most of the course.
  • Comfort with a spreadsheet; Intro to Programming helps for the optional Python versions of the labs.
  • Calculus III and Differential Equations pair well with this course but are not required.

How to study

  • Do every computation by hand first on small (2 × 2 and 3 × 3) examples, then check it with a spreadsheet or calculator.
  • Think of Ax two ways: as dot products of the rows with x, and as a combination of the columns of A.
  • Check every answer: plug x back into Ax = b, multiply A by its inverse, or test Av = λv.
  • Keep track of sizes: an m × n matrix times an n × p matrix gives m × p, and sizes that do not match mean an error.
  • Draw pictures in 2D and 3D: lines, planes, projections and stretches make the rules easy to remember.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer, and retake the quizzes a few days later.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer with free spreadsheet software (its MMULT, MINVERSE, MDETERM and LINEST functions do matrix work).
  • Graph paper, a ruler, a protractor and a pencil.
  • Optional: free Python with NumPy for the larger versions of the labs.
  • A string and push pins or tape for the trilateration lab, and a printed map or sheet of paper.
  • A grayscale photo or a small grid of numbers for the image-compression lab.

Unit 1 · Weeks 1–2

Vectors: Linear Combinations, Lengths and Dot Products

Vectors in two, three and n dimensions, adding and scaling them, linear combinations and span, length, unit vectors, the dot product, the angle between vectors, orthogonality and the Cauchy–Schwarz and triangle inequalities.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Add, scale and combine vectors by hand and in pictures.
  • Compute lengths, unit vectors and dot products.
  • Find the angle between two vectors and test for orthogonality.
  • Describe the span of one, two or three vectors.

Key concepts

  • A vector in ℝⁿ is an ordered list of n numbers. In 2D and 3D it is an arrow; in higher dimensions it is a list, such as the 11 band values of one satellite pixel.
  • A linear combination c₁v₁ + c₂v₂ + … scales vectors and adds them. The span is the set of all linear combinations: a line, a plane or all of space.
  • Length ‖v‖ = √(v·v). Dividing by the length gives a unit vector u = v/‖v‖ with length 1.
  • Dot product v·w = v₁w₁ + v₂w₂ + … = ‖v‖‖w‖cos θ. If v·w = 0 the vectors are orthogonal (perpendicular).
  • Cauchy–Schwarz: |v·w| ≤ ‖v‖‖w‖. Triangle inequality: ‖v + w‖ ≤ ‖v‖ + ‖w‖.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 Find the length of v = (3, 4) and the unit vector in its direction.

Show the steps
  1. ‖v‖ = √(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5.
  2. u = v/5.

Answer: ‖v‖ = 5, u = (0.6, 0.8).

Example 1.2 Find the angle between v = (3, 4) and w = (1, 2).

Show the steps
  1. v·w = 3 + 8 = 11.
  2. ‖v‖ = 5, ‖w‖ = √5 = 2.236.
  3. cos θ = 11/(5 × 2.236) = 0.9839.

Answer: θ = 10.30°.

Example 1.3 Write 2(1, 0, 1) + 3(0, 1, 1) as one vector. Is (2, 3, 4) in the span of these two vectors?

Show the steps
  1. 2(1, 0, 1) + 3(0, 1, 1) = (2, 3, 5).
  2. Any combination c₁(1, 0, 1) + c₂(0, 1, 1) = (c₁, c₂, c₁ + c₂), so the third entry must equal the sum of the first two.

Answer: (2, 3, 5); (2, 3, 4) is not in the span because 2 + 3 ≠ 4.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Find ‖(1, 2, 2)‖.

    Show answer

    √(1 + 4 + 4) = 3.

  2. Find (1, 2, 3)·(4, −5, 6).

    Show answer

    4 − 10 + 18 = 12.

  3. Are (2, −1) and (1, 2) orthogonal?

    Show answer

    Yes: 2 − 2 = 0.

  4. Find the unit vector in the direction of (6, 8).

    Show answer

    ‖(6, 8)‖ = 10, so (0.6, 0.8).

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: The dot product is a vector.

    Actually: It is a single number (a scalar).

  • Myth: ‖v + w‖ = ‖v‖ + ‖w‖ always.

    Actually: Only when v and w point the same way; otherwise it is smaller.

  • Myth: Vectors only make sense in 2D or 3D.

    Actually: Data vectors can have hundreds of entries; the same rules apply.

Flashcards

    c₁v₁ + c₂v₂ + …

  • Span

  • Length

    ‖v‖ = √(v·v).

  • Dot product

    v·w = Σ vᵢwᵢ = ‖v‖‖w‖cos θ.

  • Orthogonal

    v·w = 0.

  • Unit vector

    v/‖v‖.

How this helps save the planet

Every Landsat 9 pixel is a vector. According to USGS, Landsat 9’s Operational Land Imager 2 records 9 spectral bands at 30 m (15 m for the panchromatic band) and its Thermal Infrared Sensor 2 records 2 thermal bands at 100 m, so each spot on the ground becomes a list of 11 numbers. The data are 14-bit, 2¹⁴ = 16,384 shades per band, and Landsat 9 collects about 750 scenes a day, all free.

Forest and crop health is read from the red band (band 4, 0.64–0.67 µm) and the near-infrared band (band 5, 0.85–0.88 µm). The vegetation index NDVI = (NIR − red)/(NIR + red) compares two entries of the pixel vector. With sample reflectances of 0.40 (NIR) and 0.08 (red), NDVI = 0.32/0.48 = 0.667, typical of healthy vegetation; bare soil gives values near zero.

Unit 2 · Weeks 2–3

Systems of Linear Equations and Gaussian Elimination

Linear systems in row and column pictures, augmented matrices, elimination to echelon form, back substitution, reduced row echelon form, pivots and free variables, and when a system has one, none or infinitely many solutions.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Write a system as Ax = b and as an augmented matrix.
  • Solve systems by elimination and back substitution.
  • Find the reduced row echelon form and identify pivots and free variables.
  • Decide whether a system has one, no or infinitely many solutions.

Key concepts

  • Row picture: each equation is a line (2D) or plane (3D) and the solution is where they meet. Column picture: b is a combination of the columns of A, with weights x.
  • Three row operations do not change the solutions: swap rows, multiply a row by a nonzero number, and add a multiple of one row to another.
  • Elimination produces an upper-triangular (echelon) form; back substitution then solves from the bottom up. Pivots are the first nonzero entries of each row.
  • A column without a pivot gives a free variable and infinitely many solutions. A row 0 = c with c ≠ 0 means no solution (inconsistent).
  • Squared-distance equations like (x − a)² + (y − b)² = d² are not linear, but subtracting one from another cancels x² and y² and leaves linear equations. That is how positioning problems become linear systems.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 Solve x + y + z = 6, 2x + y − z = 1, x − y + 2z = 5.

Show the steps
  1. R₂ − 2R₁: −y − 3z = −11. R₃ − R₁: −2y + z = −1.
  2. R₃ − 2R₂: 7z = 21, so z = 3.
  3. Back substitute: −y − 9 = −11 gives y = 2; then x = 6 − 2 − 3 = 1.

Answer: (x, y, z) = (1, 2, 3).

Example 2.2 Beacons sit at (0, 0), (10, 0) and (0, 10) km. A receiver measures squared distances 25, 65 and 45 km². Find its position.

Show the steps
  1. Equation 1 minus equation 2: x² + y² − [(x − 10)² + y²] = 25 − 65, so 20x − 100 = −40.
  2. Equation 1 minus equation 3: 20y − 100 = −20.
  3. Solve the two linear equations.

Answer: (x, y) = (3, 4) km.

Example 2.3 How many solutions do x + 2y = 3, 2x + 4y = 6 have? And x + 2y = 3, 2x + 4y = 7?

Show the steps
  1. R₂ − 2R₁ gives 0 = 0 in the first system: one pivot, one free variable.
  2. In the second, R₂ − 2R₁ gives 0 = 1.

Answer: Infinitely many; none.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Solve x + y = 5, x − y = 1.

    Show answer

    Adding gives 2x = 6: x = 3, y = 2.

  2. A GPS-style signal takes 0.00505783 s to arrive. How far did it travel at 299,792,458 m/s?

    Show answer

    d = ct = 1,516,299 m, about 1,516 km.

  3. Find the reduced row echelon form of [[1, 2, 3], [2, 5, 8]] (augmented).

    Show answer

    R₂ − 2R₁ = [0, 1, 2]; R₁ − 2R₂ = [1, 0, −1]: x = −1, y = 2.

  4. A 3 × 5 system has 3 pivots. How many free variables?

    Show answer

    5 − 3 = 2.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Multiplying a row by 0 is a legal row operation.

    Actually: It destroys information; the multiplier must be nonzero.

  • Myth: A system with more unknowns than equations has no solution.

    Actually: If consistent, it has infinitely many; it can also be inconsistent.

  • Myth: A zero row means no solution.

    Actually: Only a row 0 = c with c ≠ 0 does; 0 = 0 just means a free variable.

Flashcards

  • Augmented matrix

    [A | b].

  • Pivot

    First nonzero entry in a row of echelon form.

  • Free variable

    Variable whose column has no pivot.

  • RREF

    Pivots 1, zeros above and below.

  • Inconsistent

    A row 0 = c, c ≠ 0.

  • Back substitution

    Solve the triangular system from the bottom up.

How this helps save the planet

GPS is a linear algebra machine. GPS.gov reports that at least 24 operational satellites are kept available 95% of the time and that 31 operational satellites have been flown for more than a decade, at about 20,200 km altitude, each circling Earth twice a day. They sit in six equally spaced orbital planes so that users can view at least four satellites from virtually any point on the planet.

GPS.gov’s trilateration activity explains the idea: distance = rate × time, with the rate equal to the speed of light, 299,792,458 m/s, and distances from at least 3 satellites fix a 3D position. A real receiver’s clock is also unknown, so in the standard textbook model the unknowns are x, y, z and the clock offset, four unknowns that need four satellites; differencing the range equations, as in the worked example, turns them into a linear system. A clock error of just one microsecond would put a range off by 299.8 m. Precise positions guide farm tractors that apply less fertilizer, ships on efficient routes and the survey stations that measure sea level and ice.

Unit 3 · Week 4

Matrix Algebra: Multiplication, Inverses and LU Factorization

Matrix–vector and matrix–matrix products, the rules (and non-rules) of matrix algebra, transposes, elimination matrices, the inverse and Gauss–Jordan, the 2 × 2 inverse formula, A = LU, and the cost of elimination.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Multiply matrices and check that sizes match.
  • Find inverses by the 2 × 2 formula and by Gauss–Jordan.
  • Factor A = LU and use it to solve systems.
  • Estimate the cost of elimination as n grows.

Key concepts

  • (AB)ᵢⱼ = row i of A dotted with column j of B. An m × n times an n × p matrix is m × p. In general AB ≠ BA.
  • The inverse A⁻¹ satisfies AA⁻¹ = A⁻¹A = I. It exists exactly when A has n pivots (det A ≠ 0). (AB)⁻¹ = B⁻¹A⁻¹ and (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ.
  • Gauss–Jordan: row-reduce [A | I] to [I | A⁻¹].
  • Elimination without row swaps records its multipliers in a lower-triangular L with ones on the diagonal, giving A = LU. Solve Lc = b, then Ux = c; with L and U saved, each new right side is cheap.
  • Elimination on an n × n matrix costs about n³/3 multiply–add operations; solving with saved L and U costs about n² per right side.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 Find AB and BA for A = [[1, 2], [3, 4]] and B = [[0, 1], [1, 0]].

Show the steps
  1. AB: row 1 · columns = (2, 1); row 2 = (4, 3).
  2. BA swaps the rows of A: [[3, 4], [1, 2]].

Answer: AB = [[2, 1], [4, 3]], BA = [[3, 4], [1, 2]], so AB ≠ BA.

Example 3.2 Invert A = [[4, 7], [2, 6]].

Show the steps
  1. ad − bc = 24 − 14 = 10.
  2. A⁻¹ = (1/10)[[6, −7], [−2, 4]].

Answer: A⁻¹ = [[0.6, −0.7], [−0.2, 0.4]].

Example 3.3 Factor A = [[2, 1], [6, 8]] as LU.

Show the steps
  1. Multiplier ℓ₂₁ = 6/2 = 3.
  2. R₂ − 3R₁ = [0, 5].

Answer: L = [[1, 0], [3, 1]], U = [[2, 1], [0, 5]].

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. What size is a 3 × 2 matrix times a 2 × 4 matrix?

    Show answer

    3 × 4.

  2. Invert [[2, 1], [5, 3]].

    Show answer

    det = 1, inverse [[3, −1], [−5, 2]].

  3. About how many operations does elimination take for n = 1000?

    Show answer

    n³/3 ≈ 3.33 × 10⁸.

  4. If A and B are invertible, what is (AB)⁻¹?

    Show answer

    B⁻¹A⁻¹.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: AB = BA.

    Actually: Matrix multiplication is usually not commutative.

  • Myth: (AB)⁻¹ = A⁻¹B⁻¹.

    Actually: The order reverses: B⁻¹A⁻¹.

  • Myth: Computers solve Ax = b by computing A⁻¹.

    Actually: They factor A = LU (or similar); forming A⁻¹ is slower and less accurate.

Flashcards

  • Identity

    AI = IA = A.

  • Inverse

    AA⁻¹ = I.

  • 2 × 2 inverse

    Swap a, d; negate b, c; divide by ad − bc.

  • Transpose rule

    (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ.

  • LU

    A = LU from elimination.

  • Cost

    About n³/3 for elimination.

How this helps save the planet

Weather and climate forecasts are, at their core, enormous linear-algebra jobs repeated many times a day. NOAA reported in August 2023 that its twin operational supercomputers, Dogwood in Manassas, Virginia and Cactus in Phoenix, Arizona, were upgraded by 20% to 14.5 petaflops each, so that together the forecast system can process 29 quadrillion calculations per second, and that with its research supercomputers its total capacity is 49 petaflops.

The n³/3 rule shows why speed matters. As a sample, a dense system with n = 100,000 unknowns needs about 3.33 × 10¹⁴ operations; at a sustained 14.5 × 10¹⁵ operations per second that is about 0.023 s, but doubling n multiplies the work by 8. Real models use sparse matrices and clever solvers, yet the same cubic growth is what NOAA says the extra power will pay for: higher-resolution models that improve forecasts and warnings.

Unit 4 · Week 5

Linear Models: Input–Output (Leontief) Economics, Networks and Balancing

Building linear models from real systems: the Leontief input–output model x = Ax + d and its solution x = (I − A)⁻¹d, environmentally extended models that turn spending into emissions, network flows and balancing chemical equations.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Set up and solve a Leontief input–output model.
  • Interpret the entries of (I − A)⁻¹.
  • Extend an input–output model with emission intensities to find a footprint.
  • Write flow-balance and chemical-balance problems as linear systems.

Key concepts

  • In an input–output model, column j of the consumption matrix A lists what sector j buys from each sector to make one dollar of output.
  • Total output x must cover what industries use (Ax) plus final demand d: x = Ax + d, so (I − A)x = d and x = (I − A)⁻¹d.
  • Entry (i, j) of the Leontief inverse (I − A)⁻¹ is the total output of sector i needed, directly and through all supply chains, for one dollar of final demand from sector j.
  • Environmentally extended models multiply output by a row of emission intensities e (for example kg CO₂ per dollar): footprint = e·x.
  • Conservation laws give linear equations too: flow in equals flow out at every node of a network, and atoms of each element balance in a chemical reaction.

Vector Spaces, Subspaces, Independence, Basis, Rank and the Four Fundamental Subspaces

Vector spaces and subspaces, the column space and null space, solving Ax = 0 and Ax = b completely, linear independence, basis and dimension, rank, the rank–nullity theorem and Strang’s four fundamental subspaces.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Test whether a set is a subspace.
  • Find bases for the column space and null space of a matrix.
  • Test vectors for linear independence.
  • Use rank–nullity and the dimensions of the four fundamental subspaces.

Key concepts

  • A subspace contains the zero vector and is closed under addition and scalar multiplication. Lines and planes through the origin are subspaces; ones that miss the origin are not.
  • Column space C(A): all Ax, the set of b for which Ax = b is solvable. Null space N(A): all x with Ax = 0.
  • Vectors are linearly independent if the only combination giving zero is the all-zero one. A basis is an independent spanning set; every basis of a space has the same number of vectors, its dimension.
  • Rank r = number of pivots = dim C(A). Rank–nullity: r + dim N(A) = n, the number of columns.
  • Four fundamental subspaces of an m × n matrix of rank r: C(A) (dim r) and N(Aᵀ) (dim m − r) in ℝᵐ; C(Aᵀ) (dim r) and N(A) (dim n − r) in ℝⁿ. N(A) is orthogonal to the row space.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 Find the rank and a null-space basis of A = [[1, 2, 3], [2, 4, 6], [1, 0, 1]].

Show the steps
  1. R₂ − 2R₁ = 0; R₃ − R₁ = [0, −2, −2]. Two pivots, so r = 2.
  2. Ax = 0: x + z = 0 and −2y − 2z = 0, with z free.
  3. Take z = 1: x = −1, y = −1.

Answer: r = 2, N(A) spanned by (−1, −1, 1); dim N(A) = 3 − 2 = 1.

Example 5.2 Are (1, 2, 3), (4, 5, 6), (7, 8, 9) independent?

Show the steps
  1. Row-reduce the matrix with these columns: R₂ − 2R₁ and R₃ − 3R₁ give rows [0, −3, −6] and [0, −6, −12], which are multiples.
  2. Only two pivots.

Answer: No: (7, 8, 9) = 2(4, 5, 6) − (1, 2, 3).

Example 5.3 A 5 × 8 matrix has rank 3. Give the dimensions of its four fundamental subspaces.

Show the steps
  1. C(A) and C(Aᵀ): r = 3.
  2. N(A): n − r = 8 − 3; N(Aᵀ): m − r = 5 − 3.

Answer: 3, 3, 5 and 2.

Unit 6 · Weeks 8–9

Orthogonality, Projections, Least Squares and Gram–Schmidt

Orthogonal vectors and subspaces, projection onto a line and onto a subspace, the normal equations AᵀAx̂ = Aᵀb, fitting lines and curves to data by least squares, orthonormal bases, Gram–Schmidt and A = QR.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Project a vector onto a line and onto a column space.
  • Fit a line to data with the normal equations.
  • Build an orthonormal basis with Gram–Schmidt.
  • Explain why least squares is a projection.

Key concepts

  • The projection of b onto the line through a is p = (a·b/a·a) a; the error e = b − p is orthogonal to a.
  • When Ax = b has no solution (more equations than unknowns, noisy data), choose x̂ to make ‖b − Ax̂‖ as small as possible. The error must be orthogonal to C(A), giving the normal equations AᵀAx̂ = Aᵀb.
  • For a line y = C + Dt through points (tᵢ, yᵢ), the columns of A are all ones and the tᵢ values.
  • Orthonormal vectors qᵢ satisfy qᵢ·qⱼ = 0 (i ≠ j) and ‖qᵢ‖ = 1. With Q having orthonormal columns, QᵀQ = I and projections become p = QQᵀb.
  • Gram–Schmidt subtracts from each new vector its projections on the earlier ones, then normalizes. The result is A = QR with R upper triangular.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

  2. p = 2.5(1, 1); e = b − p = (−0.5, 0.5), and e·a = 0.

Answer: p = (2.5, 2.5).

Example 6.2 Fit y = C + Dt to (0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 4) by least squares.

Show the steps

  2. det = 6; C = (5 × 7 − 3 × 10)/6, D = (3 × 10 − 3 × 7)/6.

Answer: C = 0.833, D = 1.5: y = 0.833 + 1.5t.

Example 6.3 Apply Gram–Schmidt to a₁ = (1, 1, 0), a₂ = (1, 0, 1).

Show the steps
  1. q₁ = (1, 1, 0)/√2.
  2. a₂ − (a₂·q₁)q₁ = (1, 0, 1) − ½(1, 1, 0) = (0.5, −0.5, 1), length √1.5 = 1.225.

Answer: q₁ = (0.707, 0.707, 0), q₂ = (0.408, −0.408, 0.816).

Determinants: Properties, Cofactors, Cramer’s Rule and Volume

The determinant from its properties, computing it by elimination and by cofactor expansion, det(AB) = det A det B, the test for invertibility, Cramer’s rule, and the determinant as the area or volume scale factor of a linear map.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute 2 × 2 and 3 × 3 determinants by cofactors and by elimination.
  • Use the properties of determinants under row operations.
  • Solve small systems by Cramer’s rule.
  • Interpret |det A| as an area or volume scale factor.

Key concepts

  • det A ≠ 0 exactly when A is invertible. The determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries, so det A = ± (product of pivots).
  • Row operations: a swap flips the sign, scaling a row by c scales det by c, and adding a multiple of one row to another leaves det unchanged.
  • det(AB) = det A · det B, det(Aᵀ) = det A and det(A⁻¹) = 1/det A.
  • Cofactor expansion along a row: det A = Σ aᵢⱼCᵢⱼ with Cᵢⱼ = (−1)ⁱ⁺ʲ times the minor. Cramer’s rule: xᵢ = det(Bᵢ)/det A, where Bᵢ has b in column i.
  • A linear map x ↦ Ax multiplies areas (2D) or volumes (3D) by |det A|; a negative determinant also flips orientation.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 7.1 Find det [[1, 2, 0], [0, 1, 3], [2, 0, 1]] by cofactors along row 1.

Show the steps
  1. 1 × (1 × 1 − 3 × 0) = 1.
  2. − 2 × (0 × 1 − 3 × 2) = −2 × (−6) = 12.
  3. + 0 × (…) = 0.

Answer: det = 13.

Example 7.2 Solve 2x + y = 5, x + 3y = 10 by Cramer’s rule.

Show the steps
  1. det A = 6 − 1 = 5.
  2. x = det[[5, 1], [10, 3]]/5 = (15 − 10)/5.
  3. y = det[[2, 5], [1, 10]]/5 = (20 − 5)/5.

Answer: x = 1, y = 3.

Example 7.3 Find the area of the parallelogram with sides (3, 0) and (1, 2).

Show the steps
  1. Area = |det [[3, 1], [0, 2]]| = |6 − 0|.

Answer: 6 square units.

Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

Eigenvectors as directions a matrix only stretches, the characteristic equation det(A − λI) = 0, trace and determinant checks, eigenvalues of special matrices, complex eigenvalues and rotation, power iteration, and natural modes of vibrating structures.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Find eigenvalues from det(A − λI) = 0 and eigenvectors from the null space of A − λI.
  • Check eigenvalues with the trace and determinant.
  • Recognize real, repeated and complex eigenvalues.
  • Use power iteration to find the dominant eigenvalue.

Key concepts

  • Av = λv with v ≠ 0: the eigenvector v keeps its direction and is scaled by the eigenvalue λ.
  • The eigenvalues are the roots of det(A − λI) = 0, a polynomial of degree n. For each λ, the eigenvectors fill the null space of A − λI.
  • Checks: the eigenvalues sum to the trace (sum of the diagonal) and multiply to det A.
  • Symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors. Rotation matrices have complex eigenvalues. Triangular matrices show their eigenvalues on the diagonal.
  • Power iteration: repeatedly multiply a starting vector by A and rescale; it lines up with the eigenvector of the largest |λ|. For vibration, Kv = ω²Mv gives the natural frequencies ω and mode shapes v.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 8.1 Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]].

Show the steps
  1. det(A − λI) = (2 − λ)² − 1 = λ² − 4λ + 3 = 0.
  2. λ = 3: A − 3I = [[−1, 1], [1, −1]] gives v = (1, 1). λ = 1: v = (1, −1).
  3. Check: 3 + 1 = 4 = trace; 3 × 1 = 3 = det.

Answer: λ = 3 with (1, 1); λ = 1 with (1, −1).

Example 8.2 Find the eigenvalues of [[4, 1], [2, 3]].

Show the steps
  1. λ² − 7λ + 10 = 0 (trace 7, det 10).
  2. (λ − 5)(λ − 2) = 0.

Answer: λ = 5 and λ = 2.

Example 8.3 A two-story sample building has equal floor masses m and story stiffness k, with K/m = (k/m)[[2, −1], [−1, 1]] and k/m = 100 s⁻². Find the fundamental frequency.

Show the steps

  2. ω₁² = 100 × 0.382 = 38.2 s⁻², ω₁ = 6.18 rad/s.
  3. f₁ = ω₁/(2π), T₁ = 1/f₁.

Answer: f₁ = 0.984 Hz, period T₁ = 1.02 s (sample values).

A USGS study by Mehmet Çelebi (2022) used the fundamental periods and critical damping percentages of 41 tall buildings in several countries. It found a clear linear trend between building height and fundamental period for steel and reinforced-concrete buildings, though with a large scatter from differing structural designs, and that critical damping was clearly lower than 3%, consistent with other recent tall-building studies.

The fundamental period is set by the smallest eigenvalue of the stiffness–mass problem, as in the two-story sample example. Low damping means a tall building can ring for a long time when long-period earthquake waves match that period, which is why instrumented buildings and their measured modes guide safer, longer-lasting cities.

Unit 9 · Week 12

Diagonalization, Matrix Powers, Markov Chains and Linear Dynamical Systems

Diagonalizing A = PDP⁻¹, computing powers Aᵏ quickly, symmetric matrices and the spectral theorem A = QΛQᵀ, Markov (stochastic) matrices and steady states, and the long-run behavior of xₖ₊₁ = Axₖ.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Diagonalize a matrix with n independent eigenvectors.
  • Compute Aᵏ and predict the long-run behavior of xₖ₊₁ = Axₖ.
  • Find the steady state of a Markov chain.
  • Use the spectral theorem for symmetric matrices.

Key concepts

  • If A has n independent eigenvectors (the columns of P), then A = PDP⁻¹ with the eigenvalues on the diagonal of D, and Aᵏ = PDᵏP⁻¹.
  • Write x₀ = c₁v₁ + … + cₙvₙ. Then xₖ = Aᵏx₀ = c₁λ₁ᵏv₁ + … + cₙλₙᵏvₙ; the largest |λ| dominates as k grows.
  • A Markov matrix has nonnegative entries and columns that sum to 1. It always has λ = 1, and the other eigenvalues satisfy |λ| ≤ 1. The steady state is the eigenvector for λ = 1, scaled so its entries sum to 1.
  • The second-largest |λ| sets how fast a Markov chain forgets its start: the gap shrinks like |λ₂|ᵏ.
  • Spectral theorem: a real symmetric matrix can be written A = QΛQᵀ with orthonormal eigenvectors in Q.

Key equations

Unit 10 · Weeks 13–14

The Singular Value Decomposition, Principal Component Analysis and Positive Definite Matrices

Positive definite matrices and AᵀA, the SVD A = UΣVᵀ, singular values and the geometry of a matrix as rotate–stretch–rotate, best low-rank approximation and image compression, the condition number, and principal component analysis of data.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Test a symmetric matrix for positive definiteness.
  • Compute singular values from the eigenvalues of AᵀA.
  • Build a rank-k approximation and measure what it keeps.
  • Run a small principal component analysis.

Key concepts

  • A symmetric matrix is positive definite when xᵀAx > 0 for every x ≠ 0; equivalently all eigenvalues (or all pivots) are positive. AᵀA is always symmetric and positive semidefinite.
  • Every m × n matrix has an SVD A = UΣVᵀ: V and U have orthonormal columns, and Σ holds the singular values σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ … ≥ 0, the square roots of the eigenvalues of AᵀA.
  • A = σ₁u₁v₁ᵀ + σ₂u₂v₂ᵀ + …. Keeping the first k terms gives the best rank-k approximation (Eckart–Young); the error in the 2-norm is σₖ₊₁.
  • The condition number σ₁/σₙ measures how much errors in b can be amplified when solving Ax = b.
  • PCA: center the data (subtract column means), then the right singular vectors (eigenvectors of the covariance matrix) are the principal directions, and σᵢ² measures the variance each explains.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 10.1 Find the singular values of A = [[3, 0], [4, 5]].

Show the steps

  2. σ₁ = √45, σ₂ = √5. Check: σ₁σ₂ = 15 = |det A|.

Answer: σ₁ = 6.708, σ₂ = 2.236.

Example 10.2 What share of AᵀA’s “energy” Σσ² does the rank-1 approximation of that A keep?

Show the steps

Answer: 90%.

Example 10.3 A 1000 × 1000 grayscale image is stored as a rank-50 SVD. How many numbers are stored, compared with the full image?

Show the steps
  1. Each term needs one u (1000), one v (1000) and one σ: 2001 numbers.
  2. 50 × 2001 = 100,050 versus 1,000,000.

Answer: 100,050 numbers, about 10.0% of the original.

How this helps save the planet

NASA JPL’s EMIT imaging spectrometer on the International Space Station maps the minerals in Earth’s dust-source regions. JPL says it collects more than 100,000 spectra every second, which is 3.6 × 10⁸ spectra an hour, and that its mission has been extended through at least 2026. PCA and the SVD compress such data into a few components, separate signal from noise, and pick out the signatures of individual minerals, the same mathematics as the image-compression lab.

Glossary

Basis
A linearly independent set that spans a space.
Column space
All combinations Ax of the columns of A.
Condition number
σ₁/σₙ, how much errors can grow when solving Ax = b.
Determinant
Signed volume scale factor of A; zero exactly when A is singular.
Diagonalization
A = PDP⁻¹ with eigenvectors in P.
Dimension
Number of vectors in any basis.
Eigenvalue
λ with Av = λv for some nonzero v.
LU factorization
A = LU from elimination.
Least squares
The x̂ minimizing ‖b − Ax‖.
Leontief inverse
(I − A)⁻¹, total output needed per unit of final demand.
Linear independence
Only the zero combination gives the zero vector.
Markov matrix
Nonnegative entries, columns summing to 1.
Null space
All x with Ax = 0.
Orthonormal
Mutually orthogonal unit vectors.
Pivot
First nonzero entry of a row in echelon form.
Positive definite
Symmetric with xᵀAx > 0 for x ≠ 0.
Projection
Closest point to b in a subspace.
Rank
Number of pivots; dimension of the column space.
Singular value
σᵢ = √λᵢ(AᵀA).
Span
All linear combinations of a set of vectors.
Subspace
A set containing 0 that is closed under addition and scaling.
