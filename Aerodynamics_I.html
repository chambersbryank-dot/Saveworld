About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a one-semester junior aerodynamics course. It is modeled on the topics and depth of MIT 16.100 Aerodynamics and the fluids parts of MIT Unified Engineering (both free on MIT OpenCourseWare), and on junior aerodynamics courses such as Purdue AAE 333 and Georgia Tech AE 3030, using NASA Glenn’s free Beginner’s Guide to Aeronautics and FoilSim simulator, which we link where they help. None of those authors or schools endorses this page.

What we add: every unit ties to real work with a cited public source (NASA winglets, supercritical and truss-braced wings, wind turbines, aircraft emissions rules and flight on Mars), every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab has a free simulation or a safe home version.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Aerodynamics decides how much fuel an airplane burns and how much electricity a wind turbine makes. Every bit of drag removed from a wing is fuel not burned and carbon not emitted, and every improvement in a blade’s lift-to-drag ratio is more clean power from the same wind.

The same equations fly helicopters on Mars and bring spacecraft safely through Earth’s atmosphere. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Calculus III: partial derivatives, gradient, divergence, curl and line integrals.
  • Differential Equations: first- and second-order ODEs.
  • Dynamics and Physics I: Newton’s laws, momentum and energy; Physics II helps with fields.
  • Intro to Aerospace Engineering gives the vocabulary of lift, drag and the atmosphere.

How to study

  • Start every problem by naming the flow regime: incompressible or compressible (Mach number), inviscid or viscous (Reynolds number).
  • Draw a control volume or a streamline before writing an equation, and say which conservation law you are using.
  • Non-dimensionalize: work in coefficients (C_L, C_D, C_p) and similarity parameters (Re, M), then return to forces.
  • Check limits: at zero angle of attack a symmetric airfoil has zero lift; as aspect ratio grows, induced drag falls.
  • Keep SI units: Pa, kg/m³, m/s, N; convert knots and feet at the start.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer, and retake the quizzes a few days later.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer or tablet for NASA FoilSim and the free PhET fluid simulations (they run in a browser).
  • Printer paper, tape, paper clips, scissors and a ruler for paper gliders.
  • Paper coffee filters (cone or basket style) and a tape measure for drag tests.
  • A phone with a stopwatch and slow-motion video.
  • Spreadsheet software for the atmosphere, drag-polar and performance labs.

Unit 1 · Week 1

Fluid Properties and the Standard Atmosphere

Air as a continuum, pressure, density, temperature and viscosity, the ideal-gas law, the speed of sound, hydrostatics in the atmosphere, the standard atmosphere and its layers, and why aerodynamic forces depend on density.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use the ideal-gas law to relate p, ρ and T.
  • Compute the speed of sound and the Mach number.
  • Use the standard atmosphere model to find properties at altitude.
  • Explain how density changes lift and drag with altitude.

Key concepts

  • Air behaves as a continuum when the flow scale is much larger than the distance between molecules. Its state is set by pressure p, density ρ and temperature T, linked by the ideal-gas law p = ρRT with R = 287 J/(kg·K).
  • Viscosity μ measures internal friction; it matters near surfaces (Unit 8). Kinematic viscosity is ν = μ/ρ.
  • Speed of sound a = √(γRT) depends only on temperature for a given gas; Mach number M = V/a.
  • Hydrostatics: dp/dh = −ρg. Pressure falls with altitude because there is less air above. In the standard troposphere (0–11 km) temperature falls linearly with a lapse rate of about 6.5 K/km; in the lower stratosphere it is nearly constant.
  • Aerodynamic forces scale with density, so an aircraft at altitude must fly faster, or at a higher lift coefficient, to make the same lift.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 Find the density of air at sea level with p = 101,325 Pa and T = 288.15 K.

Show the steps
  1. ρ = p/(RT) = 101,325/(287 × 288.15).

Answer: ρ = 1.225 kg/m³.

Example 1.2 Use NASA Glenn’s metric atmosphere model to find T, p and ρ at 11,000 m.

Show the steps
  1. T = 15.04 − 0.00649 × 11,000 = −56.35 °C.
  2. p = 101.29 × [(T + 273.1)/288.08]^5.256 = 22.71 kPa.
  3. ρ = p/[0.2869 × (T + 273.1)].

Answer: T = −56.35 °C, p = 22.71 kPa, ρ = 0.3652 kg/m³, about 29.8% of sea-level density.

Example 1.3 Find the speed of sound at sea level (288.15 K) and at 11 km (216.8 K), and the Mach number of a jet flying 250 m/s at 11 km.

Show the steps
  1. a₀ = √(1.4 × 287 × 288.15).
  2. a = √(1.4 × 287 × 216.8).
  3. M = 250/a.

Answer: a₀ = 340.3 m/s, a = 295.1 m/s, M = 0.847.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Air pressure falls because air gets colder.

    Actually: It falls because there is less air above; temperature affects how fast.

  • Myth: The speed of sound depends on pressure.

    Actually: For an ideal gas it depends only on temperature.

  • Myth: Density is the same at all altitudes for low-speed flow.

    Actually: Low-speed flow is incompressible locally, but density still changes with altitude.

How this helps save the planet

NASA Glenn’s Beginner’s Guide explains that the atmosphere is an extremely thin sheet of air, that pressure falls with altitude because there is always less air above, and that aerodynamic forces depend directly on air density. Its standard atmosphere model, used in the FoilSim simulator, has a troposphere from the surface to 11,000 m where temperature falls linearly, a lower stratosphere from 11,000 to 25,000 m at a constant −56.46 °C, and an upper stratosphere above.

Airliners cruise near the top of the troposphere, where the density is only about 30% of sea level, so drag at a given speed is much lower; that is a big reason cruise altitude matters for fuel burn. The same model, with Mars values, explains why NASA’s Ingenuity helicopter had to spin its rotors so fast: NASA notes the Martian atmosphere is less than 1 percent as dense as Earth’s.

Unit 2 · Week 2

Fluid Statics, Bernoulli’s Equation and Airspeed Measurement

Static, dynamic and total pressure, Bernoulli’s equation and its assumptions, the venturi, pitot-static tubes and airspeed (indicated, equivalent and true), manometers, and the pressure coefficient.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • State when Bernoulli’s equation applies.
  • Compute dynamic pressure and airspeed from a pitot-static reading.
  • Analyze a venturi with continuity and Bernoulli.
  • Use the pressure coefficient C_p.

Key concepts

  • Along a streamline in steady, inviscid, incompressible flow, p + ½ρV² = p₀ is constant: static pressure plus dynamic pressure equals total (stagnation) pressure.
  • Dynamic pressure q = ½ρV² sets the scale of every aerodynamic force; forces are written as coefficient × q × area.
  • A pitot-static tube measures p₀ − p, so V = √(2(p₀ − p)/ρ). Airspeed indicators assume sea-level density and read equivalent airspeed; true airspeed is higher at altitude by √(ρ₀/ρ).
  • Continuity A₁V₁ = A₂V₂ plus Bernoulli explains the venturi: where the area shrinks the speed rises and the pressure drops.
  • Pressure coefficient C_p = (p − p∞)/q∞ = 1 − (V/V∞)² for incompressible flow; C_p = 1 at a stagnation point.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 A pitot-static tube at sea level reads p₀ − p = 1500 Pa. Find the airspeed.

Show the steps
  1. V = √(2 × 1500/1.225).

Answer: V = 49.5 m/s.

Example 2.2 A venturi in a wind tunnel halves the area. Upstream V₁ = 10 m/s at sea level. Find V₂ and the pressure drop.

Show the steps
  1. V₂ = 2 × 10 = 20 m/s (continuity).
  2. Δp = ½ × 1.225 × (20² − 10²).

Answer: V₂ = 20 m/s, Δp = 183.8 Pa.

Example 2.3 An airliner flies with an equivalent airspeed of 140 m/s at 11 km (ρ = 0.3652 kg/m³). Find the dynamic pressure and the true airspeed.

Show the steps
  1. q = ½ × 1.225 × 140² (equivalent airspeed uses sea-level density).
  2. V_true = 140 × √(1.225/0.3652).

Answer: q = 12.0 kPa, V_true = 256.4 m/s.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Bernoulli applies everywhere in any flow.

    Actually: It needs steady, inviscid, incompressible flow along a streamline (or irrotational flow).

  • Myth: Lift happens because air takes equal time over the top and bottom.

    Actually: There is no equal-transit rule; the top flow is faster still, and circulation explains lift (Unit 5).

  • Myth: Indicated airspeed equals true airspeed.

    Actually: At altitude true airspeed is much higher.

How this helps save the planet

Dynamic pressure is why flying on Mars is so hard. NASA reports that Ingenuity flew in an atmosphere less than 1 percent as dense as Earth’s, with rotors just under 1.2 m tip to tip and a mass of 1.8 kg. At 1% of Earth’s density, the same dynamic pressure needs √100 = 10 times the speed.

NASA says Ingenuity completed 72 flights between April 19, 2021 and January 18, 2024, logging 128.8 minutes in the air, covering 17.0 km and reaching altitudes of up to 24.0 m. Getting there meant understanding q = ½ρV² better than ever, which is also what lets Earth aircraft and wind turbines be designed for thinner air at altitude.

Unit 3 · Weeks 3–4

Conservation Laws: Mass, Momentum and Energy; Control Volumes and Momentum Theory

Lagrangian and Eulerian descriptions, the substantial derivative, integral conservation of mass, momentum and energy for a control volume, differential continuity, Euler’s equation, drag from a wake survey, and actuator-disk momentum theory for propellers and rotors.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Apply integral mass and momentum conservation to a control volume.
  • Derive the continuity and Euler equations in differential form.
  • Find drag from a wake velocity profile.
  • Use momentum theory to estimate rotor thrust and hover power.

Key concepts

  • Mass: ∂/∂t∫ρ dV + ∮ρV·dA = 0. For steady flow, mass in = mass out: ṁ = ρAV is constant through a stream tube.
  • Momentum: the net force on a control volume (pressure, body and surface forces) equals the net momentum flux out plus the rate of change inside: ΣF = ∮(ρV·dA)V for steady flow.
  • Differential forms: ∂ρ/∂t + ∇·(ρV) = 0; for incompressible flow ∇·V = 0. Euler’s equation ρ DV/Dt = −∇p + ρg is Newton’s law for an inviscid fluid particle; integrating it along a streamline gives Bernoulli.
  • Drag from a wake: a body slows the air behind it; the momentum deficit in the wake equals the drag, D′ = ρ∫u(V∞ − u) dy per unit span.
  • Actuator-disk (momentum) theory: a rotor of area A producing thrust T induces velocity v = √(T/(2ρA)) at the disk; the ideal hover power is P = T^(3/2)/√(2ρA). Real rotors need more; the ratio is the figure of merit.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 Air at 1.225 kg/m³ flows at 20 m/s through a 0.5 m² duct. Find the mass flow and the speed where the duct narrows to 0.2 m².

Show the steps
  1. ṁ = 1.225 × 0.5 × 20 = 12.25 kg/s.
  2. V₂ = 20 × 0.5/0.2.

Answer: ṁ = 12.25 kg/s, V₂ = 50 m/s.

Example 3.2 A helicopter of 1000 kg hovers at sea level with a 10 m diameter rotor. Find the induced velocity and the ideal hover power.

Show the steps
  1. T = 9810 N; A = π × 5² = 78.54 m².
  2. v = √(9810/(2 × 1.225 × 78.54)) = 7.14 m/s.
  3. P = Tv.

Answer: v = 7.14 m/s, P = 70.0 kW (ideal).

Example 3.3 A wake survey behind a 2D body (sea level, V∞ = 30 m/s) finds a deficit equivalent to ∫u(V∞ − u)dy = 6 m³/s². Find the drag per unit span.

Show the steps
  1. D′ = ρ∫u(V∞ − u) dy = 1.225 × 6.

Answer: D′ = 7.35 N/m.

  • Myth: Momentum conservation ignores pressure.

    Actually: Pressure forces on the control-volume surface must be included.

  • Myth: A bigger rotor needs more power to hover.

    Actually: At the same thrust, a bigger disk needs less induced power.

  • Myth: Steady flow means the fluid is not accelerating.

    Actually: Particles can accelerate (convective acceleration) even when the field does not change in time.

How this helps save the planet

Momentum theory shows why Ingenuity was a feat. NASA lists its mass as 1.8 kg, its rotors as about 1.2 m tip to tip, and its average flight power as about 350 W, from a solar panel charging lithium-ion batteries for one 90-second flight per Martian day. With Mars gravity (3.71 m/s², a textbook value), the thrust needed is 6.68 N.

Taking a sample Mars density of 0.0115 kg/m³ (within NASA’s “less than 1 percent” of Earth’s) and one 1.2 m disk, momentum theory gives an ideal hover power of about 107 W; the 350 W NASA reports also covers real rotor losses (profile drag, tip losses, non-uniform inflow), climbing and onboard systems. The same equation sizes rotors for electric air taxis and drones on Earth, and it is behind this site’s rotor hover power calculator.

Unit 4 · Weeks 5–6

Potential Flow and the Kutta–Joukowski Theorem

Vorticity and circulation, irrotational flow, the velocity potential and stream function, Laplace’s equation and superposition, uniform flow, sources, sinks, doublets and vortices, flow over a cylinder with and without circulation, d’Alembert’s paradox, and the Kutta–Joukowski lift theorem.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Relate vorticity, circulation and irrotational flow.
  • Build flows by superposing elementary solutions of Laplace’s equation.
  • Find the pressure distribution on a cylinder.
  • Apply L′ = ρ∞V∞Γ.

Key concepts

  • Vorticity ω = ∇ × V is twice the local rotation rate. Circulation Γ = −∮V·ds around a closed curve equals the vorticity flux through it (Stokes’ theorem; aerodynamics uses the clockwise-positive sign).
  • If ω = 0 the flow is irrotational and V = ∇φ. With ∇·V = 0, φ satisfies Laplace’s equation ∇²φ = 0, which is linear, so solutions add (superposition). In 2D the stream function ψ has u = ∂ψ/∂y, v = −∂ψ/∂x, and lines of constant ψ are streamlines.
  • Elementary flows: uniform flow φ = V∞x; source of strength Λ, V_r = Λ/(2πr); doublet, the limit of a source–sink pair; point vortex, V_θ = −Γ/(2πr).
  • Uniform flow plus a doublet gives flow over a cylinder of radius R: surface speed V_θ = −2V∞ sin θ, so C_p = 1 − 4 sin²θ. The pressure is symmetric, so there is no drag (d’Alembert’s paradox) and no lift.
  • Adding a vortex makes the flow asymmetric and produces lift per unit span L′ = ρ∞V∞Γ, the Kutta–Joukowski theorem. It holds for any 2D body: lift is always tied to circulation.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 Find the maximum surface speed and the minimum C_p on a non-lifting cylinder in a 20 m/s stream.

Show the steps
  1. |V_θ| = 2V∞|sin θ| is largest at θ = 90°.
  2. C_p = 1 − 4 × 1.

Answer: V_max = 40 m/s; C_p,min = −3.

Airfoils, Lift, Drag and Moment Coefficients

Airfoil geometry and NACA designations, angle of attack, the lift, drag and moment coefficients, the lift curve and stall, center of pressure and aerodynamic center, the Kutta condition, and how pressure distributions make lift.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Describe airfoil geometry: chord, camber and thickness.
  • Compute forces from L = qSC_L and D = qSC_D.
  • Read a lift curve: slope, zero-lift angle and stall.
  • Find the stall speed and the required lift coefficient.

Key concepts

  • An airfoil has a chord c from leading to trailing edge, a mean camber line and a thickness distribution. In a NACA 4-digit name such as 2412, the first digit is max camber (2% of chord), the second is its location (40% of chord) and the last two are thickness (12%).
  • Forces are made dimensionless: C_L = L/(qS), C_D = D/(qS), C_M = M/(qSc). For a 2D section use lowercase c_l, c_d, c_m per unit span.
  • The lift curve c_l(α) is nearly a straight line up to stall, where the boundary layer separates from the upper surface and lift falls. Cambered airfoils make lift at zero angle of attack, so their zero-lift angle α_L=0 is negative.
  • The Kutta condition says the flow leaves the sharp trailing edge smoothly; it fixes the circulation and hence the lift.
  • The center of pressure moves with α; the aerodynamic center (near quarter chord at low speed) is the point where the moment does not change with α, which makes it useful for stability.

Thin Airfoil Theory

Vortex sheets, modeling the camber line with a vortex sheet, the Kutta condition, the Fourier-series solution, the lift slope of 2π per radian, the zero-lift angle of cambered airfoils, the quarter-chord aerodynamic center, and panel methods.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Model an airfoil by a vortex sheet on its camber line.
  • Derive c_l = 2πα for a symmetric thin airfoil.
  • Find the lift coefficient of a cambered airfoil from α and α_L=0.
  • Explain why the aerodynamic center is at quarter chord.

Key concepts

  • A vortex sheet of strength γ(s) per unit length creates a jump in tangential velocity across it. Placing one on the camber line and requiring the camber line to be a streamline gives an integral equation for γ.
  • With the Kutta condition γ(TE) = 0 and a Fourier series in θ (x = c(1 − cos θ)/2), the solution gives c_l = 2π(α − α_L=0): the lift slope is 2π per radian (about 0.11 per degree) for every thin airfoil.
  • For a symmetric airfoil α_L=0 = 0, c_m,c/4 = 0 and c_m,LE = −c_l/4, so the center of pressure is at quarter chord. Camber shifts α_L=0 negative and adds a constant nose-down c_m,c/4, but the aerodynamic center stays at quarter chord.
  • Real airfoils of moderate thickness have lift slopes close to 2π until the boundary layer begins to separate; thin airfoil theory does not predict stall or drag.
  • Panel methods cover the real surface with many source and vortex panels and solve for their strengths numerically; they are how designers compute pressure on any shape, from blades to wings.

Finite Wings and Induced Drag

Wingtip vortices and downwash, the induced angle of attack, Prandtl’s lifting-line theory, elliptic lift distribution, span efficiency, induced drag C_Di = C_L²/(πeAR), the finite-wing lift slope, aspect ratio, winglets and truss-braced wings.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Explain how tip vortices create downwash and induced drag.
  • Compute induced drag with the span efficiency factor.
  • Find the finite-wing lift slope.
  • Explain how aspect ratio and winglets reduce induced drag.

Key concepts

  • On a finite wing, high-pressure air below curls around the tips to the low-pressure top, forming trailing vortices. They induce a downward velocity (downwash) at the wing, tilting the local flow by the induced angle α_i.
  • Tilting the lift vector back by α_i creates a drag component: induced drag, the price of making lift with a finite span. It is largest at low speed and high C_L.
  • Prandtl’s lifting-line theory models the wing as a bound vortex with a sheet of trailing vortices. An elliptic lift distribution gives uniform downwash, α_i = C_L/(πAR), and the minimum induced drag C_Di = C_L²/(πAR).
  • Real wings have span efficiency e < 1: C_Di = C_L²/(πeAR). Aspect ratio AR = b²/S is the key lever: doubling AR halves induced drag.
  • The finite-wing lift slope is lower than 2π: a = a₀/(1 + a₀/(πeAR)). Winglets and long, thin truss-braced wings both cut induced drag by weakening or spreading the tip vortices.

Show the steps

Viscous Flow, Reynolds Number and Boundary Layers

The no-slip condition, the Reynolds number and similarity, the Navier–Stokes equations in words, laminar and turbulent boundary layers, the Blasius flat-plate solution, skin friction, transition, separation and pressure drag, and the drag breakdown of an aircraft.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute the Reynolds number and use it for similarity.
  • Find boundary-layer thickness and skin-friction drag on a flat plate.
  • Distinguish laminar and turbulent boundary layers.
  • Explain separation, pressure drag and the total drag breakdown.

Key concepts

  • Viscous fluid sticks to a surface (no slip), so a thin boundary layer forms where speed rises from zero to the outer flow. Outside it, the inviscid theory of Units 4–7 works well.
  • The Reynolds number Re = ρVL/μ compares inertial to viscous forces. Two geometrically similar flows with equal Re (and Mach number) have the same coefficients; that is why wind-tunnel tests work.
  • The Navier–Stokes equations add viscous stresses to Euler’s equation. Prandtl’s boundary-layer simplification and the Blasius solution give, for a laminar flat plate, δ = 5.0x/√Re_x and C_f = 1.328/√Re_L.
  • Above a transition Reynolds number (about 5 × 10⁵ on a smooth flat plate) the layer becomes turbulent: thicker, with more skin friction, C_f ≈ 0.074/Re_L^0.2, but more resistant to separation.
  • In an adverse pressure gradient the boundary layer can separate, leaving a wide wake and pressure drag. Total drag = skin friction + pressure (form) drag + induced drag + wave drag (Unit 9).

Compressible Flow: Isentropic Relations and Shock Waves

When compressibility matters, stagnation properties, isentropic relations, area–velocity relation and nozzles, normal shocks, oblique shocks and expansion waves in brief, the critical Mach number, drag divergence, wave drag and supercritical airfoils.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Use isentropic relations to find stagnation properties.
  • Explain the area–velocity relation and choked nozzles.
  • Compute properties across a normal shock.
  • Explain critical Mach number and why supercritical airfoils delay drag rise.

Key concepts

  • Below about M = 0.3 density changes are under 5% and flow can be treated as incompressible. Above that, energy and density changes must be included.
  • Stagnation properties for adiabatic, isentropic flow: T₀/T = 1 + (γ − 1)M²/2, p₀/p = (T₀/T)^(γ/(γ−1)), ρ₀/ρ = (T₀/T)^(1/(γ−1)).
  • Area–velocity: dA/A = (M² − 1)dV/V. Subsonic flow speeds up in a converging duct; supersonic flow speeds up in a diverging one. Sonic flow occurs at a throat; A/A* sets the Mach number.
  • A normal shock is a thin, irreversible jump: supersonic flow becomes subsonic, and pressure, density and temperature jump while total pressure falls. For γ = 1.4 and M₁ = 2, M₂ = 0.577 and p₂/p₁ = 4.5.
  • On a wing at high subsonic speed, the flow over the top reaches M = 1 first, at the critical Mach number. Beyond it a shock forms and drag rises sharply (drag divergence). Supercritical airfoils and wing sweep push this rise to higher speeds.

How this helps save the planet

NASA explains that as airliners approach the speed of sound, about 660 mph at cruise altitude, a standing shock wave forms on a conventional wing and adds drag. Whitcomb’s supercritical airfoil, flatter on top and rounded on the bottom, minimized that shock, raising efficiency by as much as 15% according to NASA.

That is the critical-Mach and drag-divergence story from this unit. NASA says the design became the industry standard and saves billions of dollars in fuel a year; less fuel burned at cruise means less CO₂ from the flights that make up a large share of aviation emissions.

Unit 10 · Weeks 14–15

Aircraft Performance: Drag Polar, Glide, Range and Endurance

The drag polar C_D = C_D0 + KC_L², thrust and power required, minimum-drag speed, maximum lift-to-drag ratio, gliding flight, the Breguet range and endurance equations, and how aerodynamic efficiency translates into fuel and emissions.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Build a drag polar and find (L/D)max.
  • Find the minimum-drag speed and glide distance.
  • Use the Breguet range equation.
  • Connect drag reductions to fuel burn.

Key concepts

  • The drag polar C_D = C_D0 + KC_L² with K = 1/(πeAR) combines zero-lift (parasite) drag and induced drag.
  • In level flight L = W and T = D. Drag is lowest where parasite and induced drag are equal: C_L* = √(C_D0/K), giving (L/D)max = 1/(2√(KC_D0)).
  • Gliding: with no thrust, the glide ratio equals L/D, so the best glide distance from height h is h(L/D)max.
  • Breguet range for a jet: R = (V/c)(L/D) ln(W_i/W_f), with c the thrust-specific fuel consumption. Range grows directly with L/D, so aerodynamic efficiency multiplies with engine efficiency and weight.
  • For a fixed trip, raising L/D lowers the fuel needed; that is why winglets, supercritical airfoils and high-aspect-ratio wings all cut CO₂.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 10.1 A wing has AR = 8, e = 0.9 and C_D0 = 0.02. Find K, (L/D)max and C_L*.

Show the steps
  1. K = 1/(π × 0.9 × 8) = 0.04421.
  2. (L/D)max = 1/(2√(0.04421 × 0.02)).
  3. C_L* = √(0.02/0.04421).

Answer: K = 0.04421, (L/D)max = 16.81, C_L* = 0.673.

Example 10.2 For that airplane (W = 9810 N, S = 16 m², sea level), find the minimum-drag speed and the best glide distance from 1000 m.

Show the steps
  1. V = √(2W/(ρSC_L*)) = √(19,620/(1.225 × 16 × 0.6726)).
  2. Glide = 1000 × 16.81.

Answer: V = 38.6 m/s; glide distance 16.8 km.

Example 10.3 A jet (sample values) cruises at V = 230 m/s with c = 1.6 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹, L/D = 17 and W_i/W_f = 1.25. Find the range.

Show the steps
  1. R = (230/1.6 × 10⁻⁴) × 17 × ln 1.25.

Answer: R = 5453 km.

Glossary

Angle of attack
Angle between the chord and the oncoming flow.
Aspect ratio
b²/S, span squared over wing area.
Boundary layer
Thin layer near a surface where viscosity slows the flow.
Camber
Curvature of an airfoil’s mean line.
Circulation
Line integral of velocity around a closed curve; tied to lift.
Critical Mach number
Free-stream Mach at which local flow first reaches M = 1.
Downwash
Downward flow induced by trailing vortices.
Drag polar
C_D as a function of C_L.
Dynamic pressure
q = ½ρV².
Induced drag
Drag from tilting lift by downwash.
Kutta condition
Flow leaves a sharp trailing edge smoothly.
Mach number
Flow speed over the speed of sound.
Normal shock
Thin jump that turns supersonic flow subsonic.
Reynolds number
ρVL/μ, inertia over viscosity.
Separation
Boundary layer leaving the surface.
Stagnation pressure
Pressure if flow is brought to rest isentropically.
Stall
Loss of lift when flow separates at high angle of attack.
Supercritical airfoil
Flat-topped airfoil that weakens shocks near M = 1.
Vorticity
∇ × V, twice the local rotation rate.
Winglet
Near-vertical tip surface that reduces induced drag.
