Circular orbits, period and escape speed

  • Teens
  • Community college
  • Undergraduate (freshman–senior)

Track: Orbital Mechanics · About 15 min · Numbers verified in build: 2026-10-19

You will be able to

Derive the circular orbit speed from Newton’s laws; compute an orbital period; explain why escape speed is √2 times circular speed.

Before you start

Algebra, Newton’s second law, centripetal acceleration v²/r. Orbits Explained

Intuition first

In a circular orbit gravity supplies exactly the centripetal force needed to keep turning. The constant μ = GM (the gravitational parameter) is known far more precisely than G or M separately, so we always use μ.

🧒 Explain like I’m 10

A satellite is like a ball on a string you swing around your head. Gravity is the string. Swing too slowly and the ball drops; swing just right and it goes round and round.

Step by step

  1. GMmr2=mv2r\frac{GMm}{r^2}=\frac{mv^2}{r}r2GMm​=rmv2​
    Gravity = mass × centripetal acceleration.
  2. vc=μrv_c=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r}}vc​=rμ​​
    Cancel m and solve for v.
  3. T=2πrvc=2πr3μT=\frac{2\pi r}{v_c}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{\mu}}T=vc​2πr​=2πμr3​​
    Period = circumference / speed.
  4. 12vesc2−μr=0  ⇒  vesc=2μr=2 vc\tfrac12 v_{esc}^2-\frac{\mu}{r}=0\;\Rightarrow\; v_{esc}=\sqrt{\frac{2\mu}{r}}=\sqrt2\,v_c21​vesc2​−rμ​=0⇒vesc​=r2μ​​=2​vc​
    Escape: total energy just reaches zero.

Worked example

Given
Circular orbit at 400 km altitude.
Find
Speed v_c and period T.
Strategy
Use r = R⊕ + h, then v_c = √(μ/r) and T = 2πr/v_c.
Steps
  1. r = 6378.137 + 400 = 6778.137 km
  2. v_c = √(398600.4418 / 6778.137) = 7.6686 km/s
  3. T = 2π · 6778.137 / 7.6686 s = 92.56 min
Answer
v_c = 7.669 km/s, T = 92.6 min.
Sanity check
NASA lists the space station at roughly 7.7 km/s and about 90 minutes per orbit, so the numbers agree.
Significance
About 15–16 orbits a day: the basis for ground-station pass planning.
Show the Python
import math
mu, RE = 398600.4418, 6378.137   # km^3/s^2, km
r = RE + 400
v = math.sqrt(mu / r)
T = 2 * math.pi * r / v
print(v, T / 60)   # km/s, min

Auto-checked practice

  1. km/s

    1. r = R⊕ + h, not h.
    2. v = √(μ/r) with r = 7178.137 km.
    Full solution

    r = 7178.137 km; v = √(398600.4418/7178.137) = 7.4518 km/s.

  2. min

    1. T = 2π√(r³/μ), in seconds; then divide by 60.
    Full solution

    T = 2π√(7178.137³/398600.4418) s = 100.87 min.

Practice

  1. Find the radius of an orbit whose period is one sidereal day (86 164.09 s).

    Show solution

    r = (μ T²/4π²)^(1/3) = 42164 km (geostationary), where v_c = 3.075 km/s.

Common mistake

Using altitude instead of radius. r is measured from Earth’s centre: r = R⊕ + h.

References

  • NASA, Basics of Space Flight.
  • H. D. Curtis, Orbital Mechanics for Engineering Students, 4th ed., Butterworth-Heinemann (Elsevier), 2020.

Kepler’s three laws

  • Teens
  • Community college
  • Undergraduate (freshman–senior)

Track: Orbital Mechanics · About 15 min · Numbers verified in build: 2026-10-19

You will be able to

State Kepler’s laws; show that equal areas in equal times is conservation of angular momentum; derive the third law.

Before you start

Ellipse geometry (a, e), cross product, derivative of an area. Orbits Explained

Intuition first

1) Orbits are conic sections with the central body at one focus. 2) The line from the focus sweeps equal areas in equal times. 3) T² is proportional to a³.

🧒 Explain like I’m 10

Planets speed up when they come close to the Sun and slow down when far away, like a skateboarder in a half-pipe: fastest at the bottom, slowest at the top.

Step by step

  1. dA=12 ∣r×dr∣  ⇒  dAdt=12∣r×v∣=h2dA=\tfrac12\,|\mathbf r\times d\mathbf r|\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{dA}{dt}=\tfrac12|\mathbf r\times\mathbf v|=\frac{h}{2}dA=21​∣r×dr∣⇒dtdA​=21​∣r×v∣=2h​
    Area swept per unit time is half the specific angular momentum h.
  2. h˙=r˙×v+r×(−μr3r)=0\dot{\mathbf h}=\dot{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf v+\mathbf r\times\left(-\frac{\mu}{r^3}\mathbf r\right)=\mathbf 0h˙=r˙×v+r×(−r3μ​r)=0
    For a central force h is constant, so dA/dt is constant (2nd law).
  3. T=πabh/2,h=μa(1−e2),b=a1−e2T=\frac{\pi ab}{h/2},\quad h=\sqrt{\mu a(1-e^2)},\quad b=a\sqrt{1-e^2}T=h/2πab​,h=μa(1−e2)​,b=a1−e2​
    Total area of the ellipse divided by the areal rate.
  4. T=2πa3μT=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}}T=2πμa3​​
    Third law: depends only on a, not on e.

Worked example

Given
Geostationary radius r = 42 164.17 km (circular).
Find
The orbital period T.
Strategy
Kepler’s third law with a = r.
Steps
  1. T = 2π √(a³/μ) = 2π √(42164.17³ / 398600.4418)
  2. T = 23.9345 h
Answer
T = 23.9345 h (23 h 56 min).
Sanity check
It equals one sidereal day (86 164 s), exactly what "geostationary" requires.
Significance
Kepler’s law, found from planet data in 1619, still sets where every TV and weather satellite is parked.
Show the Python
import math
mu, RE = 398600.4418, 6378.137   # km^3/s^2, km
a = 42164.17
T = 2 * math.pi * math.sqrt(a**3 / mu)
print(T / 3600)   # h

Practice

  1. Two orbits have the same a but e = 0 and e = 0.7. Which has the longer period?

    Show solution

    Neither: T depends only on a.

Common mistake

Using 24 h for geostationary orbit. Earth turns once relative to the stars in 23 h 56 min 4 s.

References

  • H. D. Curtis, Orbital Mechanics for Engineering Students, 4th ed., Butterworth-Heinemann (Elsevier), 2020.
  • R. R. Bate, D. D. Mueller, J. E. White, W. W. Saylor, Fundamentals of Astrodynamics, 2nd ed., Dover, 2020.

The two-body problem and the orbit equation

  • Undergraduate (freshman–senior)

Track: Orbital Mechanics · About 30 min · Numbers verified in build: 2026-10-19

You will be able to

Reduce two bodies to one relative equation; integrate it with vector calculus to get the conic r(ν); identify the eccentricity vector.

Before you start

Vector calculus (dot/cross products, BAC-CAB identity), first-order ODEs. Orbits Explained

Intuition first

Subtracting the two equations of motion leaves one ODE for the relative position. Two vector integrals, h and e, then give the whole shape of the orbit without solving for time.

🧒 Explain like I’m 10

Earth and a satellite both pull on each other. We can pretend Earth stands still and only watch the satellite, and the path always comes out as a circle, an oval, or a curve that flies away.

Step by step

  1. r¨=−μr3r,μ=G(m1+m2)\ddot{\mathbf r}=-\frac{\mu}{r^3}\mathbf r,\qquad \mu=G(m_1+m_2)r¨=−r3μ​r,μ=G(m1​+m2​)
    Relative equation of motion.
  2. r¨×h=−μr3 r×(r×r˙)=μddt ⁣(rr)\ddot{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf h=-\frac{\mu}{r^3}\,\mathbf r\times(\mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf r})=\mu\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf r}{r}\right)r¨×h=−r3μ​r×(r×r˙)=μdtd​(rr​)
    Cross with constant h and use BAC-CAB.
  3. r˙×h−μrr=μ e\dot{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf h-\mu\frac{\mathbf r}{r}=\mu\,\mathbf er˙×h−μrr​=μe
    Integrate once: e is a constant vector pointing to periapsis.
  4. r⋅(r˙×h)=h2=μr+μrecos⁡ν  ⇒  r=h2/μ1+ecos⁡ν\mathbf r\cdot(\dot{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf h)=h^2=\mu r+\mu r e\cos\nu\;\Rightarrow\; r=\frac{h^2/\mu}{1+e\cos\nu}r⋅(r˙×h)=h2=μr+μrecosν⇒r=1+ecosνh2/μ​
    Dot with r: the conic equation.

Worked example

Given
r = [7000, 1000, 500] km, v = [−1.0, 7.4, 1.0] km/s.
Find
h, e and the orbit size a.
Strategy
Use the two vector integrals h = r × v and μe = v × h − μ r/r, then the energy.
Steps
  1. |h| = |r × v| = 53398.3 km²/s
  2. |e| = 0.019306
  3. ε = v²/2 − μ/r = -27.850 km²/s²
  4. a = −μ/(2ε) = 7156.15 km
Answer
Nearly circular (e = 0.0193) with a = 7156.1 km.
Sanity check
Check h²/μ = a(1 − e²): both sides agree to rounding.
Significance
Two conserved vectors give the full shape of the orbit from a single radar fix.
Show the Python
import numpy as np
mu = 398600.4418
r = np.array([7000., 1000., 500.]); v = np.array([-1.0, 7.4, 1.0])
h = np.cross(r, v)
e = np.cross(v, h) / mu - r / np.linalg.norm(r)
eps = v @ v / 2 - mu / np.linalg.norm(r)
print(np.linalg.norm(h), np.linalg.norm(e), -mu / (2 * eps))

Practice

  1. Show that e < 1 gives a closed orbit and e > 1 an open one.

    Show solution

    The denominator 1 + e cosν stays positive for every ν only if e < 1; for e > 1 it vanishes at cosν = -1/e, so r → ∞.

Common mistake

Forgetting that μ = G(m₁+m₂). For a satellite m₂ is negligible, but not for the Earth–Moon pair.

References

  • H. D. Curtis, Orbital Mechanics for Engineering Students, 4th ed., Butterworth-Heinemann (Elsevier), 2020.
  • D. A. Vallado, Fundamentals of Astrodynamics and Applications, 4th ed., Microcosm Press, 2013.
  • R. R. Bate, D. D. Mueller, J. E. White, W. W. Saylor, Fundamentals of Astrodynamics, 2nd ed., Dover, 2020.

Energy and the vis-viva equation

  • Community college
  • Undergraduate (freshman–senior)

Track: Orbital Mechanics · About 20 min · Numbers verified in build: 2026-10-19

You will be able to

Show that specific orbital energy is conserved and equals -μ/2a; use vis-viva to find speed anywhere on an orbit.

Before you start

Kinetic and potential energy; dot product; the conic r(ν). Orbits Explained

Intuition first

Vis-viva ("living force") is the single most useful equation in mission design: it links speed, distance and orbit size.

🧒 Explain like I’m 10

Going up a hill you slow down; coming down you speed up. An orbit is the same: close to Earth you are fast, far away you are slow, and the total energy stays the same.

Step by step

  1. r˙⋅r¨+μr3 r⋅r˙=0  ⇒  ddt ⁣(v22−μr)=0\dot{\mathbf r}\cdot\ddot{\mathbf r}+\frac{\mu}{r^3}\,\mathbf r\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}=0\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{v^2}{2}-\frac{\mu}{r}\right)=0r˙⋅r¨+r3μ​r⋅r˙=0⇒dtd​(2v2​−rμ​)=0
    Dot the equation of motion with velocity.
  2. ε=vp22−μrp,rp=a(1−e),vp=hrp\varepsilon=\frac{v_p^2}{2}-\frac{\mu}{r_p},\quad r_p=a(1-e),\quad v_p=\frac{h}{r_p}ε=2vp2​​−rp​μ​,rp​=a(1−e),vp​=rp​h​
    Evaluate at periapsis, where v is perpendicular to r.
  3. ε=−μ2a\varepsilon=-\frac{\mu}{2a}ε=−2aμ​
    Simplify with h² = μa(1-e²).
  4. v2=μ(2r−1a)v^2=\mu\left(\frac{2}{r}-\frac{1}{a}\right)v2=μ(r2​−a1​)
    Vis-viva.

Worked example

Given
Transfer ellipse from r_p = 6678.137 km to r_a = 42 164.17 km.
Find
Speeds at perigee and apogee.
Strategy
a = (r_p + r_a)/2, then vis-viva at each end.
Steps
  1. a = 24421.2 km
  2. v_p = √(μ(2/r_p − 1/a)) = 10.1515 km/s
  3. v_a = √(μ(2/r_a − 1/a)) = 1.6078 km/s
Answer
v_p = 10.151 km/s, v_a = 1.608 km/s.
Sanity check
Angular momentum check: r_p v_p = r_a v_a (both about 67 793 km²/s).
Significance
These two speeds are exactly what the Hohmann burns are measured against.
Show the Python
import math
mu, RE = 398600.4418, 6378.137   # km^3/s^2, km
rp, ra = RE + 300, 42164.17
a = (rp + ra) / 2
vv = lambda r: math.sqrt(mu * (2/r - 1/a))
print(vv(rp), vv(ra), rp*vv(rp), ra*vv(ra))

Auto-checked practice

  1. km/s

    1. v² = μ(2/r − 1/a).
    Full solution

    v = √(398600.4418 × (2/8000 − 1/10000)) = 7.7324 km/s.

Practice

  1. What is the sign of ε for an escape (parabolic) trajectory?

    Show solution

    Zero (a → ∞). Bound orbits have ε < 0, hyperbolas ε > 0.

Common mistake

Mixing units: with μ in km³/s², r and a must be in km and v comes out in km/s.

References

  • H. D. Curtis, Orbital Mechanics for Engineering Students, 4th ed., Butterworth-Heinemann (Elsevier), 2020.
  • R. R. Bate, D. D. Mueller, J. E. White, W. W. Saylor, Fundamentals of Astrodynamics, 2nd ed., Dover, 2020.

Orbital elements from a state vector

  • Undergraduate (freshman–senior)
  • Graduate

Track: Orbital Mechanics · About 30 min · Numbers verified in build: 2026-10-19

You will be able to

Convert position and velocity (r, v) into the six classical elements a, e, i, Ω, ω, ν using linear algebra.

Before you start

Two-body problem, vectors, arccos quadrant checks. Orbits Explained

Intuition first

Six numbers fix an orbit: size (a), shape (e), tilt (i), where it crosses the equator going north (Ω), where periapsis lies (ω) and where the satellite is now (ν).

🧒 Explain like I’m 10

Six numbers are like an address for an orbit: how big, how stretched, how tilted, which way it faces, where its closest point is, and where the satellite is right now.

Step by step

  1. h=r×v,n=k^×h,e=v×hμ−rr\mathbf h=\mathbf r\times\mathbf v,\quad \mathbf n=\hat{\mathbf k}\times\mathbf h,\quad \mathbf e=\frac{\mathbf v\times\mathbf h}{\mu}-\frac{\mathbf r}{r}h=r×v,n=k^×h,e=μv×h​−rr​
    Three key vectors.
  2. a=−μ2ε,cos⁡i=hzh,cos⁡Ω=nxn  (ny<0⇒Ω=360∘−Ω)a=-\frac{\mu}{2\varepsilon},\quad \cos i=\frac{h_z}{h},\quad \cos\Omega=\frac{n_x}{n}\;(n_y<0\Rightarrow\Omega=360^\circ-\Omega)a=−2εμ​,cosi=hhz​​,cosΩ=nnx​​(ny​<0⇒Ω=360∘−Ω)
    Size, tilt, node (with quadrant check).
  3. cos⁡ω=n⋅ene  (ez<0⇒360∘−ω),cos⁡ν=e⋅rer  (r⋅v<0⇒360∘−ν)\cos\omega=\frac{\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf e}{ne}\;(e_z<0\Rightarrow 360^\circ-\omega),\quad \cos\nu=\frac{\mathbf e\cdot\mathbf r}{er}\;(\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf v<0\Rightarrow 360^\circ-\nu)cosω=nen⋅e​(ez​<0⇒360∘−ω),cosν=ere⋅r​(r⋅v<0⇒360∘−ν)
    Periapsis and current position.

Worked example

Given
r = [7000, 1000, 500] km, v = [−1.0, 7.4, 1.0] km/s.
Find
a, e, i, Ω, ω, ν.
Strategy
Build h, n, e; read off each angle with its quadrant check (Vallado, Algorithm 9).
Steps
  1. i = arccos(h_z/h) = 8.585°
  2. Ω = 340.20° (n_y < 0)
  3. ω = 326.44° (e_z < 0)
  4. ν = 61.76° (r·v > 0)
  5. a = 7156.15 km, e = 0.019306
Answer
a = 7156.1 km, e = 0.0193, i = 8.59°, Ω = 340.2°, ω = 326.4°, ν = 61.8°.
Sanity check
Converting the elements back to r and v returns the inputs (round trip).
Significance
Elements are how orbits are published (e.g. two-line element sets) and compared.
Show the Python
import numpy as np
mu = 398600.4418
r = np.array([7000., 1000., 500.]); v = np.array([-1.0, 7.4, 1.0])
h = np.cross(r, v); n = np.cross([0, 0, 1], h)
e = np.cross(v, h) / mu - r / np.linalg.norm(r)
i = np.degrees(np.arccos(h[2] / np.linalg.norm(h)))
W = np.degrees(np.arccos(n[0] / np.linalg.norm(n))); W = 360 - W if n[1] < 0 else W
print(i, W)

Practice

  1. Why are Ω and ω undefined for an equatorial circular orbit?

    Show solution

    n = 0 (no node line) and e = 0 (no periapsis); use true longitude instead.

Common mistake

Skipping the quadrant checks: arccos only returns 0–180°.

References

  • D. A. Vallado, Fundamentals of Astrodynamics and Applications, 4th ed., Microcosm Press, 2013.
  • H. D. Curtis, Orbital Mechanics for Engineering Students, 4th ed., Butterworth-Heinemann (Elsevier), 2020.

Kepler’s equation and Newton–Raphson

  • Undergraduate (freshman–senior)
  • Graduate

Track: Orbital Mechanics · About 30 min · Numbers verified in build: 2026-10-19

You will be able to

Relate time to position with mean, eccentric and true anomaly; solve the transcendental equation with Newton–Raphson.

Before you start

Derivatives, Taylor series to first order, radians. Orbits Explained

Intuition first

There is no closed-form x(t) for an ellipse. Kepler’s equation must be solved numerically; Newton–Raphson converges quadratically from E₀ = M (or M + e for large e).

🧒 Explain like I’m 10

Some puzzles can’t be solved in one step, so you guess, check how wrong you are, and fix the guess. Each try gets much closer, like a game of hot and cold.

Step by step

  1. M=n(t−tp),n=μ/a3M=n(t-t_p),\qquad n=\sqrt{\mu/a^3}M=n(t−tp​),n=μ/a3​
    Mean anomaly grows uniformly in time.
  2. M=E−esin⁡EM=E-e\sin EM=E−esinE
    Kepler’s equation.
  3. Ek+1=Ek−Ek−esin⁡Ek−M1−ecos⁡EkE_{k+1}=E_k-\frac{E_k-e\sin E_k-M}{1-e\cos E_k}Ek+1​=Ek​−1−ecosEk​Ek​−esinEk​−M​
    Newton–Raphson: f/f′.
  4. tan⁡ν2=1+e1−etan⁡E2\tan\frac{\nu}{2}=\sqrt{\frac{1+e}{1-e}}\tan\frac{E}{2}tan2ν​=1−e1+e​​tan2E​
    Convert to true anomaly.

Worked example

Given
e = 0.37255, M = 3.6029 rad (the same inputs as Curtis, Example 3.2; our own computation).
Find
The eccentric anomaly E to 10⁻¹⁰ rad.
Strategy
Newton–Raphson on f(E) = E − e sin E − M with f′(E) = 1 − e cos E, starting at E₀ = M (e ≤ 0.8); stop when |ΔE| < 10⁻¹⁰.
Steps
Iteration table
nE_n (rad)|ΔE|
13.47855373871.24e-01
23.47942201008.68e-04
33.47942204433.44e-08
43.47942204430.00e+00
Answer
E = 3.479422 rad after 4 iterations.
Sanity check
Substitute back: E − e sin E returns M = 3.6029 rad. Since M > π the body is past apoapsis, and E < M as expected when sin E < 0. Curtis reports E = 3.479 rad.
Significance
The error roughly squares each step (quadratic convergence), which is why flight software solves Kepler’s equation thousands of times per second.

Try it: Kepler solver, one step at a time

nE_n (rad)|ΔE|
Show the Python
import math
def kepler_E(M, e, tol=1e-10):
    E = math.pi if e > 0.8 else M
    while True:
        dE = (E - e*math.sin(E) - M) / (1 - e*math.cos(E))
        E -= dE
        print(E, abs(dE))
        if abs(dE) < tol:
            return E
kepler_E(3.6029, 0.37255)

Auto-checked practice

  1. rad

    1. Start at E₀ = M = 1.
    2. E₁ = 1 − (1 − 0.2 sin 1 − 1)/(1 − 0.2 cos 1).
    Full solution

    Newton–Raphson converges in a few steps to E = 1.185324 rad.

Practice

  1. Why must M and E be in radians inside the iteration?

    Show solution

    Because the E term and the e sin E term must share units; e sin E is dimensionless (radians).

Common mistake

Mixing degrees and radians in M = E - e sin E.

Numerical propagation with RK4

  • Graduate

Track: Orbital Mechanics · About 35 min · Numbers verified in build: 2026-10-19

You will be able to

Write the orbit as a first-order ODE system; integrate it with the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method; check accuracy with energy conservation.

Before you start

ODEs, Taylor series, basic programming. Orbits Explained

Intuition first

Once perturbations are added (J2, drag, third bodies) there is no exact solution, so we integrate. RK4 has local error O(Δt⁵) and global error O(Δt⁴).

🧒 Explain like I’m 10

A computer moves the satellite forward in tiny steps, like a flip-book: each page is a fraction of a second later. Smarter steps (RK4) peek ahead before moving.

Step by step

  1. y=[rv],y˙=f(y)=[v−μr/r3]\mathbf y=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf r\\ \mathbf v\end{bmatrix},\qquad \dot{\mathbf y}=\mathbf f(\mathbf y)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf v\\ -\mu\mathbf r/r^3\end{bmatrix}y=[rv​],y˙​=f(y)=[v−μr/r3​]
    Second-order ODE becomes a 6-state first-order system.
  2. k1=f(yn), k2=f(yn+Δt2k1), k3=f(yn+Δt2k2), k4=f(yn+Δt k3)\mathbf k_1=\mathbf f(\mathbf y_n),\ \mathbf k_2=\mathbf f(\mathbf y_n+\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf k_1),\ \mathbf k_3=\mathbf f(\mathbf y_n+\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf k_2),\ \mathbf k_4=\mathbf f(\mathbf y_n+\Delta t\,\mathbf k_3)k1​=f(yn​), k2​=f(yn​+2Δt​k1​), k3​=f(yn​+2Δt​k2​), k4​=f(yn​+Δtk3​)
    Four slope samples.
  3. yn+1=yn+Δt6(k1+2k2+2k3+k4)\mathbf y_{n+1}=\mathbf y_n+\frac{\Delta t}{6}\left(\mathbf k_1+2\mathbf k_2+2\mathbf k_3+\mathbf k_4\right)yn+1​=yn​+6Δt​(k1​+2k2​+2k3​+k4​)
    Weighted average (Simpson-like).

Worked example

Given
Initial state from the Lambert example: r₁ = [7000, 0, 0] km and its v₁; 3600 s; 20 000 RK4 steps.
Find
Final position and its distance from the target r₂ = [0, 8000, 0] km.
Strategy
Integrate ẏ = f(y) with RK4, Δt = 0.18 s.
Steps
  1. v₁ = (4.6069, 5.8532, 0) km/s
  2. Δt = 3600 / 20000 = 0.18 s
  3. |r(3600) − r₂| = 1.7×10⁻¹⁰ km
Answer
The orbit lands on the target to within 1.7×10⁻¹⁰ km.
Sanity check
Two independent methods (Lambert solver, RK4) agree, so both are working.
Significance
The same integrator, with J2 and drag added to f(y), is how real orbits are predicted.
Show the Python
import numpy as np
mu = 398600.4418
def f(y): r = y[:3]; return np.r_[y[3:], -mu * r / np.linalg.norm(r)**3]
def rk4(y, dt):
    k1 = f(y); k2 = f(y + dt/2*k1); k3 = f(y + dt/2*k2); k4 = f(y + dt*k3)
    return y + dt/6*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)

Practice

  1. Halving Δt reduces RK4 global error by roughly what factor?

    Show solution

    About 2⁴ = 16.

Common mistake

Trusting a long run without checking a conserved quantity (energy, h). RK4 is not symplectic and drifts slowly.

Before you start

Vectors, law of cosines, Hohmann transfer. Orbits Explained

Intuition first

Turning the velocity vector costs speed proportional to how fast you are going, so plane changes are cheapest where the orbit is slowest.

🧒 Explain like I’m 10

Turning a fast bike is hard; turning a slow one is easy. Spacecraft tilt their orbits where they move slowest.

Step by step

  1. Δv=v12+v22−2v1v2cos⁡Δi\Delta v=\sqrt{v_1^2+v_2^2-2v_1v_2\cos\Delta i}Δv=v12​+v22​−2v1​v2​cosΔi​

    Pure plane change at constant speed.

Worked example

Given
GEO insertion from a 28.5° transfer orbit: v_a = 1.6078 km/s, v_GEO = 3.0747 km/s.

Steps

The combined burn is less than the sum, as the triangle inequality requires.
Significance

For teachers and instructors

Suggested use: one 50-minute session per two sections. Undergraduate path: circular → Kepler → vis-viva → Hohmann → rocket equation, with the Orbit Lab as a live demo of the Hohmann worked example. Graduate path: elements → Kepler’s equation → RK4 → J2 → Lambert (assign the Python snippets as labs). Every numeric answer is computed by the site’s build script, so the practice answers double as an answer key. Aligned with MIT OCW 16.346 Astrodynamics topics.
