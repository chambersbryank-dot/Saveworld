About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a one-semester senior spacecraft design course for aerospace engineers. It is modeled on the topics and depth of MIT 16.851 Satellite Engineering (free on MIT OpenCourseWare), using NASA’s free Systems Engineering Handbook and Small Spacecraft Technology State of the Art report, which we link where they help. None of those authors, schools or agencies endorses this page.

What we add: every unit ties to real work with a cited public source (NASA’s Landsat 9, Europa Clipper and PACE missions and NASA’s small-spacecraft technology survey), every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab has a free simulation or a spreadsheet.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Earth-observing satellites are spacecraft design made useful: power, thermal, pointing and communications all have to work for years so scientists can track forests, water, crops and climate.

Good design also means long life and less waste in orbit. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample or textbook values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Orbital Mechanics: orbits, periods and Δv.
  • Thermodynamics: heat transfer and radiation basics.
  • Circuits: power, voltage and current.
  • Flight Dynamics & Controls and Aerospace Structures: attitude control, stiffness and loads.

How to study

  • Start every design from requirements, and keep a budget (mass, power, Δv, link) for every subsystem.
  • Carry margins and say where they live.
  • Track units: watts versus watt-hours, kelvin for radiation, decibels for links.
  • Check every answer against a real mission’s order of magnitude.
  • Sketch the spacecraft and its orbit before computing anything.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer, and retake the quizzes a few days later.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer or tablet for the free PhET Blackbody Spectrum and Circuit Construction Kit simulations.
  • Spreadsheet software (any free one works), or Python.
  • NASA mission pages and the Small Spacecraft Technology report in a web browser.
  • A calculator.

Unit 1 · Week 1

Mission Requirements and Systems Engineering

Mission objectives, requirements, the project life cycle, trade studies, subsystem budgets, and design margins.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Turn mission goals into measurable requirements.
  • Build a mass budget with margin.
  • Run a simple weighted trade study.
  • Explain why margins shrink through a project.

Key concepts

  • A mission starts with objectives; requirements make them measurable and verifiable (a number, a unit and a test).
  • Projects move through phases from concept to operations, with reviews that check requirements are met.
  • Every resource gets a budget: mass, power, Δv, data, pointing and cost, each with a margin.
  • Early designs carry large margins (often 20–30%, textbook values) because estimates are uncertain; margins shrink as hardware matures.
  • Trade studies score options against weighted criteria; the weights matter as much as the scores.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 A dry-mass estimate is 800 kg. Allocate with a 20% margin (sample values).

Show the steps
  1. 800 × 1.20.

Answer: 960 kg.

Example 1.2 NASA says Landsat 9 had to collect at least 400 scenes a day and collects 740. Find the margin.

Show the steps
  1. (740 − 400)/400.

Answer: 85% above the requirement (740/400 = 1.85).

Example 1.3 Option A scores 8 (cost) and 6 (performance); B scores 5 and 9. Weights 0.4 and 0.6. Which wins?

Show the steps
  1. A = 3.2 + 3.6 = 6.8.
  2. B = 2.0 + 5.4 = 7.4.

Answer: B wins, 7.4 to 6.8.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Allocate 1200 kg at 25% margin.

    Show answer

    1500 kg.

  2. If weights flip to 0.6/0.4, who wins?

    Show answer

    A: 7.2 vs B: 6.6.

  3. What makes a requirement verifiable?

    Show answer

    A measurable value and a test or analysis to check it.

  4. Why carry margin?

    Show answer

    Estimates grow as designs mature.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Requirements are wishes.

    Actually: They are measurable and verifiable.

  • Myth: Margins are waste.

    Actually: They absorb growth and surprises.

  • Myth: Trade studies are objective.

    Actually: The weights carry judgment.

Flashcards

  • Requirement

    Measurable, verifiable need.

  • Budget

    Allocation of a resource.

  • Margin

    Reserve above estimate.

  • Trade study

    Weighted comparison.

  • Life cycle

    Phases from concept to disposal.

  • Verification

    Proof a requirement is met.

How this helps save the planet

NASA says Landsat 9’s program requirements included collecting at least 400 scenes a day and a 5-year design life; Landsat 9 collects 740 scenes a day, almost double the minimum, and carries enough fuel to operate for at least 10 years.

Exceeding requirements like these extends a land record that, NASA notes, spans more than five decades and supports deforestation alerts, water-quality monitoring and crop reports.

Unit 2 · Week 2

Orbits and the Space Environment

Choosing an orbit, period and speed, sun-synchronous and geostationary orbits, ground tracks and swaths, eclipse, vacuum, radiation and atomic oxygen.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute orbital period and speed.
  • Count orbits per day.
  • Estimate eclipse duration in low orbit.
  • List environment hazards by orbit.

Key concepts

  • Period T = 2π√(a³/μ); circular speed v = √(μ/a).
  • Sun-synchronous orbits are near-polar and precess once a year, so the satellite crosses the equator at the same local time each day.
  • Low orbits pass through Earth’s shadow; the eclipse fraction for a circular orbit with the Sun in the orbit plane is about (2/2π)·asin(R_E/a).
  • The space environment includes vacuum, extreme temperatures, radiation belts, atomic oxygen in low orbit and debris.
  • Higher orbits see more of Earth but need bigger optics and antennas for the same resolution and data rate.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 Landsat 9 flies at 705 km. Find its period.

Show the steps
  1. a = 6378.137 + 705 = 7083.1 km.
  2. T = 2π√(7083.1³/398,600.4).

Answer: T = 98.9 min (NASA lists 99 min).

Example 2.2 Find orbits per day and speed.

Show the steps
  1. 86,400/5932.7 s.
  2. v = √(398,600.4/7083.1).

Answer: 14.56 orbits/day; 7.50 km/s.

Example 2.3 Estimate the worst-case eclipse.

Show the steps
  1. asin(6378.1/7083.1) = 1.121 rad.
  2. f = 1.121/π = 0.357.

Answer: About 35.3 min per orbit.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Why near-polar?

    Show answer

    To cover the whole globe.

  2. Inclination of Landsat 9?

    Show answer

    98.2°.

  3. Main low-orbit erosion hazard?

    Show answer

    Atomic oxygen.

  4. Does eclipse shrink at higher altitude?

    Show answer

    Yes, the fraction drops.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Space is cold, so spacecraft freeze.

    Actually: Sunlit parts can overheat; balance matters.

  • Myth: Higher is always better.

    Actually: Resolution and links suffer.

  • Myth: Sun-synchronous means always sunlit.

    Actually: Only dawn–dusk versions avoid eclipse.

  • Eclipse

    Time in Earth’s shadow.

  • Swath

    Ground width imaged.

  • Atomic oxygen

    Erodes LEO surfaces.

  • Radiation belts

    Trapped charged particles.

How this helps save the planet

NASA lists Landsat 9 in a near-polar, sun-synchronous orbit at 705 km with a 98.2° inclination, a 99-minute period, about 14.5 orbits a day, a 185 km swath and an equatorial crossing near 10:12 a.m. local time.

With Landsat 8, it observes the same place every 8 days. Same-time-of-day lighting is what lets scientists compare images across decades.

Unit 3 · Week 3

Power Generation: Solar Arrays

Solar flux and distance, cell efficiency, sun angle, beginning- and end-of-life power, array sizing and specific power.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute array output per square meter.
  • Apply the cosine and inverse-square laws.
  • Size an array for a load.
  • Compare cell technologies.

Key concepts

  • Solar flux near Earth is about 1361 W/m² (textbook value) and falls with the inverse square of distance from the Sun.
  • Power drops with the cosine of the angle between the array normal and the Sun.
  • NASA’s small-spacecraft survey reports multi-junction space cells at a nominal 30% efficiency (up to 34%), versus about 20% for single-junction silicon.
  • Degradation from radiation, contamination and aging lowers end-of-life power, so arrays are sized for EOL.
  • Specific power (W/kg) often governs feasibility; NASA notes solar-array missions cluster around 30 W/kg.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 Find output per m² for 30% cells facing the Sun at 1 AU.

Show the steps
  1. 1361 × 0.30.

Answer: 408.3 W/m².

Example 3.2 Find the area for 2 kW with a 0.85 degradation-and-packing factor (sample value).

Show the steps
  1. 2000/(1361 × 0.30 × 0.85).

Answer: 5.76 m².

Example 3.3 Find solar flux at Jupiter (5.2 AU).

Show the steps
  1. 1361/5.2².

Answer: 50.3 W/m², 27 times less.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Output per m² tilted 30°?

    Show answer

    353.6 W/m².

  2. Flux at Mars (1.524 AU)?

    Show answer

    586 W/m².

  3. Output per m² for 20% silicon?

    Show answer

    272.2 W/m².

  4. Array mass for 2 kW at 30 W/kg?

    Show answer

    66.7 kg.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Distance hardly matters.

    Actually: Flux falls as 1/r².

  • Myth: BOL power is enough.

    Actually: Size for end of life.

  • Myth: Efficiency is all that matters.

    Actually: Mass and stowed volume matter too.

Flashcards

    ≈1361 W/m² at 1 AU.

  • Multi-junction cell

    Stacked bandgaps, ~30%.

  • BOL/EOL

    Beginning/end of life.

  • Cosine loss

    P ∝ cos θ.

  • Inverse square

    S ∝ 1/r².

  • Specific power

    W/kg.

How this helps save the planet

NASA describes Europa Clipper as a solar-powered spacecraft that, with its solar arrays deployed, spans more than 100 feet (about 30 meters); it launched Oct. 14, 2024, and will travel 1.8 billion miles to reach Jupiter in April 2030.

At Jupiter, sunlight is about 27 times weaker than at Earth, which is why Clipper’s arrays are so large: this unit’s inverse-square law, at mission scale.

Unit 4 · Week 4

Energy Storage and the Power Budget

Load lists, sunlight and eclipse energy balance, battery sizing with depth of discharge, specific energy, power management and distribution.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Build a load list and orbit-average power.
  • Size a battery for eclipse.
  • Compute battery mass from specific energy.
  • Explain depth-of-discharge limits.

Key concepts

  • The power budget sums every load by mode; the array must power loads in sunlight and recharge the battery for eclipse.
  • Battery energy = load × eclipse time ÷ allowed depth of discharge (DoD).
  • Shallow DoD extends cycle life; low orbits see about 15 cycles a day, so DoD is often kept low (textbook guidance).
  • NASA’s survey reports commercial Li-ion cells at about 150–270 Wh/kg and 3.6 V average.
  • Power management and distribution regulates bus voltage, switches loads and isolates faults.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 A 500 W load must ride through the 35.3-minute eclipse from Unit 2. Find the energy used.

Show the steps
  1. 500 × 35.28/60.

Answer: 294 Wh.

Example 4.2 Size the battery for 30% DoD (sample value).

Show the steps
  1. 294/0.30.

Answer: 980 Wh.

Example 4.3 Find battery mass at 150 Wh/kg pack level.

Show the steps
  1. 980/150.

Answer: 6.53 kg.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Capacity at 28 V?

    Show answer

    35 Ah.

  2. Mass using 260 Wh/kg cells?

    Show answer

    3.77 kg (cells only).

  3. Cycles per day at 14.56 orbits/day?

    Show answer

    About 14.6.

  4. Why not 100% DoD?

    Show answer

    Cycle life collapses.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Watts and watt-hours are the same.

    Actually: Power vs energy.

  • Myth: Deep discharge is fine.

    Actually: It shortens life.

  • Myth: Cell energy = pack energy.

    Actually: Packs add mass for structure and management.

    Power per subsystem and mode.

  • DoD

    Fraction of capacity used.

  • Specific energy

    Wh/kg.

  • PMAD

    Power management and distribution.

    No solar power.

  • Orbit-average power

    Time-weighted load.

How this helps save the planet

NASA’s small-spacecraft technology survey lists 18650 lithium-ion cells with flight heritage on NASA missions, including LG Chem INR18650-MJ1 cells at 260 Wh/kg for NASA’s PACE mission and LG Chem ICR18650 B3 cells at 191–205 Wh/kg for Europa Clipper.

Unit 5 · Week 5

Thermal Control

Radiation balance, absorptivity and emissivity, equilibrium temperature, radiator sizing, coatings, heaters and multilayer insulation.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute an equilibrium temperature.
  • Size a radiator.
  • Choose coatings by α/ε.
  • Explain heaters and insulation.

Key concepts

  • In vacuum, heat leaves mainly by radiation: Q = εσAT⁴.
  • Absorbed sunlight is αSA_proj; the ratio α/ε of a surface sets its equilibrium temperature.
  • A black sphere at 1 AU with α = ε settles near (S/4σ)^¼ ≈ 278 K.
  • Radiators reject waste heat; white paints and mirrors have low α/ε, staying cool in sunlight.
  • Multilayer insulation blocks radiation; heaters keep batteries and propellant warm in eclipse.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 Find the equilibrium temperature of a sphere at 1 AU with α = ε.

Show the steps
  1. T = (1361/(4 × 5.6704 × 10⁻⁸))^¼.

Answer: T = 278.3 K (5 °C).

Example 5.2 Size a radiator to reject 500 W at 290 K with ε = 0.85 (ignoring absorbed sunlight).

Show the steps
  1. A = 500/(0.85 × 5.6704 × 10⁻⁸ × 290⁴).

Answer: A = 1.47 m².

Example 5.3 How does equilibrium temperature scale with α/ε?

Show the steps
  1. T ∝ (α/ε)^¼.

Answer: Doubling α/ε raises T by 2^¼ = 1.189.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. T for α/ε = 2?

    Show answer

    331 K.

  2. Radiator at 260 K for 500 W?

    Show answer

    2.27 m².

  3. What does MLI do?

    Show answer

    Blocks radiative heat flow.

  4. Why heaters in eclipse?

    Show answer

    To keep parts above minimum temperatures.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Space conducts heat away.

    Actually: Vacuum: radiation dominates.

  • Myth: Black is always hot.

    Actually: Depends on α/ε.

  • Myth: Thermal is an afterthought.

    Actually: It drives layout and power.

Flashcards

    Multilayer insulation.

    Heat-rejecting surface.

    Keeps parts warm.

How this helps save the planet

Unit 6 · Week 6

Structures, Mechanisms and Launch Loads

Launch loads, quasi-static design loads, stiffness and natural frequency requirements, materials, deployables, and heritage buses.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute a quasi-static launch load.
  • Compute a natural frequency.
  • Explain stiffness requirements.
  • Weigh heritage versus new design.

Key concepts

  • Launch subjects spacecraft to steady acceleration, vibration, acoustics and shock; design starts from quasi-static load factors (often several g, textbook values).
  • Launch vehicles require the spacecraft’s fundamental frequency to stay above a minimum so it does not couple with the rocket.
  • For a simple spring-mass model, f = (1/2π)√(k/m): stiffness raises frequency, mass lowers it.
  • Aluminum honeycomb and composites give stiffness for little mass; mechanisms deploy arrays, antennas and booms.
  • Reusing a flight-proven bus (heritage) cuts risk and schedule.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 6.1 Find the design load for a 1000 kg spacecraft at 6 g (sample values).

Show the steps
  1. 6 × 1000 × 9.80665.

Answer: 58.8 kN.

Example 6.2 A 100 kg instrument on a mount of stiffness 10⁶ N/m (sample values). Find f.

Show the steps
  1. (1/2π)√(10⁶/100).

Answer: f = 15.9 Hz.

Example 6.3 How stiff must the mount be to double f?

Show the steps
  1. f ∝ √k.

Answer: 4 × 10⁶ N/m (31.8 Hz).

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

    Show answer

  2. Load at 4 g for 500 kg?

    Show answer

    19.6 kN.

  3. Why minimum frequency?

    Show answer

    Avoid coupling with launcher dynamics.

  4. What is a heritage bus?

    Show answer

    A flight-proven spacecraft platform.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Strength is the only goal.

    Actually: Stiffness often drives design.

  • Myth: Heavier is safer.

    Actually: Mass lowers frequency and costs launch.

  • Myth: Deployables are simple.

    Actually: They are common single points of failure.

Unit 7 · Week 7

Propulsion and the Delta-v Budget

Δv budgets for orbit insertion, station-keeping, attitude and disposal; the rocket equation for propellant mass; gravity assists; and propulsion choices.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Build a Δv budget.
  • Compute propellant mass from Δv.
  • Compute Δv from propellant.
  • Explain gravity assists.

Key concepts

  • A Δv budget lists every maneuver: insertion, orbit maintenance, attitude control, collision avoidance and disposal, plus margin.
  • Propellant needed: m_p = m₀(1 − e^(−Δv/v_e)).
  • Monopropellant thrusters are simple; bipropellant gives higher Isp; electric gives very high Isp at low thrust.
  • Gravity assists borrow momentum from a planet to change speed and direction without propellant.
  • Leftover propellant can extend a mission past its design life.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

  2. m_p = 1000(1 − e^(−100/2942)).

Answer: 33.4 kg.

Show the steps
  1. 2942 ln(1000/950).

Answer: 150.9 m/s.

Example 7.3 Budget: 60 m/s insertion trim, 30 m/s station-keeping, 10 m/s disposal, 20% margin.

Show the steps
  1. 100 × 1.2.

Unit 8 · Weeks 8–9

Attitude Determination and Control Subsystem

Pointing requirements from payloads, sensors and actuators, disturbance torques, momentum budgets, and pointing error on the ground.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Derive a pointing requirement from ground resolution.
  • Compute ground error from angle error.
  • Build a momentum budget.
  • Pick sensors and actuators.

Key concepts

  • Payloads set pointing: an imager’s pixel subtends an angle of (ground pixel)/(altitude).
  • Ground error ≈ altitude × angle error (small angles).
  • Sensors: star trackers, sun sensors, magnetometers, gyros; actuators: reaction wheels, magnetic torquers, thrusters.
  • Disturbances (gravity gradient, drag, solar pressure, magnetic) build momentum H = τt that wheels must store.
  • Momentum is dumped with magnetic torquers or thrusters before wheels saturate.

Unit 9 · Weeks 10–11

Communications and Data Handling

Data volume, downlink rates, the link budget in decibels, free-space path loss, antenna gain, and onboard computing and storage.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute free-space path loss.
  • Compute dish antenna gain.
  • Build a simple link budget.
  • Estimate daily data volume.

Key concepts

  • Free-space path loss L = 20 log₁₀(4πd/λ) dB grows with distance and frequency.
  • Dish gain G = η(πD/λ)²; bigger dishes and higher frequencies give narrower, stronger beams.
  • Link budget: received power = transmit power + gains − losses (all in dB); the margin over what the receiver needs must be positive.
  • Data volume = images × bits per image; rate × contact time must exceed it.
  • Command and data handling computers store data, run software and manage faults.

Unit 10 · Weeks 12–13

Reliability, Testing and End of Life

Failure rates and reliability, redundancy, environmental testing, calibration and verification, operations and responsible disposal.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute reliability from a failure rate.
  • Compute the benefit of redundancy.
  • Describe environmental tests.
  • Plan end-of-life disposal.

Key concepts

  • With a constant failure rate λ, reliability R(t) = e^(−λt) and mean time between failures is 1/λ.
  • Two units in parallel: R = 1 − (1 − R₁)²; redundancy trades mass and cost for reliability.
  • “Test like you fly”: vibration, acoustic, shock, thermal-vacuum and electromagnetic tests before launch.
  • Calibration keeps science data accurate over time; cross-calibration with other satellites ties records together.
  • At end of life, spacecraft should be deorbited or moved to a disposal orbit to limit debris.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

  2. e^(−0.438).

Answer: R = 0.645.

Example 10.2 Add a redundant unit in parallel.

Show the steps
  1. 1 − 0.355².

Answer: R = 0.874.

Example 10.3 Find the MTBF.

Show the steps
  1. 1/10⁻⁵ h.

Answer: 100,000 h = 11.4 years.
