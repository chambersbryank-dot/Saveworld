About this course

This is a free self-study course outline written by Saving Planets. It is not a university course, it carries no academic credit, and no school is involved.

Its scope follows a one-semester junior fluid mechanics course. It is modeled on the topics and depth of MIT 2.06 Fluid Dynamics and MIT 1.060 Engineering Mechanics II (both free on MIT OpenCourseWare), and on courses such as Purdue ME 309 and Georgia Tech CEE 3040, using the free OpenStax University Physics fluids chapter and PhET simulations, which we link where they help. None of those authors or schools endorses this page.

What we add: every unit ties to real work with a cited public source (hydropower, pumped storage, marine energy, household water leaks, ocean circulation and wind turbines), every number on the page is re-computed by a checking script before it goes live, and every lab has a free simulation or a safe home version.

Work through it in order. Each unit has goals, key ideas, equations, step-by-step examples, practice with full answers, common mistakes, flashcards and a quiz. Your progress is saved only in this browser.

How this course helps save the planet

Moving water and air carries most of the world’s renewable energy: rivers through hydropower turbines, wind across turbine blades, tides and waves into marine energy devices. Fluid mechanics also decides how much energy it takes to pump water to cities and farms, and how much is lost to leaks and friction.

Ocean currents move heat around the planet and shape the climate. Each unit ends with a short, sourced section. Values that are not from a source are labeled as sample values.

Before you start

Prerequisites

  • Calculus III: partial derivatives, divergence and multiple integrals.
  • Differential Equations: first- and second-order ODEs.
  • Statics and Dynamics: free-body diagrams, Newton’s laws and momentum.
  • Thermodynamics helps with the energy equation but is not required.

How to study

  • Start every problem with a sketch: control volume, streamline, or free-body diagram of the fluid.
  • Write the governing equation in words first (mass, momentum or energy), then cancel terms you can justify.
  • Work in heads (metres of fluid) for liquid systems; they make pumps, pipes and turbines easy to compare.
  • Compute the Reynolds number before choosing a friction or drag correlation.
  • Check units and limits: zero flow means zero loss; doubling speed roughly quadruples turbulent losses.
  • Do every practice problem on paper before opening the answer, and retake the quizzes a few days later.

Lab equipment (for the wet labs)

  • A computer or tablet for the free PhET Density, Buoyancy, Under Pressure and Fluid Pressure and Flow simulations.
  • A clear plastic bottle, a nail or skewer, a ruler and tape.
  • A kitchen scale, a measuring jug, a bucket and a stopwatch.
  • A garden hose or a sink tap.
  • Spreadsheet software for the pipe and pump labs.

Unit 1 · Week 1

Fluid Properties: Density, Viscosity and Surface Tension

What makes a fluid, the continuum assumption, density and specific gravity, pressure, viscosity and Newton’s law of viscosity, kinematic viscosity, non-Newtonian fluids, surface tension and capillary rise, and vapor pressure and cavitation.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Define a fluid and use the continuum assumption.
  • Use Newton’s law of viscosity to find shear stress.
  • Compute capillary rise from surface tension.
  • Explain how density differences drive flows.

Key concepts

  • A fluid deforms continuously under any shear stress, however small. Treating it as a continuum lets us define density, velocity and pressure at every point.
  • Density ρ (kg/m³) and specific gravity SG = ρ/ρ_water. Water at 20 °C has ρ = 998 kg/m³; air at 15 °C has 1.225 kg/m³.
  • Newton’s law of viscosity: shear stress τ = μ du/dy. Dynamic viscosity μ measures resistance to shearing; kinematic viscosity ν = μ/ρ. Water is about 56 times more viscous than air but has about 15 times lower ν.
  • Non-Newtonian fluids (paint, blood, toothpaste) have a viscosity that changes with shear rate.
  • Surface tension σ pulls liquid surfaces tight; in a thin tube water rises h = 4σ cos θ/(ρgD). If local pressure drops to the vapor pressure, the liquid boils: cavitation, which damages pumps and propellers.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 1.1 Water (μ = 1.002 × 10⁻³ kg/(m·s)) fills a 1 mm gap between a fixed plate and one moving at 2 m/s. Find the shear stress.

Show the steps
  1. du/dy = 2/0.001 = 2000 s⁻¹.
  2. τ = 1.002 × 10⁻³ × 2000.

Answer: τ = 2.00 Pa.

Example 1.2 Find the capillary rise of water (σ = 0.0728 N/m, θ = 0) in a 1 mm glass tube.

Show the steps
  1. h = 4 × 0.0728/(998 × 9.81 × 0.001).

Answer: h = 29.7 mm.

Example 1.3 Find ν for water at 20 °C.

Show the steps
  1. ν = 1.002 × 10⁻³/998.

Answer: ν = 1.004 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Find the SG of seawater (1025 kg/m³).

    Show answer

    1025/998 = 1.027.

  2. Find the capillary rise in a 0.5 mm tube.

    Show answer

    Twice as much: 59.5 mm.

  3. Find μ_water/μ_air.

    Show answer

    1.002 × 10⁻³/1.789 × 10⁻⁵ = 56.0.

  4. What fraction of an iceberg floats below seawater?

    Show answer

    917/1025 = 89.5%.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Viscosity and density are the same thing.

    Actually: Honey is viscous; mercury is dense but not very viscous.

  • Myth: Fluids resist shear like solids.

    Actually: They flow under any shear stress.

  • Myth: Cavitation needs high temperature.

    Actually: Low pressure is enough.

  • Viscosity

  • Kinematic viscosity

    ν = μ/ρ.

  • Specific gravity

    ρ/ρ_water.

  • Capillary rise

    4σ cos θ/(ρgD).

  • Cavitation

    Boiling at low pressure.

How this helps save the planet

NOAA explains that winds drive ocean currents in the upper 100 meters, but deep-ocean currents thousands of meters down are driven by differences in water density, controlled by temperature and salinity: thermohaline circulation.

In polar regions, sea ice forms and leaves salt behind, so the surrounding water becomes saltier and denser and sinks; surface water flows in to replace it, starting the global conveyor belt. Density, the first property in this unit, moves heat around the whole planet.

Unit 2 · Weeks 2–3

Fluid Statics, Manometers and Buoyancy

Pressure at a point, the hydrostatic equation, gauge and absolute pressure, manometers, hydrostatic forces on plane submerged surfaces and the center of pressure, forces on curved surfaces, Archimedes’ principle, and stability of floating bodies.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute pressure at depth and read manometers.
  • Find the resultant force and center of pressure on a submerged gate.
  • Apply Archimedes’ principle.
  • Judge whether a floating body is stable.

Key concepts

  • Pressure at a point acts equally in all directions. In a static fluid dp/dz = −ρg, so with constant density p = p₀ + ρgh at depth h. Gauge pressure is measured relative to the atmosphere.
  • Manometers balance columns of fluid: move through the tube adding ρgh going down and subtracting going up.
  • On a plane surface the resultant force is F = ρg h_c A (h_c = depth of the centroid). It acts at the center of pressure, below the centroid: y_cp = y_c + I_xc/(y_c A).
  • On curved surfaces, find the horizontal component as the force on the vertical projection and the vertical component as the weight of fluid above.
  • Archimedes: the buoyant force equals the weight of displaced fluid, F_B = ρ_fluid g V_displaced. A floating body is stable if its metacenter lies above its center of gravity.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 2.1 Find the gauge pressure 10 m below the surface of fresh water (ρ = 998 kg/m³).

Show the steps
  1. p = 998 × 9.81 × 10.

Answer: p = 97.9 kPa (about one atmosphere).

Example 2.2 A vertical rectangular gate 2 m wide and 3 m tall has its top edge at the water surface. Find the force and the depth of the center of pressure.

Show the steps
  1. h_c = 1.5 m; A = 6 m².
  2. F = 998 × 9.81 × 1.5 × 6 = 88.1 kN.
  3. I_xc = 2 × 3³/12 = 4.5 m⁴; y_cp = 1.5 + 4.5/(1.5 × 6).

Answer: F = 88.1 kN acting 2.0 m below the surface (two-thirds of the depth).

Example 2.3 A mercury manometer (ρ = 13,600 kg/m³) connected across a water pipe shows a 0.1 m difference. Find the pressure difference.

Show the steps
  1. Δp = (13,600 − 1000) × 9.81 × 0.1 (water above the mercury, ρ_water ≈ 1000 kg/m³).

Answer: Δp = 12.4 kPa.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. Find the buoyant force on a fully submerged 2 L object in water (ρ ≈ 1000 kg/m³).

    Show answer

    1000 × 9.81 × 0.002 = 19.6 N.

  2. What is the absolute pressure 10 m down if p_atm = 101.3 kPa?

    Show answer

    199.2 kPa.

  3. Does the force on a dam depend on how long the reservoir is?

    Show answer

    No; only depth and wall area.

  4. Why is the center of pressure below the centroid?

    Show answer

    Pressure grows with depth.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Pressure depends on container shape.

    Actually: Only on depth (the hydrostatic paradox).

  • Myth: Buoyancy depends on the object’s weight.

    Actually: It depends on displaced volume.

  • Myth: The force on a gate acts at its center.

    Actually: It acts at the center of pressure, lower down.

Flashcards

  • Plane force

  • Center of pressure

    y_c + I/(y_c A).

  • Archimedes

    F_B = ρgV.

  • Stability

    Metacenter above G.

How this helps save the planet

DOE notes that the United States has more than 90,000 dams, and that less than 3% of them produce power; the rest serve recreation, farm ponds, flood control, water supply and irrigation. Every one of them is designed with this unit’s equations: the water load on a dam grows with the square of the depth and acts low on the wall.

DOE describes the most common hydropower plant, an impoundment facility, as a dam that stores river water in a reservoir; water released through a turbine spins a generator, and releases can also serve flood control, recreation, fish passage and water quality. The stored water’s pressure on the dam and its head at the turbine both come straight from p = ρgh.

Unit 3 · Week 4

Bernoulli, Heads and the Energy Equation

Streamlines and flow visualization, continuity Q = AV, Bernoulli’s equation and its limits, pressure, velocity and elevation heads, the energy grade line, Torricelli’s law, the steady-flow energy equation with pump, turbine and loss heads, and hydropower output.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Apply continuity and Bernoulli along a streamline.
  • Work in heads: pressure, velocity and elevation.
  • Use the energy equation with pump, turbine and loss heads.
  • Compute hydropower and pumping power.

Key concepts

  • For incompressible steady flow, volume flow Q = AV is the same at every section of a pipe (continuity).
  • Bernoulli along a streamline (steady, inviscid, incompressible): p/(ρg) + V²/(2g) + z = constant. Each term is a head in metres.
  • Torricelli: a jet leaving a tank at depth h has speed V = √(2gh).
  • Real systems add pumps and turbines and lose energy to friction: p₁/(ρg) + V₁²/(2g) + z₁ + h_pump = p₂/(ρg) + V₂²/(2g) + z₂ + h_turbine + h_L.
  • Power from a head: P = ρgQH. Turbines deliver P = ηρgQH; pumps need P = ρgQH/η. DOE notes that hydropower output depends on flow and head.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 3.1 Find the jet speed from a hole 5 m below a tank’s surface.

Show the steps
  1. V = √(2 × 9.81 × 5).

Answer: V = 9.90 m/s.

Example 3.2 A hydropower plant has Q = 50 m³/s, head H = 100 m and overall efficiency 0.9 (sample values; ρ = 1000 kg/m³). Find the power.

Show the steps
  1. P = 0.9 × 1000 × 9.81 × 50 × 100.

Answer: P = 44.1 MW, a “large” plant by DOE’s 30 MW definition.

Example 3.3 A pump lifts 0.05 m³/s of water 20 m with 5 m of pipe losses at 75% efficiency. Find the power input.

Show the steps
  1. h_pump = 20 + 5 = 25 m.
  2. P = 1000 × 9.81 × 0.05 × 25/0.75.

Answer: P = 16.4 kW.

Practice problems

Work each on paper first, then check.

  1. A hose carries 0.4 L/s through a 20 mm bore into a 10 mm nozzle. Find both speeds.

    Show answer

    1.27 m/s and 5.09 m/s.

  2. Convert 200 kPa to metres of water head.

    Show answer

    200,000/(1000 × 9.81) = 20.4 m.

  3. Double the head of a hydropower plant at the same Q. Power?

    Show answer

    Doubles.

  4. Why does a pitot tube read higher than a static tap?

    Show answer

    It adds the velocity head.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Bernoulli applies across a pump.

    Actually: Use the energy equation with h_pump.

  • Myth: Faster flow means higher pressure.

    Actually: Along a streamline, faster means lower static pressure.

  • Myth: Head is a pressure.

    Actually: It is energy per unit weight, in metres.

Flashcards

    Q = AV.

  • Bernoulli

  • Torricelli

    V = √(2gh).

  • Energy equation

    Adds h_pump, h_turbine, h_L.

  • Hydropower

    P = ηρgQH.

  • Pump power

    ρgQH/η.

How this helps save the planet

DOE explains that hydropower is powered by the kinetic energy of flowing water, and that the energy available depends on both the volume of flow and the change in elevation, the head: the greater the flow and the higher the head, the more electricity can be generated. Water flows through a penstock and spins a turbine connected to a generator.

DOE classes plants by size: large hydropower above 30 MW, small hydropower from 100 kW to 10 MW, and micro hydropower up to 100 kW, enough for a single home, farm or village. P = ηρgQH lets you size any of them from a flow measurement and a survey of the drop.

Unit 4 · Week 5

Integral Momentum: Jets, Vanes, Bends and Turbines

Reynolds transport theorem, the linear momentum equation for control volumes, forces of jets on plates and vanes, forces on pipe bends and nozzles, moving control volumes, impulse turbines (Pelton wheels), and the angular momentum equation for turbomachines.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • State the Reynolds transport theorem.
  • Apply the momentum equation to jets and vanes.
  • Analyze a moving vane and a Pelton wheel.
  • Use angular momentum for turbine and pump torque.

Key concepts

  • Reynolds transport theorem: the rate of change of a property of a fluid system = rate of change inside a control volume + net outflow across its surface.
  • Steady linear momentum: ΣF = Σṁ_out V_out − Σṁ_in V_in (vectors). Include pressure forces on cut sections and the support force.
  • A jet of speed V and flow Q hitting a flat plate head-on exerts F = ρQV. A vane turning the jet through angle θ exerts F = ρQV(1 − cos θ) along the jet.
  • For a vane moving at speed U, use the relative speed (V − U). A Pelton wheel’s buckets turn the jet nearly 180°; power F·U is largest at U = V/2.
  • Angular momentum: torque = ṁ(r₂V_θ2 − r₁V_θ1) (Euler turbomachine equation), the basis of pump and turbine design.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 4.1 A 50 mm water jet at 20 m/s hits a flat plate head-on. Find the force.

Show the steps
  1. A = π × 0.05²/4 = 1.963 × 10⁻³ m².
  2. F = ρAV² = 1000 × 1.963 × 10⁻³ × 400.

Answer: F = 785 N.

Example 4.2 The same jet is turned 120° by a fixed vane. Find the force along the jet.

Show the steps
  1. F = ρAV²(1 − cos 120°) = 785 × 1.5.

Answer: F = 1178 N.

Example 4.3 A Pelton bucket moving at 15 m/s receives a 0.01 m³/s jet at 30 m/s and turns it 165°. Find the force and power.

Show the steps
  1. F = 1000 × 0.01 × (30 − 15)(1 − cos 165°) = 294.9 N.
  2. P = F × U.

Answer: F = 294.9 N; P = 4.42 kW (the jet carries ½ρQV² = 4.5 kW).

Practice problems

  1. At what bucket speed does a Pelton wheel give the most power?

  2. Double jet speed at the same area. Force on a plate?

  3. Name the forces in a pipe-bend momentum balance.

  4. Why are Pelton buckets split in the middle?

    Show answer

    Actually: For a jet fully deflected, only on ρQV.

  • Myth: A moving vane sees the full jet speed.

    Actually: It sees V − U.

  • Myth: Momentum is a scalar.

    Actually: It is a vector; resolve components.

How this helps save the planet

DOE explains that pumped storage hydropower works like a giant battery: when demand is low it pumps water to an upper reservoir, and when demand is high the water flows back down through a turbine. DOE reports that pumped storage accounts for 88% of all utility-scale energy storage in the United States, at 43 plants, with room to more than double capacity.

Each pump-turbine is designed with the momentum and angular-momentum equations in this unit: the runner’s blade angles set how much torque the water gives up or receives. Storing solar and wind power this way depends on getting those blades right.

Unit 5 · Weeks 6–7

Differential Analysis: Continuity, Navier–Stokes and Exact Flows

Fluid kinematics (velocity field, acceleration, vorticity, strain), the differential continuity equation, the stream function, Euler and Navier–Stokes equations, boundary conditions, and exact laminar solutions: Couette flow, plane Poiseuille flow and Hagen–Poiseuille pipe flow.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute the material acceleration of a velocity field.
  • Check continuity and find a stream function.
  • State the Navier–Stokes equations and their boundary conditions.
  • Solve Couette and Hagen–Poiseuille flows.

Key concepts

  • In the Eulerian view, acceleration has a local part ∂V/∂t and a convective part (V·∇)V; the sum is the material derivative DV/Dt.
  • Incompressible continuity: ∇·V = ∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y + ∂w/∂z = 0. In 2D, a stream function ψ with u = ∂ψ/∂y, v = −∂ψ/∂x satisfies it automatically.
  • Navier–Stokes (incompressible, Newtonian): ρ DV/Dt = −∇p + ρg + μ∇²V. With no-slip at walls, it governs nearly every flow in this course; only a few cases solve exactly.
  • Couette flow between a fixed and a moving plate: linear velocity profile, shear τ = μU/h.
  • Hagen–Poiseuille flow in a pipe: parabolic profile, u_max = 2V_avg, and Q = πR⁴Δp/(8μL). It holds while the flow stays laminar (Re below about 2300).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 5.1 Water flows in a 4 mm diameter tube with Δp/L = 100 Pa/m. Find Q, V_avg, u_max and Re.

Show the steps
  1. Q = π × 0.002⁴ × 100/(8 × 1.002 × 10⁻³) = 6.27 × 10⁻⁷ m³/s.
  2. V = Q/(π × 0.002²) = 0.0499 m/s; u_max = 2V.
  3. Re = 998 × 0.0499 × 0.004/1.002 × 10⁻³.

Answer: Q = 6.27 × 10⁻⁷ m³/s, V = 0.0499 m/s, u_max = 0.0998 m/s, Re = 199 (laminar).

Example 5.2 A plate moves at 1 m/s over a 2 mm water film. Find the shear stress.

Show the steps
  1. τ = μU/h = 1.002 × 10⁻³ × 1/0.002.

Answer: τ = 0.501 Pa.

Example 5.3 Is V = (2x, −2y) incompressible, and what is its acceleration at (1, 1)?

Show the steps
  1. ∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y = 2 − 2 = 0.
  2. a_x = u ∂u/∂x = 2x × 2 = 4x; a_y = v ∂v/∂y = (−2y)(−2) = 4y.

Answer: Yes; a = (4, 4) m/s².

Unit 6 · Week 8

Dimensional Analysis, Similitude and Model Testing

Dimensional homogeneity, the Buckingham Pi theorem, common dimensionless groups (Reynolds, Froude, Mach, Weber, Euler), geometric, kinematic and dynamic similarity, model laws and scale effects, and nondimensionalizing the governing equations.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Find dimensionless groups with the Pi theorem.
  • Name what Re, Fr, Ma and We compare.
  • Scale model test results to full size with Froude or Reynolds similarity.
  • Explain why full similarity is often impossible.

Key concepts

  • Every valid equation is dimensionally homogeneous. The Buckingham Pi theorem says a problem with n variables and k basic dimensions has n − k independent dimensionless groups.
  • Common groups: Re = ρVL/μ (inertia/viscous), Fr = V/√(gL) (inertia/gravity), Ma = V/a (compressibility), We = ρV²L/σ (inertia/surface tension), Eu = Δp/(ρV²) (pressure).
  • Dynamic similarity: if the model and prototype have the same geometry and the same governing dimensionless groups, their dimensionless results (C_D, C_p, efficiency) match.
  • Free-surface flows (ships, spillways, waves) use Froude scaling: V_m = V_p√(L_m/L_p); forces scale with the length scale cubed and power with it to the 3.5 power.
  • Matching Re and Fr together usually needs an impossible fluid, so engineers match the dominant group and correct for the other (scale effects).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Show the steps
  1. n = 5 variables, k = 3 dimensions (M, L, T).
  2. Groups: F/(ρV²D²) and ρVD/μ.

Answer: 2 groups: C_D = f(Re).

Example 6.2 A 1:20 wave-energy model is tested with Froude scaling. The full-size wave speed is 10 m/s. Find the model speed.

Show the steps

Answer: V_m = 2.24 m/s.

Example 6.3 For that model, how do forces and power scale?

Show the steps
  1. F_p/F_m = 20³.
  2. P_p/P_m = 20^3.5.

Answer: Forces × 8000; power × 35,777.

Unit 7 · Weeks 9–10

Pipe Flow: Friction, the Moody Chart and Minor Losses

Laminar and turbulent pipe flow, entrance length, the Darcy–Weisbach equation, laminar friction factor 64/Re, the Moody chart and Colebrook and Haaland correlations, minor losses, noncircular ducts and hydraulic diameter, and series, parallel and network piping.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Decide whether pipe flow is laminar or turbulent.
  • Compute friction losses with Darcy–Weisbach and the Haaland formula.
  • Add minor losses from fittings and valves.
  • Solve simple series and parallel pipe problems.

Key concepts

  • In pipes, flow is laminar below Re ≈ 2300, turbulent above about 4000. Turbulent flow mixes momentum strongly and has a flatter profile.
  • Darcy–Weisbach: h_L = f(L/D)(V²/2g). For laminar flow f = 64/Re.
  • For turbulent flow f depends on Re and relative roughness ε/D (Moody chart). The Haaland formula is explicit: 1/√f = −1.8 log₁₀[(ε/D/3.7)^1.11 + 6.9/Re].
  • Fittings, valves, bends and entrances add minor losses h_m = K V²/(2g). In long pipelines friction dominates; in household plumbing fittings matter.
  • Noncircular ducts use the hydraulic diameter D_h = 4A/P. Pipes in series share Q and add losses; in parallel they share head loss and add flows.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 7.1 Water flows at 2 m/s in a 50 mm commercial steel pipe (ε = 0.045 mm). Find Re and f.

Show the steps
  1. Re = 998 × 2 × 0.05/1.002 × 10⁻³ = 99,600.
  2. ε/D = 0.0009; Haaland gives f.

Answer: Re = 9.96 × 10⁴ (turbulent); f = 0.0216.

Example 7.2 Find the friction head loss over 100 m of that pipe.

Show the steps
  1. h_L = 0.0216 × (100/0.05) × 2²/(2 × 9.81).

Answer: h_L = 8.82 m.

Example 7.3 Find the laminar friction factor at Re = 1500, and the loss of a sharp entrance (K = 0.5) at 2 m/s.

Show the steps
  1. f = 64/1500.
  2. h_m = 0.5 × 4/19.62.

Answer: f = 0.0427; h_m = 0.102 m.

Unit 8 · Week 11

Pumps, Turbines and Pump Systems

Centrifugal and axial pumps, pump head, power and efficiency, pump performance curves, system curves and the operating point, pumps in series and parallel, affinity laws and variable-speed drives, cavitation and NPSH, and hydraulic turbines (Pelton, Francis, Kaplan).

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Read a pump curve and find the operating point with a system curve.
  • Compute pump power and efficiency.
  • Apply the affinity laws to speed changes.
  • Explain NPSH and how to avoid cavitation.

Key concepts

  • A pump adds head h_p to the flow; water power is ρgQh_p and shaft power is ρgQh_p/η.
  • The pump curve (head falling as flow rises) meets the system curve (static lift plus losses rising with Q²) at the operating point.
  • Affinity laws for the same pump at a new speed N: Q ∝ N, H ∝ N², P ∝ N³. Slowing a pump with a variable-speed drive instead of throttling a valve saves a lot of energy.
  • Cavitation starts when inlet pressure falls near vapor pressure. The available net positive suction head (NPSH_A) must exceed what the pump requires (NPSH_R).
  • Hydraulic turbines are pumps in reverse: Pelton (impulse) for high head, Francis for medium head, Kaplan (propeller) for low head and large flow; many pumped-storage units are reversible pump-turbines.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 8.1 A pump curve is H = 40 − 7000Q² and the system curve is H = 10 + 5000Q² (H in m, Q in m³/s). Find the operating point.

Show the steps
  1. 40 − 7000Q² = 10 + 5000Q², so Q² = 30/12,000.
  2. Q = 0.05 m³/s; H = 10 + 5000 × 0.0025.

Answer: Q = 0.05 m³/s, H = 22.5 m.

Example 8.2 Find the shaft power at that point with η = 0.75 (ρ = 1000 kg/m³).

Show the steps
  1. P = 1000 × 9.81 × 0.05 × 22.5/0.75.

Answer: P = 14.7 kW.

Example 8.3 A pump delivering 0.05 m³/s at 30 m head is slowed from 1800 to 1500 rpm. Find the new flow, head and power ratio.

Show the steps
  1. Q = 0.05 × 1500/1800.
  2. H = 30 × (1500/1800)².
  3. P ratio = (1500/1800)³.

Answer: Q = 0.0417 m³/s, H = 20.8 m, power 57.9% of the original.

Unit 9 · Weeks 12–13

External Flow: Boundary Layers, Drag and Lift

Flow past bodies, boundary layers on flat plates, friction and pressure drag, drag coefficients of spheres, cylinders and streamlined bodies, the drag crisis, Stokes flow and settling, lift, and the power available in a wind or water stream.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Compute drag from a drag coefficient.
  • Explain the roles of friction and pressure drag.
  • Use Stokes’ law for small particles.
  • Find the power in a wind stream and the Betz limit.

Key concepts

  • Drag F_D = C_D ½ρV²A and lift F_L = C_L ½ρV²A, with A a reference area. C_D depends on shape and Re.
  • Streamlined bodies have mostly friction drag; bluff bodies (spheres, cylinders, trucks) have mostly pressure drag from a separated wake.
  • For a smooth sphere C_D ≈ 0.47 for 10³ < Re < 2 × 10⁵; around Re ≈ 3 × 10⁵ the boundary layer turns turbulent, separates later, and C_D drops sharply (the drag crisis).
  • At very low Re (Re < 1), Stokes drag F = 3πμVD; a small particle settles at V = (ρ_p − ρ)gD²/(18μ).
  • A stream of area A carries power ½ρAV³. No turbine can capture more than 16/27 ≈ 59.3% of it (the Betz limit).

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 9.1 A 0.22 m ball moves at 20 m/s in air (C_D = 0.47, sample value). Find Re and drag.

Show the steps
  1. Re = 1.225 × 20 × 0.22/1.789 × 10⁻⁵ = 3.01 × 10⁵.
  2. F = 0.47 × ½ × 1.225 × 400 × π × 0.11².

Answer: Re = 3.01 × 10⁵ (near the drag crisis); F = 4.38 N.

Example 9.2 Find the settling speed of a 50 µm sand grain (ρ_p = 2500 kg/m³) in water.

Show the steps
  1. V = (2500 − 998) × 9.81 × (50 × 10⁻⁶)²/(18 × 1.002 × 10⁻³).
  2. Check Re = 998 × 0.00204 × 50 × 10⁻⁶/1.002 × 10⁻³ = 0.102.

Answer: V = 2.04 mm/s; Re < 1, so Stokes’ law holds.

Example 9.3 Find the wind power through a 100 m diameter rotor at 10 m/s, and the Betz maximum.

Show the steps
  1. A = π × 50² = 7854 m².
  2. P = ½ × 1.225 × 7854 × 1000.
  3. P_max = P × 16/27.

Answer: P = 4.81 MW; Betz maximum 2.85 MW.

Unit 10 · Weeks 14–15

Open-Channel Flow: Rivers, Canals and Hydraulic Jumps

Open channels, hydraulic radius, the Froude number and subcritical, critical and supercritical flow, specific energy and critical depth, uniform flow and Manning’s equation, hydraulic jumps and energy dissipation, and weirs for flow measurement.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

  • Classify channel flow with the Froude number.
  • Find critical depth and specific energy.
  • Use Manning’s equation for uniform flow.
  • Compute the depth after and energy lost in a hydraulic jump.

Key concepts

  • In open channels gravity drives the flow and the free surface is at atmospheric pressure. The hydraulic radius is R_h = A/P (wetted perimeter).
  • Froude number Fr = V/√(gy): Fr < 1 subcritical (slow, deep), Fr = 1 critical, Fr > 1 supercritical (fast, shallow). Surface waves can travel upstream only in subcritical flow.
  • Specific energy E = y + V²/(2g). For flow per unit width q, the minimum E occurs at critical depth y_c = (q²/g)^(1/3).
  • Uniform flow (Manning, SI): V = (1/n) R_h^(2/3) S^(1/2), with roughness n (about 0.013 for finished concrete) and bed slope S.
  • A hydraulic jump turns supercritical flow subcritical: y₂/y₁ = ½(√(1 + 8Fr₁²) − 1), and dissipates head h_L = (y₂ − y₁)³/(4y₁y₂). Spillways use jumps to protect riverbeds below dams.

Key equations

Worked examples, step by step

Example 10.1 A 2 m wide concrete channel (n = 0.013) flows 1 m deep on a slope of 0.001. Find V and Q.

Show the steps
  1. A = 2 m², P = 4 m, R_h = 0.5 m.
  2. V = (1/0.013) × 0.5^(2/3) × 0.001^0.5.
  3. Q = VA.

Answer: V = 1.53 m/s; Q = 3.06 m³/s.

Example 10.2 Water flows 0.5 m deep at 6 m/s below a spillway. Find Fr, the depth after a hydraulic jump and the head lost.

Show the steps
  1. Fr = 6/√(9.81 × 0.5) = 2.71.
  2. y₂ = 0.25 × (√(1 + 8 × 2.71²) − 1).
  3. h_L = (y₂ − y₁)³/(4y₁y₂).

Answer: Fr = 2.71; y₂ = 1.68 m; h_L = 0.491 m.

Example 10.3 Find the critical depth for q = 2 m²/s.

Show the steps
  1. y_c = (4/9.81)^(1/3).

Answer: y_c = 0.742 m.

  3. What depth would carry q = 2 m²/s at V = 1.53 m/s?

    Show answer

    2/1.532 = 1.31 m.

  4. Why build a stilling basin below a dam?

    Show answer

    To force a jump that dissipates energy.

Common misconceptions

  • Myth: Fast flow is always supercritical.

    Actually: Compare V with √(gy).

  • Myth: A jump gains energy.

    Actually: It loses energy.

  • Myth: Manning’s n is the same everywhere.

    Actually: It depends on the surface.

How this helps save the planet

DOE describes a diversion, or run-of-river, hydropower facility as one that channels part of a river through a canal and/or a penstock, using the natural decline of the riverbed to produce energy, sometimes without a dam. Designing that canal is an open-channel problem: Manning’s equation sizes it for the flow, and the drop sets the head.

DOE adds that impoundment releases can serve fish passage and water-quality needs. Engineers use the Froude number and hydraulic jumps to shape spillways and channels that protect riverbeds and keep flows safe for wildlife.

Glossary

Boundary layer
Thin layer near a wall where viscosity matters.
Buoyancy
Upward force equal to the weight of displaced fluid.
Cavitation
Vapor bubbles forming where pressure falls to vapor pressure.
Continuity
Conservation of mass in a flow.
Drag coefficient
Drag divided by ½ρV²A.
Froude number
V/√(gL), inertia over gravity.
Head
Energy per unit weight of fluid, in metres.
Head loss
Mechanical energy lost to friction, per unit weight.
Hydraulic jump
Sudden change from supercritical to subcritical flow.
Hydraulic radius
Flow area over wetted perimeter.
Laminar flow
Smooth, layered flow at low Reynolds number.
Manometer
Tube of liquid used to measure pressure differences.
Navier–Stokes equations
Momentum equations for a viscous fluid.
Operating point
Where a pump curve meets the system curve.
Reynolds number
ρVL/μ, inertia over viscous forces.
Specific gravity
Density relative to water.
Stokes flow
Creeping flow at Re much less than 1.
Surface tension
Force per length along a liquid surface.
Turbulent flow
Chaotic, mixing flow at high Reynolds number.
Viscosity
Resistance of a fluid to shearing.
